The burn is not this year’s mod.

Thanksgiving is Thursday. There is no January 1 rate filing to wait on. A burn, a cut, or a slip in the kitchen is the claim that can move an experience modification, and it does not show up on the renewal you are holding.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. Written for owners, not for insurance people.

The question usually shows up when somebody comes off the line with a towel on a hand. The fryer is going to stay hot longer than a Tuesday. The prep list is longer than the people who already know the board. The owner wants to know what that burn, that cut, or that slip does to the workers’ comp bill.

This is the version for a restaurant, bar, or café in the San Gabriel Valley. Monrovia, Pasadena, Arcadia, Duarte, Azusa, Glendora, Sierra Madre, San Gabriel, Alhambra. The statewide benchmark and the restaurant class codes are the September note. What the auditor counts as payroll is the audit note. A guest who goes down at the door is the rain note. A grease fire that stays in the hood is the hood note. This one is the experience modification, and the person on the clock.

There is no January 1 rate to wait on

I looked for a new advisory pure premium rate to hang this week on. The Workers’ Compensation Insurance Rating Bureau’s filings page organizes those filings by effective date. The latest section on it is still September 1, 2026. The bulletins through November 19, 2026 do not add a January 1, 2027 pure premium rate filing. The January items on that list are committee nominations, a change to an aggregate-data program effective April 1, 2027, and revisions to the bureau’s premium-audit program effective January 1, 2027. None of those is a new advisory rate. The rate that is in force, and the restaurant classes that sit on it, are already written up. I am not reprinting them.

The burn on Thursday is not the modification on the renewal you are holding.

The threshold is expected losses, not a headcount

An experience modification compares what your claims cost with what a business of your size, in your classifications, is expected to cost. The bureau’s plain page says actual losses are the medical and indemnity the insurer has paid or expects to pay, and expected losses are the statistical average for that size and industry. A modification under 100 is better than that average. One over 100 is worse. The simple ratio is actual divided by expected. The formula in force has extra pieces, so one bad Saturday does not throw a small dining room off the chart. Those pieces are the rest of this note.

You do not get a modification because you have a certain number of employees. The Experience Rating Plan effective September 1, 2026, which is Title 10 of the California Code of Regulations, section 2353.1, says a risk qualifies when the approved expected loss rates in Table I of that plan, applied to the remuneration that would be used in the calculation, come to at least $11,700. The prior threshold in that same plan’s memorandum was $10,800. Only completed policy periods count. If you qualify, you are experience rated.

Expected losses are payroll, per $100, times the expected loss rate for the classification. In Table I of this edition, classifications 9080, 9081, 9082, 9083, and 9084 each show an expected loss rate of $1.358. Those are the full-service, not-otherwise-classified, caterer, fast-food, and bar classes. The rate is an experience-rating rate. It is a different number from the advisory pure premium rate in the September note. I am not mixing them.

At $1.358 per $100 of payroll, $11,700 of expected losses is $11,700 divided by 1.358, times 100. That is $861,561 of payroll across the completed policies in the experience period, if every dollar of that payroll sits in one of those five classes. Split into three equal years, that is $287,187 a year. The plan does not publish a headcount, and it does not publish that payroll figure. The payroll figure is the division. A clerical class, a bakery class, or a split between two operations uses the rate for that class. What the plan counts as payroll, including tips, overtime, and the owner, is the audit note. A place under the line may have no modification at all. There is a narrow exception, in the same eligibility rule, for a risk that was experience rated the year before, when unaudited payroll is left out and the resulting modification is over 100. That path is the audit note too.

If you are experience rated, the plan says the bureau gives you one copy of the current Experience Rating Form free, on request. The notice also shows a loss-free rating, which is the hypothetical modification if you had no losses, or only claims the formula treats as zero. I want that worksheet. I will not guess your percentage from the sidewalk.

Primary is the part that moves the number

The plan’s formula, in Section VI, is actual primary losses, plus expected excess losses, divided by expected losses. Actual primary is the piece of your own claims the formula uses. Expected excess is the average excess for a business of your size and class. It is in the formula whether or not you had a large claim. Your own excess, the part of a claim above your primary threshold, gets no weight. The bureau’s page dates that shape to 2017. Before that, a slice of the excess could still count, more for a large employer than for a small one. Since 2017, the excess tail of your claim is out of the numerator.

The primary threshold is not one number for every restaurant. It depends on your total expected losses for the experience period. The same page says there are over 90 primary-loss values, from $4,500 to $75,000. Table II of the September 1, 2026 plan is the table. A place whose expected losses are under $7,083 has a threshold of $4,500. Expected losses of $4,928,289 and over have a threshold of $75,000. A just-eligible employer, at $11,700, falls in the row from $11,291 to $12,911. That row’s threshold is $6,500. A busier payroll produces more expected losses, and a higher threshold, so more of each claim counts as primary. I will not assign you $6,500 unless the worksheet’s expected losses are actually in that row.

Actual losses, before that split, are the indemnity incurred plus the medical incurred on the claim, and Table II caps that sum at a maximum loss value of $175,000. The formula then uses only the primary piece. For each claim, actual primary is zero when the loss is $250 or less. It is the loss minus $250 when the loss is over $250 and no more than your threshold. It is the threshold minus $250 when the loss is over the threshold.

The bureau’s formula page uses a $10,000 threshold as its example, which is a larger row than $6,500. On that example, a $5,000 claim enters the formula at $4,750, and a $15,000 claim enters at $9,750. The same subtraction, on the $6,500 row, sends a $5,000 claim in at $4,750 and sends anything over $6,500 in at $6,250. A $15,000 burn and a $5,000 cut do not hit a just-eligible café in proportion to the bills. The burn is capped. The cut is almost entirely inside the threshold.

One more limit, for a place with a single claim whose actual primary is above zero. The plan caps that modification at 25 percentage points above the loss-free modification. A claim of $250 or less is still printed on the worksheet, and the formula page says it does not count as that “one claim” for the cap. A second claim that clears $250 removes the cap. The cap also does not apply when the bureau has computed the modification by leaving unaudited payroll out. Two ordinary kitchen claims can matter more, on a small worksheet, than the tail of one bad one.

The figures below are the plan’s rule applied to a $6,500 threshold. They are an illustration for the eligibility-line row. They are not your modification, and they are not a price. On a phone, swipe the table.

The first $250, and a claim that is only medical

I looked for a separate rule that throws out medical-only claims, or that counts them at a percentage. The September 1, 2026 plan does not have one. Actual losses are the indemnity incurred plus the medical incurred. A claim that never pays a day of indemnity is still in that sentence, through the medical. What the plan does instead is take the first $250 off every claim. The formula page says that change was adopted so there would be no experience-rating reason to keep a small or first-aid claim off the report to the insurer. A claim of $250 or less stays on the worksheet and is not used in the calculation. A medical bill of $2,000, with no lost time, enters at $1,750 if your threshold is above that. If the person then loses time, the indemnity is added to the same incurred loss, and the primary piece is recomputed from the new total, still capped at the threshold minus $250.

The bureau’s plain page also says a published modification is not revised simply because a claim changes in value. The plan is stricter than a slogan. Section VI says values are not revised for developments in the injury between two valuation dates, except for a short list: a mistake other than an error of judgment, a claim found non-compensable, a subrogation recovery or a finding of fraud, a compromised death claim, or a joint-coverage claim. The data for a new modification is the latest unit statistical report due no later than one month before the rating date. A claim that gets more expensive does not rewrite the worksheet you already have. It can show up, at the new number, on a later worksheet that still includes that policy.

It stays for the experience period

The experience period is three years of completed policies. The plan says it commences four years and nine months before the modification date and terminates one year and nine months before that date. A policy is used when it incepts inside that window, and only after the policy period is completed. The claim from Thursday is not in the factor on a renewal you are holding this week.

Here is the subtraction for a policy that starts September 1, 2026, with the injury on November 26. September 1 minus nine months is December 1 of the year before. So a modification dated September 1 of year Y looks at policies that incept from December 1 of Y−5 through December 1 of Y−2.

September 1, 2027
The window runs from December 1, 2022 through December 1, 2025. A policy that started September 1, 2026 is outside it. Thursday’s burn is not in this modification.
September 1, 2028, 2029, and 2030
September 1, 2026 falls inside each of those windows. The policy has also finished, which the plan requires. The claim is in the formula for those three modifications, at whatever the incurred value is on the report used for that rating, then cut down to actual primary.
September 1, 2031
The window commences December 1, 2026. A policy that started on September 1, 2026 incepts before that date. It is outside this modification.

Your rating date may be March, or January, or the anniversary the bureau already assigned you. Do the same four-years-and-nine-months subtraction from that date. I am not going to pretend every dining room in Arcadia renews on September 1. The dates on the worksheet are the dates.

What the kitchen actually produces

The current statewide rate I can read on a page is for 2024, and it is the whole industry, not a split by burn and knife. The Bureau of Labor Statistics counts recordable injuries and illnesses per 100 full-time workers. Private industry was 2.3. Restaurants and other eating places were 2.4. Full-service restaurants were 2.2. Limited-service restaurants were 2.6. Snack and nonalcoholic beverage bars, which is the café row, were 2.7. BLS notes that the pieces may not add to the total, because of rounding, and that a dash means the figure did not meet publication guidelines.

The columns below are cases per 100 full-time workers in 2024. “Days away, restriction, or transfer” is BLS’s combined column. “Other recordable” is a case with neither days away nor a job transfer or restriction. On a phone, swipe the table.

Read the restaurant row next to private industry. The recordable rate is close. More of the restaurant cases are “other recordable,” 1.4 of the 2.4, and fewer are days-away or restricted-duty cases. A cut that is treated and finishes the shift can still be a recordable case. It is not, by itself, the indemnity that makes a claim expensive. It becomes that if the person cannot work.

The nature split I can read on a webpage is older, and I am dating it. The Bureau of Labor Statistics, on October 30, 2019, published 2017 rates for cases with days away from work, per 10,000 full-time workers. Restaurants and other eating places: sprains, strains, and tears 17.2; cuts, lacerations, and punctures 17.6; heat burns 9.1. All private industry: sprains 31.5; cuts 9.0; heat burns 1.4. In that year, a restaurant’s days-away cases were cuts and burns far more often than private industry as a whole, and sprains were still a large number. I am not going to lay those 2017 rates on top of the 2024 table. They are a different series. They are the reason the rest of this note is about a knife, a fryer, and a floor, and not about a ladder on a job site.

Cal/OSHA’s restaurant guide, the PDF titled Restaurant Employees Workplace Safety & Health Guide, lists the most common injury types in a kitchen as sprains and strains, from slips, trips, and falls, from overexertion in lifting, and from bending, climbing, reaching, and twisting; cuts, lacerations, and punctures from knives and other cutting tools; and burns and scalds from contact with hot substances such as oil and water. The file’s own metadata says it was created September 3, 2024. That list is the one I am cooking from. The 2024 incidence rates above say how often recordable cases show up. They do not, on the page I could read, split those cases into a burn and a knife for 2024.

What I want done before Thursday

The medical-legal fee schedule is not in the building. A few things are, and Thursday is close.

  1. Have a written Injury and Illness Prevention Program that somebody actually runs. Title 8, section 3203, says every employer establishes, implements, and maintains one, in writing. It names the person responsible. It has a way to get employees to follow safe practices. It communicates in a form the crew can understand, and it lets them report a hazard without reprisal. An employer with fewer than 10 employees may do that communication orally, with specific instructions for the hazards of the job. The program identifies hazards, including periodic inspections, an inspection when a new substance, process, procedure, or piece of equipment comes in, and an inspection when you learn of a hazard you had not recognized. It investigates an injury. It corrects the unsafe condition when it is found. If the hazard is imminent and cannot be fixed immediately, people come out of the area except the ones fixing it, and those people get the safeguards. Training happens when the program starts, for every new employee, for a new job the person has not been trained on, when a new hazard shows up, and for supervisors. An employee can get access within five business days, and one printed copy is free. Inspection records and training records are kept at least a year. An employer with fewer than 10 employees may keep the inspection record only until the hazard is corrected. A binder that has not been opened since the health inspection is not the program. A hire who starts Wednesday gets the training before the station, not during the rush.
  2. Put a cut-resistant glove on the knife hand, where the work is cutting. Title 8, section 3384, says you select, provide, and require hand protection when the hands are exposed to cuts, lacerations, punctures, chemical burns, or thermal burns. The exception for cuts is narrow. It applies when the hazard assessment finds that the risk of a cut is infrequent and superficial. A board that breaks down produce for a holiday service is not the picture in that exception. The same section says a glove does not get worn where it can be caught and pulled into moving machinery. The slicer is that sentence. The cutting board is the glove. Cal/OSHA’s guide says, where it is feasible, have people use cut-resistant gloves, and consider steel mesh or another type that can be kept sanitary. OSHA’s restaurant eTool says the same thing in the kitchen: steel mesh or Kevlar when cutting, and oven mitts or pot holders for hot items. The guide’s knife page wants the right knife, a cutting board, and the person in control of the blade. I want the glove on for that cutting, and I want it off the slicer.
  3. Leave the hot oil where it is until it is cool. OSHA’s eTool says deep fryers are the number one cause of burns in that guidance, and then it gets specific. Do not leave hot oil or grease unattended. Do not pour water or ice into oil. Do not spill a drink into the fryer, and do not keep a drink where it can be bumped in. Do not pour a pile of frozen fries in at once, and do not dump the ice crystals off the bag into the vat. Do not move a container of hot oil, and do not strain it until it is cool. Do not store oil on the floor by the grill, where a slip becomes a burn. Wear long sleeves, and slip-resistant shoes that are not canvas and not open-toed. Cal/OSHA’s guide says to let oils and other liquids cool. The water, the ice, and the instruction not to move or strain the oil while it is hot are the OSHA page. A worker younger than 16 does not cook, under the child-labor line on that OSHA page, except at a soda fountain, a lunch counter, a snack bar, or a cafeteria serving counter, and the page says a state rule can be stricter. A fifteen-year-old on a Thanksgiving shift does not take the fryer. If the oil does light, the extinguisher and the hood are the other note. A cook who is burned is this one.
  4. Keep the floor free of grease, and put a mat where the work is wet. Title 8, section 3273, says permanent floors stay in good repair and reasonably free of oil, grease, or water. Where the job has to happen on a slippery surface, you protect against slipping with mats, grates, cleats, or something equivalent. Where the process is wet, you keep drainage, and you provide false floors, platforms, mats, or other dry standing places. The dish pit and the mop sink are that sentence, not only the tile by the fryer. OSHA’s eTool wants the floor clean and dry, slip-resistant shoes, and nobody working tight to a hot fryer while the floor is wet. A mat that has curled is a trip. A mat that is soaked through is not a dry standing place. The guest at the door, the rain, and the roof are the rain note. The employee on the kitchen tile is the claim in the sections above.
  5. Hand over the claim form when the injury is past first aid, and do not sit on a small one to protect the modification. Labor Code section 5401 says that within one working day of notice of an injury that causes lost time beyond the shift, or medical treatment beyond first aid, you give the claim form and a notice of potential eligibility, in person or by first-class mail. The statute’s first aid is one-time treatment, and a follow-up to look at a minor scratch, cut, burn, splinter, or similar injury that does not ordinarily need medical care. The Division of Workers’ Compensation’s employer FAQ then lists the clock once you are in a claim: the form within one working day after it is reported, a copy back to the employee within one working day of getting it, the form and your report to the claims administrator within one working day of getting it, and, within one working day of receiving the claim, authorization of up to $10,000 in appropriate medical treatment. The bureau’s $250 exclusion is the reason I do not want a small bill hidden. A claim at $250 or less does not move the formula. A claim you do not report, that later becomes a lost-time file, is a worse story than a worksheet line at zero. Write down the time, the station, and what the oil, the knife, or the floor looked like, the same night, including a burn that finishes the shift.
  6. Bring the person back when the doctor says the work can be done. The division’s employer page says getting people back to work is one of the most important things you can do for their health and for the business, and that people who return as soon as medically possible recover faster and lose less in wages. The FAQ says to provide transitional work, light duty, whenever it is appropriate. If the treating doctor says the person cannot work at all while recovering, the person cannot be required to work. If the doctor writes restrictions, any work you assign has to meet them. Change the tasks, or change the equipment. If you do not have work that meets the restrictions, you cannot require the person to come in. If the doctor says the person will not be able to return to the job they had, the FAQ says you are encouraged to offer modified or alternative work instead of a supplemental job displacement benefit. That offer has to meet the restrictions, last at least 12 months, pay at least 85 percent of the wages and benefits at the time of injury, and sit within a reasonable commuting distance of where the person lived then. The person may have 30 days to accept. The dollar on the voucher, when an offer is not made, is the September note. I am not promising a lower modification because you wrote a light-duty schedule. I am telling you what the formula counts. Indemnity incurred is inside actual losses. Days away are how that indemnity gets into the number that later becomes actual primary, up to the threshold. A job inside the doctor’s note is the job. A job outside the note is not a return to work.

If you are opening, and Thursday is the first service with a crew, workers’ comp starts the day you have an employee. That list is the note on a new place. The January wage, which moves the payroll inside the expected-loss arithmetic above, is the other note.

If you would rather do this over a plate than over a worksheet, pick the restaurant. Bring the experience modification, or a photo of the class code and the percentage. I’ll buy.
