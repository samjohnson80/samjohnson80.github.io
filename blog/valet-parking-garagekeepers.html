The keys are not the general liability.

Thanksgiving is tomorrow. A holiday stand, or your own closer, is about to hold a guest’s keys. The dining-room form has a parking sentence. It was not written to pay for that car.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. Written for owners, not for insurance people. I am not your lawyer.

It shows up the same way every year, once the book gets bigger than the lot. A place in Pasadena puts a stand at the curb. A place in Monrovia hands the keys to a closer because the company that was supposed to show up did not. The guest comes out after dinner and the bumper is wrong, or the car is not where the claim check said it would be. Sometimes the guest paid for the valet. Sometimes the stand was complimentary, which is a different legal sentence and the same bad Saturday.

I am not going to tell you who wins that argument. Two different things get named, and they are not the same page. One is the guest’s car. The other is a person, or someone else’s car, that got hit while the guest’s car was being moved. The dining-room liability form can be in the conversation for the second one, in a narrow way. It is the wrong page for the first one. I would rather sort that while the vests are still in a box.

The car is a deposit. The drive is a different statute.

California calls this a deposit, not a vibe. Civil Code section 1814 says a voluntary deposit is made by one person giving to another, with that person’s consent, the possession of personal property to keep for the benefit of the first person, or of a third party. The person giving is the depositor. The person receiving is the depositary. Keys in a closer’s hand, or in a valet company’s box, are what that sentence is describing. A guest who locks the car and keeps the keys has not given possession away. I am not deciding that lawsuit. I am telling you the statute starts with giving possession.

If the deposit is not gratuitous, section 1851 calls it storage, and calls the depositary a depositary for hire. Section 1852 then says a depositary for hire must use at least ordinary care for the preservation of the thing deposited. A guest who pays a valet fee is the easier fit. The statute does not say the depositary pays for every scratch. It says at least ordinary care.

A complimentary stand is the other definition. Section 1844 says a gratuitous deposit is a deposit for which the depositary receives no consideration beyond the mere possession of the thing deposited. Section 1846(a) says a gratuitous depositary must use, at least, slight care for the preservation of the thing deposited. I will not decide from Huntington Drive whether the dinner inside is “consideration,” or whether the only thing the restaurant received was the car itself. That is the lawsuit. The policy question does not wait for it. If your people have the keys, the car is in someone’s care either way.

While the car is being driven, the Vehicle Code has a bailee sentence, and it is about operation. Section 17154 says every bailee of a motor vehicle is liable and responsible for death or injury to person or property resulting from a negligent or wrongful act or omission in the operation of the motor vehicle, in the business of the bailee or otherwise, by any person using or operating it with the permission, express or implied, of the bailee. A pole on the way to the garage is an operation question, if someone was negligent. A car that is simply gone from the key box, while nobody is driving it, is not that sentence. I am not going to stuff a theft into 17154.

There is a dollar cap in the next-door section, and I do not want it misunderstood. Section 17151(a) limits the liability of an owner, a bailee of an owner, or a personal representative, when that liability is imposed by this chapter and is “not arising through the relationship of principal and agent or master and servant,” to $15,000 for the death of or injury to one person in any one accident, $30,000 for more than one person, and $5,000 for damage to property of others. The amendment note on the section I read is 1967. The note on the catering car already walked through why those numbers are not the most a restaurant can owe when the driver is an employee. The cap sentence itself steps aside when the liability comes through the employment relationship. Do not hear $5,000 as the price of a guest’s bumper because a closer was on the clock. I am not deciding that lawsuit. I am telling you not to skip the words “not arising through.”

One more statute, because the claim check will try to do this work. Civil Code section 1630.5, added in 1970, says the provisions of any contract of bailment for the parking or storage of a motor vehicle shall not exempt the bailee from liability, in whole or in part, for the theft of any motor vehicle, when the vehicle is parked or stored with the bailee and the keys are required by the bailee to be left in the parked or stored vehicle. Read the last clause. A stand that takes the keys out and puts them in a box is not “left in the parked or stored vehicle.” I will not use 1630.5 to tear up a ticket that says “not responsible,” for a dent, or for a theft where the keys were in the vest.

Section 1668 is the broader sentence people hope will do it. All contracts which have for their object, directly or indirectly, to exempt anyone from responsibility for his own fraud, or willful injury to the person or property of another, or violation of law, whether willful or negligent, are against the policy of the law. The words are fraud, willful injury, and violation of law. I will not turn them into “every not-responsible line on a claim check is void.” Ordinary care is section 1852. I am not your lawyer.

The parking sentence does not pay for the car

The general liability form I can open is CG 00 01 04 13. A carrier can file its own words. A later edition can move one. The form on your declarations is the one that counts.

Exclusion g is Aircraft, Auto Or Watercraft. There is no coverage for bodily injury or property damage arising out of the ownership, maintenance, use, or entrustment to others of any auto owned or operated by, or rented or loaned to, any insured. Use includes operation and loading or unloading. The exclusion applies even if the claim is framed as negligence in the supervision, hiring, employment, training, or monitoring of others, when the occurrence involved that auto.

Then the exception owners hear about at the stand. The exclusion does not apply to parking an auto on, or on the ways next to, premises you own or rent, provided the auto is not owned by or rented or loaned to you or the insured. A guest’s car can fit the second half. It is not yours, and you did not rent it. The first half is the premises. Parking on, or on the ways next to, premises you own or rent. A garage you do not own or rent, on the other side of the block, is not a sentence I will finish for you. I will not call a drive down to a structure “the ways next to” the dining room unless the form you bought says so.

FC&S, on May 13, 2013, used a restaurant valet as the example of what that exception is for. If the valet staff is parking a customer’s car and hits a pedestrian or some object, FC&S said the general liability form will apply to that claim. It then said any damage to the car being driven by the valet staff may not be covered, because of the care, custody, or control exclusion. That is the split. The person who was hit can be the parking sentence. The guest’s bumper is the next exclusion.

Exclusion j(4), on the same form, is property damage to personal property in the care, custody, or control of the insured. The paragraphs that follow do not turn a guest’s car into a rented dining room. The give-back for property you rent is premises, including the contents, rented to you for seven or fewer consecutive days, and it is not damage by fire. A car is not that premises.

FC&S, on July 16, 2008, said the parking exception puts third-party bodily injury or property damage back on the form when a nonowned auto is being parked on the insured’s premises, and that the exception does not negate j(4). Their line, which I am keeping: what an exception giveth, an exclusion taketh away. The care, custody, or control exclusion takes away coverage for the damage to the vehicle in the insured’s care.

The restaurant version is FC&S, January 2, 2014. The insured was an upscale restaurant. Employees parked the cars. There was no charge. The attendant put the keys in a box. Someone talked to him, someone else took the keys, and the car left. The restaurant had a businessowners policy and no garagekeepers. FC&S said the parking language in the auto exclusion was not relevant to that claim. That language is the valet who hits another car or a person. The care, custody, or control exclusion was applicable, because the insured had the care and custody of someone else’s property, especially with the keys, and the theft happened then. The businessowners policy was not going to cover the damage to the car. FC&S was answering a form question. It was not a California judgment. It is the shape I recognize when the keys are yours and garagekeepers was never bought.

The parking sentence can put a pedestrian back on the dining-room form. It does not put the guest’s car back on it.

If a company has the keys, and your employee does not, I will not decide that your policy still has care, custody, or control. The guest will name the restaurant anyway. I want a page that answers for the car, not a theory about whose hand was on the key. The crash that hurts someone else, in a car the restaurant does not own, is the hired and non-owned note. This note is the guest’s car.

Garagekeepers is legal liability until a box is checked

The endorsement I am walking through is CA 99 37 08 15, Garagekeepers Coverage, on a specimen that modifies a commercial general liability coverage part and says it includes copyrighted material of Insurance Services Office. The other edition I opened, CA 99 37 10 01, modifies a business auto, motor carrier, or truckers coverage form. Same title. Not the same page. A carrier can file its own words. The edition on your declarations, or on the valet company’s, is the one that counts.

On the 08 15 copy, the endorsement provides only those coverages where a limit of insurance and a premium are shown in the schedule, and only for the location shown in the schedule. A blank line is not a yes. A location that is the company’s yard in another city, and not the restaurant, is a question I want answered before Saturday.

The grant, before any direct-coverage box, is legal liability. We will pay all sums the insured legally must pay as damages for loss to a customer’s auto, or a customer’s auto equipment, left in the insured’s care while the insured is attending, servicing, repairing, parking, or storing it in the insured’s garage operations. Three coverages sit under that sentence. Comprehensive is loss from any cause except the customer’s auto’s collision with another object, or its overturn. Specified causes of loss are fire, lightning, or explosion; theft; or mischief or vandalism. Collision is the customer’s auto’s collision with another object, or its overturn. A pole is collision. A car taken from the key box can be theft, which specified causes names, and which comprehensive does not carve out. If the schedule shows comprehensive and the collision line is blank, I will not treat the pole as bought. If the only line with a premium is collision, I will not treat the missing car as bought.

The direct-coverage options are two boxes, and the form says to indicate which, if any, is selected.

Excess insurance, if that box is checked: garagekeepers coverage remains applicable on a legal liability basis. Coverage also applies without regard to your legal liability, or any other insured’s, for loss to a customer’s auto, on an excess basis over any other collectible insurance. It does not matter whether that other insurance covers your interest or the owner’s interest. If the guest’s own policy pays for the car, this box is written to sit above it. If there is no other collectible insurance, the excess sentence is the one I want the company to read with you, in writing. I will not turn “excess” into “the guest’s insurer always pays first, and then we do.” “Collectible” is the word.

Primary insurance, if that box is checked: garagekeepers coverage is changed to apply without regard to your legal liability, or any other insured’s, for loss to a customer’s auto, and it is primary insurance.

If neither box is checked on the endorsement, look at the declarations before you assume direct coverage was bought. The 10 01 copy says that if no entry appears, the information required to complete the endorsement will be shown in the declarations. A blank box is not a story. It is a missing mark.

Legal liability is the part owners skip. If the company was not legally liable for the loss, the grant I just quoted does not pay, unless a direct box changes that. Section 1852’s ordinary care, or section 1846’s slight care, is the kind of question that decides legal liability. A direct box is how the form pays without having that fight, either as excess over other collectible insurance or as primary. I will not pick the box for you. I will not let a quote sheet say “garagekeepers” and stop there.

Who is an insured, on the 08 15 copy, is you. It is your partners, and their spouses, if you are a partnership, for the conduct of your garage operations. It is your members, if you are a limited liability company, for those operations, and your managers, for their duties as managers. It is your executive officers and directors, for their duties, and your stockholders, for their liability as stockholders. It is your employees, other than executive officers or managers in the cases the form carves out, for acts within the scope of employment or while performing duties related to the garage operations. The restaurant across the sidewalk is not on that list. The 10 01 copy’s list is shorter, and the restaurant is not on that list either. A certificate that names the city, or even names you, on a general liability policy, has not performed this paragraph.

Two exclusions matter more than the title, and they survive the conversation about the boxes. I am not telling you a checked box erases them. They are different paragraphs.

Theft. On both editions I opened, the insurance does not apply to loss due to theft or conversion caused in any way by you, your employees, or your stockholders. The 10 01 copy says shareholders. A stranger at the key box is not that sentence. A closer who drives the car away and does not bring it back is the sentence I want you to have read before you hear “we have garagekeepers” as “a missing car is handled.”

Contract. On the 08 15 copy, there is no coverage for liability resulting from any contract or agreement by which the insured accepts responsibility for loss. The exclusion does not apply to liability for loss that the insured would have had in the absence of the contract. On the 10 01 copy, the exclusion is liability resulting from any agreement by which the insured accepts responsibility for loss. I did not find, on that copy, the sentence that gives back the liability the insured would have had anyway. A hold-harmless that makes the valet company pay even when it was not negligent can be the agreement this exclusion is about. Direct coverage is a different checkbox. Bring both pages. I will not finish the argument between them from the office.

The limit is for each loss at each location. On the 08 15 copy, regardless of the number of customer’s autos, insureds, premiums, claims, or suits, the most the company will pay for each loss at each location is the garagekeepers limit shown for that location. One loss does not grow because more than one car was in it. The deductibles come off before that limit. Collision has a deductible for each customer’s auto. Comprehensive and specified causes have a deductible for each auto for theft, mischief, or vandalism, or for all perils, and a maximum deductible for all of that loss in any one event. If the company pays the deductible to settle, you reimburse it.

There is a defense sentence. The company has the right and duty to defend an insured against a suit asking for these damages, and no duty to defend a suit for loss the insurance does not apply to. The duty to defend or settle ends for a coverage when that coverage’s limit has been exhausted by judgments or settlements. On the 08 15 copy, the supplementary payments, including the company’s expenses, do not reduce the limit.

One definition, before you staple this endorsement to a dining room and call it done. On the 08 15 copy, garage operations means the ownership, maintenance, or use of locations for the purpose of a business of selling, servicing, repairing, parking, or storing customer’s autos, and that portion of the roads or other accesses that adjoin those locations. It also includes operations necessary or incidental to that. A company whose business is parking fits the first words more cleanly than a café whose business is dinner. If the endorsement is on your policy, I want that definition read against the location on the schedule. I will not decide that a dining room is a garage. A customer’s auto, on that copy, is a land motor vehicle, trailer, or semitrailer lawfully within your possession for service, repair, storage, or safekeeping, with or without the owner’s knowledge or consent. It also includes a vehicle left by your employees, and members of their households, who pay for the services. I am quoting the definition. I am not adjusting the claim.

Pasadena, if the street is involved

I opened Pasadena because the city put the holiday example on its own application, and because a lot of the valley’s December books end up on a Pasadena curb. I am not going to tell you Arcadia’s rule, or Monrovia’s, from Pasadena’s chapter. If the stand is in another city, ask that city before the cones go out.

The chapter I could open is the October 4, 2026 compilation of Pasadena Municipal Code Chapter 12.14. The city’s municipal-code page says Book Publishing Company keeps the official online code. I did not get that copy to open. Where the compilation and the city’s own packet say the same thing, I am using both. If the clerk’s copy differs, the clerk’s copy is the one that counts. The packet I opened is the sponsor application and the operator application. The sponsor form says it is valid July 1, 2026 through June 30, 2027. The fee sheet says the same dates.

Section 12.14.030 defines a valet parking operation as receiving, taking possession of, driving, moving, parking, or leaving standing any vehicle that is left at one location to be driven to another location for parking, whether or not a charge is levied, and whether or not it is done under contract. It does not include a parking lot where customers park their own vehicles and remove the keys themselves. A lot where the guest keeps the keys is not this chapter’s valet. A stand that takes the keys is.

Section 12.14.040 makes it unlawful to operate a valet parking operation on a public street or right-of-way, on other city property, or in a city-owned parking facility, except under a current operator’s permit and, if the drop-off or pick-up uses the public street or right-of-way in any manner, under a current sponsor’s permit, with an official valet sign for that location. The section also says that on private property the additional authorization of the owner or lessee is required. I will not read that last sentence as “a private lot needs no permit.” Section 12.14.060 says a special event sponsor permit is not required for special event valet services entirely on private property. Section 12.14.090 says a plan may not be required, in the director’s discretion, if all driving and parking is entirely on private property, and that if any portion of any public street or other public right-of-way is driven on, a plan is required. The holiday stand at the curb is the street. A lot behind the building, if the cars never touch the street, is the sentence I want you to read before you assume the packet does not apply. I am not the director.

There are two permits. Section 12.14.050 says the sponsor gets a sponsor’s permit for the place of business, and the operator gets an operator’s permit to operate anywhere in the city. The sponsor, not the operator, submits the sponsor application, and the sponsor is responsible for seeing that the operator complies with the chapter. Violations by the operator subject the sponsor, as well as the operator, to the sanctions in the chapter. The city’s sponsor rules say the same thing in shorter words: the sponsor is ultimately responsible for the operator’s compliance. Changing companies does not, by itself, kill the sponsor permit. The new operator needs a valid operator permit, and the sponsor is supposed to tell the department.

A one-night party does not create a one-night operator. Section 12.14.060 says there are no special event operator’s permits. An operator has to have a current regular operator’s permit to operate anywhere in the city, and the sponsor submits a separate certificate of insurance for each location. The special event sponsor permit is for a nonrecurring event, or for valet required for 30 calendar days or less. The city’s own example of that permit is valet for a holiday party. Its example of an annual permit is valet year-round for a restaurant. A special event sponsor permit may be renewed for one additional 30-day period. A stand every Friday from this weekend through New Year’s is more than one night, and the regular-permit sentence is the one for a location likely to be operated more than 30 calendar days in a calendar year. Do not file a party permit for a season, and do not hire a company that has no operator permit and hope the party form covers the driver.

The clock is in the packet. The code says a regular application has to be in at least 20 working days before the operation starts, and that the city cannot guarantee approval of a special event application filed less than 10 working days before the event. The sponsor packet says the estimated processing time for an annual permit is 20 working days, and 10 working days for a special event. It also says any new sponsor or operator application will require a public hearing, and that this process normally takes four to six weeks. The code’s hearing section is narrower than that banner. Section 12.14.085 says the director shall hold a public hearing on a new regular operator permit and a new sponsor permit, not on a special event and not on a renewal, and that if no concern is registered, the director may elect not to hold the hearing. I will not smooth those into one rule. If the party is the first week of December and nothing has been filed, the packet you would be filing is the one that says ten working days, and a brand-new operator is the hearing, not the ten days.

Regular sponsor permits expire on June 30, without notice, unless renewed. Operator permits expire on the anniversary of the day they were first issued, not on June 30. The packet in my hand is the July 1, 2026 through June 30, 2027 packet. If the copy at the stand still says last year, that is the conversation. I am not accusing anyone. I am reading the expiration sentence.

The fee sheet, for that same July-to-June year, is what I will quote, and the packet says fees are reviewed annually. An application processing fee of $86. A special event sponsor permit fee of $177. An annual sponsor permit fee of $104. An annual operator permit fee of $115. An expedited fee of $236 if the permit is not submitted in the specified time frame. A new sponsor or new operator public-hearing fee of $1,269, nonrefundable, on a separate check. Street usage, for a non-metered space, is $1 an hour. A parking meter cover is the full meter rate. The application form says a maximum of two spaces unless otherwise approved. Those are the city’s fees for the permit. They are not the cost of the dent.

Two operating rules, because they show up on the sidewalk. The operator, on receiving each car, gives a claim check that explicitly states the terms and conditions under which the car is being accepted. That is section 12.14.090 and the city’s operator rules. The sponsor posts a sign, during business hours, at each place they take possession of vehicles. The sign identifies the operator’s name, address, and telephone number, the rates, and the hours, and it has to be approved in advance. Section 12.14.090 also says an operator may charge the guest only if the sponsor has agreed in writing that fees may be charged, and only with advance notice of the fee. A complimentary stand that starts charging on Saturday night, because the book got big, is that sentence.

The insurance section does not contain a dollar limit. Section 12.14.130 says the city council shall by resolution establish, from time to time, the minimum levels and standards of liability insurance and claims reserve that have to be maintained, and the certification requirements, in order to apply for a valet permit and to operate with one. Failure to maintain those minimums is grounds for revocation. I looked for the resolution. I am not going to print a limit I did not find. The operator application asks for a current certificate of insurance along with the endorsements, and it says the certificate must list the City of Pasadena as certificate holder. The sponsor application says the city must be listed as an additional insured on general liability policies, and it points to the operator application for the complete insurance details. The operator application I opened does not contain a limit, a garagekeepers line, or an auto line. Ask the parking office what the current resolution requires. The packet sends that application to the Department of Transportation at 100 N. Garfield Avenue, Room N106.

The indemnity in section 12.14.120 runs to the city. The permittee, and any person acting under an operator’s or sponsor’s permit, agrees to indemnify, hold harmless, release, and defend the city, its council and each member, and its officers, employees, commission members, and representatives, from liability, loss, suits, claims, damages, costs, and expenses that result from the use of the permit, the activities of the operator or sponsor and their employees, the condition of property used in the operation, or their acts, errors, or omissions in connection with the valet operation. The section says the indemnity includes personal injury and property damage sustained by any person, including valet parking customers. That is Pasadena protecting Pasadena. It does not name your restaurant. The city’s paragraph is not your endorsement.

Section 12.14.130 also says the permittee must show compliance with section 37000 and following of the California Labor Code. That is the citation printed in the compilation I read. The workers’ compensation statute I use with a restaurant is Labor Code section 3700, which says every employer except the state shall secure the payment of compensation. I am not going to relabel 37000 as 3700. The point I will use is the one both documents are reaching for. A valet permit is not a substitute for the compensation an employer has to secure. If the attendants are the company’s employees, that is the company’s duty to sort out. If they are yours, they are yours. How the premium moves is the note on the September benchmark. A closer who is hurt at the stand is that policy. Garagekeepers is the guest’s car.

What I want before the vest goes on

This is the order, while the keys are still in the guest’s pocket.

  1. The contract, not the text thread. Who takes possession. Where the cars are stored. Whether the guest can be charged, which in Pasadena requires the sponsor’s written agreement and advance notice of the fee. If the cars go to a facility, Pasadena wants a written contract, license, or lease with that facility, and it cannot be cancelled without at least thirty days’ written notice to the other party and to the city. That is section 12.14.080 and the sponsor checklist. A handshake with a garage is not that contract.
  2. The certificate and the endorsements. The operator application already asks for both, and it asks that the city be the certificate holder. A certificate that does the city’s line has not named you. I want the endorsement that adds the restaurant, on the liability that applies to the driving, and I want to see whether anyone added the restaurant to the garagekeepers coverage. On the who-is-an-insured paragraphs I read, the restaurant is not an insured because the valet company is. The lease note is how I read an additional insured and a hold-harmless that were written for a landlord. This is the same reading, for a vest.
  3. The garagekeepers schedule, from the edition that was actually issued. Which location. Which of comprehensive, specified causes, and collision has a limit and a premium. Which direct box, if any, is checked. The deductible for each auto, and the maximum for one event. A blank collision line is not a dent. Legal liability with no direct box is not a promise to pay for a car when the company was not negligent. The theft-by-employee exclusion is on the page. Read it out loud before you hear the coverage title as the whole coverage.
  4. The hold-harmless, read against the contract exclusion. I want a written promise that names the restaurant. I also want the edition. The 08 15 copy gives back liability the insured would have had without the contract. The 10 01 copy I opened does not contain that sentence. A hold-harmless is not coverage. Coverage is the form, after the exclusion has been read.
  5. If your own staff are the valets, there is no other company’s certificate. You are the one taking possession. Ask whether garagekeepers is on your policy, at your address, with a premium on the cause of loss you actually fear, and which box is checked. Read the garage-operations definition against a business whose purpose is dinner. The dining-room liability is the wrong page for the car. The pedestrian at your own curb may be the parking exception, and only on or next to premises you own or rent, and only for a car that is not yours. The closer’s own injury is workers’ compensation.

If the only valet all year is one Saturday, say that, and then file the permit that matches one Saturday, with a company that already holds the operator permit. If the stand is going to live on the curb through the holidays, say that too. It is a different permit, and the expiration date is June 30, not the week you take the sign down. If nobody takes the keys, and the guest parks and locks the car, Pasadena’s definition says that is not a valet operation. Do not buy a garagekeepers story for a lot you do not possess, and do not skip the question if a closer is already holding a fob.

Lunch is still the easier way to lay the pages on a table. Bring the contract. Bring the certificate and the endorsements. Bring the garagekeepers schedule. I’ll buy.
