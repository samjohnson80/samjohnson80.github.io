The night drop is not the property policy.

The Saturday after Thanksgiving is cash, voids, and a bag that still has to reach the bank. A package can insure the building and still leave the drawer, a forged check, a fake hundred, and a vendor’s new account number with you. Here is the page I want read before that Saturday.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. Written for owners, not for insurance people. I am not your lawyer.

The second week of November is when the drawer gets honest. A buyout pays cash because the card machine is already behind. The host stand is holding gift cards for parties that are real. Somebody walks a bag to the night drop after close, and that somebody knows the alarm code. Then the owner wants to know which policy answers if the bag is light. What the examiners actually counted

On May 12, 2026, the Association of Certified Fraud Examiners released Occupational Fraud 2026: A Report to the Nations. It is 2,402 cases that Certified Fraud Examiners investigated between January 2024 and September 2025, in 143 countries. The cases caused more than $3.4 billion in losses. The median loss was $104,000. The average was $1,457,000, and 20 percent of the cases were over a million dollars. The press release and the report both say the examiners estimate that organizations lose 5 percent of revenue to fraud each year. The same report says the true prevalence may never be fully measurable, because the fraud is hidden. I am not putting 5 percent on a Saturday in Monrovia. An estimate is not your tape.

Food service and hospitality is one line. Fifty-three cases. A median loss of $100,000. A later table, the one that shows percentiles, lists 50 food-service cases, a 25th percentile of $17,000, and the same $100,000 median. I am not going to guess why three cases sit in one count and not the other. The report says the industry split reflects the cases the examiners submitted. It does not say a café is riskier than a bank. It is not a count of the San Gabriel Valley. A tip from a busser is not this study, and a payment from an insurer is not this study.

Asset misappropriation, an employee stealing or misusing the organization’s resources, was in 90 percent of all the cases in the study, with a median loss of $100,000. That 90 percent is every industry. Inside the 53 food-service cases, the heat map shows corruption in 45 percent, billing in 30 percent, theft of noncash assets in 28 percent, and expense reimbursements in 25 percent. The schemes that look like a dining room are on the same row, and they are smaller slices: check and payment tampering 19 percent, skimming 15 percent, cash on hand 11 percent, cash larceny 6 percent, register disbursements 6 percent. The heat map adds past 100 percent. One case can sit in more than one column.

The glossary is the kitchen. I am using the report’s definitions, not a story from a table I sat at. Skimming is an incoming payment stolen before anyone writes it down. Cash larceny is a payment stolen after it is on the books and before it reaches the bank. The report’s example is cash and checks taken from the daily receipts. Cash on hand is money kept at the premises. The example is a vault. Register disbursements are false entries on a cash register to hide cash leaving. The example is a fraudulent void. Check and payment tampering is intercepting, forging, or altering a check or an electronic payment drawn on the organization’s account, or rerouting a payment that was supposed to go to a vendor. Billing is a fake invoice, an inflated invoice, or a personal purchase run through the company. A comp for a steak that really was cold is not a definition I just read. A void that exists so the drawer will match a pocket is the shape of the register-disbursement example. I will not decide a particular ticket from a blog.

Smaller places show more of the cash schemes. In organizations with fewer than 100 employees, set next to organizations with 100 or more, billing was in 28 percent of cases against 18 percent, skimming in 17 percent against 6 percent, and check and payment tampering in 18 percent against 8 percent. The report calls those three the largest gaps. Corruption was still the most common scheme on both sides of that line, 40 percent and 42 percent. A dining room with a dozen people on the schedule is the smaller column. The median loss at organizations with fewer than 100 employees was $126,000, and the report says a loss like that is typically a larger share of a small organization’s revenue. Typically is the report’s word.

How the cases were found is the part I can use before Thanksgiving. Forty-three percent were detected by a tip, and 55 percent of those tips came from employees. The median case lasted 12 months. A third of the cases were caught in the first six months, and those had a median loss of $40,000. Where management review was already in place, the median loss was $84,000 and the median duration was 10 months. Where it was not, $186,000 and 18 months. Surprise audits lined up with a median loss of $74,000 and a duration of 8 months, against $149,000 and 16 months where there was no surprise audit. The report says the controls were associated with those differences. It does not promise that a surprise count on a Thursday cuts a loss in half. When a lack of internal controls was named as the primary reason the fraud happened, median losses were 43 percent higher than the overall median. The checklist in the back of the report asks whether the organization has separation of duties, authorizations, and physical safeguards. That is the list I can put on a closing sheet. The percentages are not the closing sheet.

Fifty-three food-service cases, and a median of $100,000, is not a census of the valley. It is the shape of the file the examiners were handed.

Two small doors, and the optional page

The most it pays for any loss, including those legal expenses, is $2,500, unless a higher limit is shown in the declarations. The legal expenses are inside the $2,500 on this wording, not stacked on top of it. What a crime policy adds, where a limit is typed

The policy I can open on a loss-sustained basis is CR 00 23 06 22, Commercial Crime Policy (Loss Sustained Form). The discovery twin I can open is CR 00 22 06 22. On both, coverage is provided under the insuring agreements for which a limit is shown in the declarations. A crime policy with a number next to employee theft, and a blank next to everything else, is employee theft. It is not automatically the night drop, the counterfeit hundred, and the vendor’s new account. Read the lines.

Employee theft pays for loss of or damage to money, securities, and other property resulting directly from theft by an employee, whether identified or not, acting alone or in collusion with other persons. For this agreement, theft includes forgery. The employee does not have to be named. A pattern of voids, when you cannot yet say which server, is why that clause is in the sentence. Money, under this agreement, includes deposits in your account at a financial institution, not only the currency in the drawer. A bookkeeper who moves the operating account is this agreement, if the limit is there. Other property is tangible property other than money and securities that has intrinsic value. The wine can be that, if you can prove the loss without leaning only on an inventory or a profit-and-loss computation. That computation exclusion is on the employee-theft agreement. Where you establish the loss wholly apart from those computations, you may offer inventory records and an actual physical count to support the amount.

An employee, on these forms, includes a natural person in your service whom you compensate by salary, wages, or commissions and whom you have the right to direct, including the first 30 days after the job ends, unless you ended it for theft or another dishonest act. It includes a person furnished to you temporarily to fill in for someone on leave, or to meet a seasonal or short-term workload, while you direct them and they are performing services for you. Holiday help you pay and direct is a conversation inside that definition. A friend who is not paid, and who is not on that temporary list, is not a person I will call an employee from this note.
