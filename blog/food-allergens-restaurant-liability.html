The peanut on the menu is not the peanut in the fryer

Since July 1, a California chain of 20 or more has had to name the major allergens in each menu item. A single café is not that chain. The guest who reacts is still a bodily-injury claim.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. I am not your health inspector, and I am not your lawyer. I am the person who has to read the general liability form when the complaint names the dish.

The guest says it before the water lands. No peanuts. Or no sesame, and the server has already nodded. You want to know whether the menu law means the nod was not enough, and whether the policy pays if the kitchen got the nod wrong. Those are two different pieces of paper. I can read the first one with you. The second one is the declarations page, and I will not guess it from the sidewalk.

Who actually has to print it

Senate Bill 68, by Senator Menjivar, is Chapter 741 of the Statutes of 2025. The Governor approved it on October 13, 2025, and it was filed with the Secretary of State the same day. It amended the allergen definition in the Retail Food Code and added Health and Safety Code section 114093.5. The code section took effect January 1, 2026. The menu duty inside it commenced July 1, 2026. That date has passed. If you are in the group the section names, the inspector can already ask to see the writing.

The section does not cover every food facility. It covers a food facility that serves or sells food to the consumer and is subject to section 343(q)(5)(H) of Title 21 of the United States Code. That is the federal calorie rule. It applies to a standard menu item sold in a restaurant or similar retail food establishment that is part of a chain with 20 or more locations, doing business under the same name, regardless of the type of ownership of the locations, and offering for sale substantially the same menu items. A franchise counts. The federal regulation’s phrase for “substantially the same menu items” is a significant proportion of items that use the same general recipe and are prepared in substantially the same way, with substantially the same food components, even if the name on the menu changes. The same definition says some items can vary inside the chain and the chain can still qualify.

One dining room, your name on the door and nobody else’s, is not that chain. Most of the owners I sit down with in Monrovia, Arcadia, Pasadena, and the rest of the valley are that dining room. I want the relief and the leftover duty in the same breath. The menu statute is probably not your printing job. The training statute further down is. The guest is either way.

Two ways a smaller place can still be inside the menu rule. You may be the one Arcadia shop of a brand that has the twenty. Your name on the lease does not take you out. The federal section says the ownership of the locations does not matter. Or you, or the brand, may have volunteered. Title 21 of the Code of Federal Regulations, section 101.11(d), lets a restaurant that is not part of a chain of 20 register with the FDA to be subject to the calorie rule. The registration certifies that each registered establishment will be subject to section 403(q)(5)(H) of the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, which is the same federal section California cited. I have not seen a court stitch that registration onto section 114093.5 for a single café. If Form FDA 3757 already went to the FDA, tell me before I tell you the menu statute is not yours.

What the writing has to say

The list is in section 113820.5, as SB 68 amended it, effective January 1, 2026. A major food allergen is milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, wheat, peanuts, soybeans, and sesame, plus a food ingredient that contains protein derived from any of those. The statute’s own examples are bass, flounder, and cod; crab, lobster, and shrimp; almonds, pecans, and walnuts. Sesame is the name the bill added to the state list. On a packaged food, the FDA has required sesame to be labeled as a major allergen since January 1, 2023. The FDA’s consumer page walks through the same nine.

The definition leaves out a highly refined oil derived from one of those foods, and any ingredient derived from that oil. It also leaves out an ingredient that is exempt under the petition or notification process in the federal Food Allergen Labeling and Consumer Protection Act of 2004. That is a labeling definition. It is not a medical clearance. I will not tell you a refined oil is safe for a guest who just told you they have the allergy. Section 114093.5 says the menu section does not alter any duty a food facility may already have to reasonably ensure the safety of its patrons.

What you disclose is the major food allergen the facility knows, or reasonably should know, is contained as an ingredient in each menu item. The words are “contained as ingredients.” A shared fryer is not an ingredient. I read the section for a cross-contact warning and did not find one. I am not handing the fryer a pass. The safety sentence is still in that section, and the training rules below were already the law for a food facility, chain or not.

The facility picks one of two ways. Put a written statement on the menu, below the item or immediately adjacent to it, naming the major allergens in that item. Or give the information in a digital format, and the statute’s example is a QR code that links to the digital menu. If you go digital, you also need an alternative for a guest who cannot get to the screen. The examples written into the section are a separate allergen-specific menu, an allergen chart, an allergen grid, an allergen booklet, or other written materials.

You use the common or usual name of the allergen, or a standardized pictogram. The section does not name whose pictogram. If you use a symbol, I still want the word where a guest can read it. That part is me.

“Menu,” for this section, means what the federal regulation calls a menu or menu board: the primary writing the guest orders from. The regulation’s list includes breakfast, lunch, and dinner menus, dessert menus, beverage menus, children’s menus, other specialty menus, electronic menus, and menus on the internet. Menu boards include the board inside and the drive-through board. California said each menu item, and it borrowed that definition of menu. It did not borrow the federal calorie rule’s exceptions for a daily special or a temporary item. I will not write an exemption the statute left out. If the special is on the writing the guest orders from, I would put the allergens on that writing.

Three things are outside the section. Prepackaged food that already has to carry the federal allergen label. A compact mobile food operation. A nonpermanent food facility. A compact mobile food operation is the nonmotorized one: an individual, or a pushcart, stand, display, pedal-driven cart, wagon, showcase, rack, or other nonmotorized conveyance. A nonpermanent food facility operates from a mobile unit or at a nonpermanent location. The definition includes a mobile food facility, a temporary food facility, a certified farmers’ market, a fishermen’s market, a mobile support unit, and a vending machine. A truck is a mobile food facility. The menu statute skipped the truck. A dining room with a front door did not get that skip.

The enforcement check in the section can be visual. An enforcement officer may confirm that the statements are on the printed menu, the digital menu, or the paper alternative. The section also allows another reasonable method consistent with its intent. It does not describe a laboratory test.

The fine I will not mix up with the calorie labels. Section 114395 says that, except as otherwise provided, a person who violates this part of the code is guilty of a misdemeanor. The punishment in that section is a fine of not less than twenty-five dollars or more than one thousand dollars, or up to six months in the county jail, or both. Section 114397 says the owner, manager, or operator is responsible for an employee’s violation, and that each day is a separate offense. The calorie-label section, 114094, has a different schedule. A violation of that section is an infraction, notwithstanding 114395, with fines that start at not less than fifty dollars and not more than five hundred, and climb on a repeat within five years. That schedule is for a violation of 114094. Section 114093.5 does not point at it. I am not moving those dollar figures onto an allergen menu, and I am not telling you whether a guest can sue over a missing line. The guest’s bodily injury is a different file from the fine.

The single café still has to teach it

Section 113947 is not limited to a chain of 20. The person in charge, and all food employees, need adequate knowledge of food safety for the duties they are assigned, and they need to be trained for those duties. The person in charge has to know major food allergens, the foods identified as major food allergens, and the symptoms a major food allergen could cause in a sensitive person who has a reaction. The person in charge has to educate the employees on that. A poster or a job aid is one way the statute allows. “Person in charge,” in this section, means a designated person who knows safe food handling and the major food allergens as they relate to the specific preparation that happens at that facility. Your line, not a generic chain manual.

Section 113948 is the card. A food handler hired on or after June 1, 2011, subject to the exceptions in that section, gets a food handler card within 30 days of hire and keeps a valid card while working as a food handler. The card lasts three years. The course has to include the allergen knowledge in section 113947, consistent with recommendations from a nationally organized allergy organization, and safe handling of major food allergens for the preparation at a food facility, including training on the avoidance of allergen cross-contamination. The code uses “cross-contamination.” The FDA’s page on wiping and cleaning in retail food establishments uses another word for the kitchen problem: cross-contact, which it defines as the unintentional incorporation of a food allergen into food.

The employer counts the training time as hours worked, pays it, and pays the necessary cost of the card. The employee is relieved of other work during the course. The employer cannot condition the job on the applicant already having a card. The section has a real list of exceptions, including certified farmers’ markets, grocery stores, school cafeterias, and a food facility that is under a collective bargaining agreement with its food handlers. A normal dining room is not on that list. Read subdivision (e) before you decide you are. The definition the course points at, section 113820.5, has included sesame since January 1, 2026.

The symptoms, so the person in charge is not inventing them. The FDA’s consumer page says they can show up within a few minutes or a few hours, and they are not the same every time or for every person. The list on that page includes hives, flushed skin or a rash, a tingling or itchy feeling in the mouth, swelling of the face, tongue, or lips, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal cramps, coughing or wheezing, dizziness, swelling of the throat and vocal cords, difficulty breathing, and loss of consciousness. The FDA says most symptoms are mild and limited to the skin or to digestion, and that some progress to anaphylaxis: constricted airways in the lungs, a severe drop in blood pressure and shock, and suffocation by swelling of the throat and larynx. The same page says a mild reaction does not mean the allergy is mild, and that any allergic reaction has the potential to lead to anaphylaxis. I am not a doctor. The FDA’s instruction to the person who is reacting is to stop eating, evaluate emergency treatment such as epinephrine, and seek medical attention. For the restaurant, the plan I want names the person who calls 911. I am not writing a medical protocol here.

The CDC’s restaurant page is a plain-language summary of Environmental Health Specialists Network studies. One of the papers it cites is from six selected sites in the United States in 2014. The page says about one in three people with food allergies report having had a reaction in a restaurant, and that more than half of the staff those studies interviewed had never been trained on food allergies. Some managers and staff incorrectly believed a person with a food allergy could safely eat a small amount of that allergen. Training often skipped what to do if a customer has a reaction. Staff with less experience in that restaurant knew less. I am not turning a 2014 study into a head count of Colorado Boulevard. I am keeping the four actions that page tells a restaurant to take. Train staff, especially the less experienced ones. Use separate equipment and areas for an allergy order when you can, and if you cannot, thoroughly clean and sanitize the surfaces and equipment first. Keep ingredient lists or recipes for the menu. Have a plan for serving a guest with a food allergy. The page says staff knew more about food allergies when the restaurant had a plan.

The menu can be right and the plate can still be wrong.

What the claim looks like

The form I can point at in public is ISO’s occurrence form, CG 00 01 04 13. A carrier can file its own wording. A later edition can add a word. The endorsement number on your forms list is the one that counts.

On this edition, bodily injury means bodily injury, sickness, or disease sustained by a person, including death resulting from any of these at any time. An allergic reaction is that kind of harm when the facts make it one. I am not diagnosing a guest from Huntington Drive.

Coverage A pays the sums the insured becomes legally obligated to pay as damages because of bodily injury or property damage to which the insurance applies. The company has the right and duty to defend the insured against a suit seeking those damages, and it has no duty to defend a suit seeking damages for injury the insurance does not apply to. The duty to defend ends when the company has used up the applicable limit paying judgments or settlements under Coverage A or B, or medical expenses under Coverage C. The company’s own defense expenses are supplementary payments. On this edition, those payments do not reduce the limits.

The dish is “your product” when you manufactured, sold, handled, distributed, or disposed of it. The definition also includes warranties or representations about the fitness, quality, durability, performance, or use of the product, and the providing of or failure to provide warnings or instructions. A wrong answer about what is in the bowl sits in that last clause. This is not a separate product-liability policy sitting next to general liability. IRMI’s products-liability slides, quoting this edition, say an unendorsed commercial general liability policy includes liability coverage for bodily injury or property damage arising from your product, absent a specific exclusion. I will read the exclusions on the policy that was issued before I say that sentence about yours.

Which aggregate the payment spends is the next page, and it is where restaurants get sloppy. On CG 00 01 04 13, the products-completed operations hazard includes bodily injury and property damage occurring away from premises you own or rent and arising out of your product or your work. Products still in your physical possession are excepted. FC&S, on September 20, 2011, put the restaurant version in plain words. A customer who gets sick on the premises from the food is paid under the general aggregate. A customer who takes the food home, or gets sick away from the premises, is paid under the products-completed operations aggregate. Either way, FC&S says, the form pays the bodily injury. The takeout bag and the catering tray are the away-from-premises version. The car that carries the order is a different policy. That one is in the note on the Thanksgiving run.

The endorsement that moves the meal eaten at the table is CG 24 07, Products/Completed Operations Hazard Redefined. The copy I can point at is the 1996 edition, CG 24 07 01 96. For bodily injury arising out of your products manufactured, sold, handled, or distributed on the premises in the schedule, or in connection with the operations in the schedule, it replaces the old paragraph. The hazard then includes all bodily injury and property damage that arises out of your products if the injury or damage occurs after you have relinquished possession of those products. You handed over the plate. FC&S said restaurants that serve food and drink on the premises usually attach this endorsement, and that it does not decide whether there is coverage. It decides which aggregate the payment spends, even though the injury happened in the dining room. The IRMI slides say restaurants typically amend the policy with CG 24 07. “Usually” and “typically” are their words, from 2011 and from those slides. I will not assume the endorsement is on yours.

The same form has a sentence owners skip. The products-completed operations hazard does not include injury from products or operations if the classification, on the declarations or on a schedule, states that products-completed operations are subject to the general aggregate limit. If the declarations show the products-completed operations aggregate as excluded, that is the conversation. I will not invent a million-dollar figure and call it the one you have. The number on the page is the number. If you are opening and you do not have a page yet, the liability line is one item on what a new restaurant actually needs. The landlord’s certificate will not wait while we debate allergens.

Medical payments are the no-fault page, and they are smaller than people hope. Coverage C pays medical expenses for bodily injury caused by an accident on premises you own or rent, on the ways next to those premises, or because of your operations. The accident has to be in the coverage territory and during the policy period. The expenses have to be incurred and reported within one year. The injured person has to submit to an examination by physicians the company chooses. The company pays regardless of fault, and not above the applicable limit of insurance. I will not type a limit you have not shown me. The expenses this edition names are first aid at the time of the accident, necessary medical, surgical, x-ray, and dental services, including prosthetic devices, and necessary ambulance, hospital, professional nursing, and funeral services. Coverage C then says it will not pay expenses for bodily injury included within the products-completed operations hazard. If CG 24 07 has put the on-premises meal into that hazard, the no-fault page may not be the page that pays the emergency room. The suit, if the insurance applies, is still Coverage A. If the endorsement is not on the policy, a reaction at the table can still sit outside that hazard, and medical payments can still be in play, up to the limit and subject to the rest of the form. I will read it. I will not sort it at the table.

What the form leaves with you

Three exclusions on this edition matter even when the guest’s injury is covered, and one exclusion is the employee.

Damage to your product is property damage to your product arising out of it or any part of it. The plate you throw out is not the guest’s hospital bill. The exclusion is the plate.

Recall of products, work, or impaired property is the batch. The form removes damages claimed for any loss, cost, or expense incurred by you or others for the loss of use, withdrawal, recall, inspection, repair, replacement, adjustment, removal, or disposal of your product, your work, or impaired property, if it is withdrawn or recalled from the market or from use because of a known or suspected defect, deficiency, inadequacy, or dangerous condition in it. Pulling the special, and paying to throw it out, is this exclusion. The guest’s bodily injury is a different sentence. The IRMI slides say the exclusion is aimed at those recall expenses, and that it presupposes bodily injury or property damage has also taken place. I am using that reading as theirs. The words on the form are the ones I will match to your endorsement.

A health-department fine is not “damages because of bodily injury.” That limit is the insuring agreement itself. I will not call a fine a covered loss because the form did not.

A cook who reacts on the line is not the guest’s claim. The workers’ compensation exclusion removes any obligation under a workers’ compensation, disability benefits, or unemployment compensation law, or any similar law. The employer’s liability exclusion removes bodily injury to an employee arising out of and in the course of employment, or of performing duties related to the business, and it removes the consequential injury to that employee’s spouse, child, parent, brother, or sister. There is an exception for liability assumed under an insured contract. A guest is not that exception. The other policy is the one in the workers’ comp note.

What I want done before Saturday

If you are in the chain rule, put the common name of each allergen below or beside the item, on the writing the guest actually orders from. That includes the menu on the internet, because that writing is in the federal definition California adopted. If you use a QR code, put a paper chart, grid, booklet, or allergen menu in the dining room the same day. Sesame is on the state list. Read the jar. The FDA’s own examples of sesame on a package are sesame seeds in the ingredient list, tahini declared as sesame, and a spice blend that would otherwise hide under the word spices. I am not auditing your bun from Monrovia. The person in charge has to know the foods at that facility. That is section 113947, and it is specific to the preparation you actually do.

If you are one dining room, I would still keep an ingredient list the line can open during service. The CDC asked for lists or recipes. The menu statute did not. The claim does not wait on the menu statute.

Separate the board, the pan, and the oil when you can. The CDC says to use separate equipment and areas when feasible, and to clean and sanitize when a separate setup is not available. The FDA’s retail study contaminated stainless steel, textured plastic, and maple with foods that contained peanut, milk, and egg. A full wash, rinse, sanitize, and air dry removed allergen and limited transfer. Scraping food off first helped. A wet cloth, and an alcohol and quat wipe, did better than a dry wipe. Cloths stored in sanitizer solution transferred less allergen from surface to surface. Textured plastic was harder to clean than stainless or wood. That study was surfaces. It was not a fryer. I will not pretend a wipe answers a vat of oil. If you cannot keep that oil separate from the allergen the guest just named, say so before the nod. A nod you cannot keep is how a failure to warn becomes a fact. Your product, on the form, includes the failure to provide warnings or instructions.

Write the allergy on the ticket, and say it to the person cooking. Have a person in charge on that shift who can answer for the food you actually serve. A new hire has 30 days to get the card. The guest is here tonight. The card is the floor. The pre-shift is the plan the CDC found staff actually knew more under.

If a guest reacts, call 911. Keep the ticket. Call me the same day, and keep the suspect batch long enough that nobody has to reconstruct it from memory. Do not decide on the sidewalk which aggregate applies. Do not assume the policy reimburses the food you throw out. The recall exclusion is the sentence I do not want you meeting for the first time on Monday.

  • Tell me whether you are one dining room, one shop of a brand that has twenty, or a truck. The menu statute sorts those three differently. The training statute is still in the room.
  • Bring the allergen sheet the line uses. If you do not have one, say that. I would rather start there than admire a QR code.
  • Bring the declarations page. I want to see whether products-completed operations is included, excluded, or dumped into the general aggregate, and whether CG 24 07 or a company version of it is on the forms list. The medical-payments limit is on that page too.
  • Tell me who is the person in charge tonight, and whether the cards are current. Section 113947 is a person, not a poster in the office nobody opens.

Lunch is the easier way to do the first pass. Bring the menu you are actually handing people, and the declarations page if you have one. I’ll buy.
