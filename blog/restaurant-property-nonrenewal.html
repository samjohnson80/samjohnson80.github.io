The non-renewal letter is not the last word.

A cancellation, a non-renewal, and a renewal that got expensive are three different pieces of paper. Here is the notice California actually requires, what the Dove Fire bulletin covers, and what I want in the file before the date.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. Written for owners, not for insurance people. I am not your lawyer.

The envelope is usually thin. Sometimes it is an email, and the subject line is the expiration date. Sometimes the renewal comes back and the premium is the thing that stops the conversation. The owner puts it on the counter between lunch and dinner and asks the same question. Is this the end of the policy?

It is a date. On a cancellation, the date sits in the middle of the term. On a non-renewal, the date is the expiration. On a renewal that got expensive, the page may be a new offer, and the policy you have stays in force until you let it go. Those are three different letters. Section 675.5 is where the commercial rules in that chapter begin, and the sections under it do not give the three letters the same number of days. I am going to walk the one you are holding. If the letter and the lease are arguing, a lawyer reads the lease. I read the policy.

The wider fall market, the national survey, and the FAIR Plan limits are the other note. This one is the letter, for a restaurant, bar, or café in the San Gabriel Valley.

Why a restaurant property file draws this letter

I will not hand you a count of dining rooms non-renewed this year. I do not have one. What I can open is why a property underwriter looks at a restaurant differently from the shop next door.

Wildfire is the reason the department has described in its own words. On March 28, 2025, when Commissioner Lara approved higher commercial limits on the FAIR Plan, the Department of Insurance said the plan had grown as traditional insurers retreated from high-risk areas. The commissioner said the higher limits were for business owners, among others, “who are being priced out or left without coverage altogether.” A café on a flat block in Arcadia and a place against the brush above Sierra Madre are not the same placement. I sorted that in the fall note. A letter whose only stated reason is that a wildfire happened in the ZIP, after a declared emergency, is a later section of this note. A letter about the roof, the losses, or a class the carrier stopped writing is a different letter.

Cooking is the loss the fire services have counted. NFPA’s February 2017 report, using 2010 through 2014, estimated that U.S. fire departments responded to an average of 7,410 structure fires a year in eating and drinking establishments. Cooking equipment was involved in 61 percent of them. Electrical distribution and lighting equipment was involved in 9 percent of the fires and 21 percent of the direct property damage. I am not going to pretend a 2017 report is this year’s kitchen. The hood, the tag, and the policy page that can argue with you after a grease fire are the hood note. The newer figure is wider than a dining room. The U.S. Fire Administration, on a page last reviewed September 28, 2026, estimates 33,200 cooking fires in nonresidential buildings in 2024, and $41 million in dollar loss. That is every nonresidential building where cooking was the cause. It is why a fryer is not invisible on the application.

The building itself is the question on the supplement. A restaurant application supplement from Hull & Company asks the age of the building and the year the roof, the plumbing, the air conditioning, and the wiring were upgraded. A restaurant supplemental from Societies Insurance, form SI CH01 APP, dated October 8, 2026, asks the year built, the construction, whether the building is fully sprinklered, whether a UL 300 hood sits over all the cooking, and whether that hood and duct are cleaned professionally at least every six months. Neither form is a promise that your carrier uses those questions. They are the questions that show up when the building is old and the file is thin. “I don’t know” on the panel is a harder file than the same dining room with the invoices.

Rebuild cost is why last year’s limit can look short even when nothing burned. The California Department of General Services publishes the California Construction Cost Index from Engineering News-Record’s building cost indexes for San Francisco and Los Angeles only. The department says the index tracks specified construction labor and materials, and that it does not reflect the current bidding environment. December 2021 on that table is 8,072. December 2025 is 10,258. That is the same December-to-December comparison the department uses for its annual percentage. The later figure is 2,186 points higher, about 27 percent. The one-year changes the page prints are 9.3 percent for 2022, 9.4 percent for 2023, 2.3 percent for 2024, and 3.9 percent for 2025. November 2026 is 10,868. December 2026 is not on the table yet. This is not a bid to rebuild a hood and a dining room on Huntington Drive. It is why I do not leave a 2021 replacement-cost number on a 2026 application.

The date on the letter is a deadline. It is a chance to answer the question the underwriter already asked.

A cancellation in the middle of the term

Section 675.5 is the gate. For policies issued or renewed on or after January 1, 1987, the chapter applies to commercial insurance, and that term includes commercial property, commercial liability, and a commercial package. The same section says commercial insurance does not include the California FAIR Plan, and it does not include surplus line insurance. If the page in your hand is one of those two, the notice rules in this section and the next one are not the rules on that paper. Those policies are further down.

Section 676.2 is the cancellation rule for a policy section 675.5 covers. After the policy has been in effect more than 60 days, or immediately if the policy is a renewal, a cancellation notice is effective only if it complies with section 677.2 and it is based on something that happened after the effective date. The grounds include nonpayment of premium, a court or administrative judgment that the named insured violated a law whose elements include an act that materially increases the risk, fraud or a material misrepresentation in obtaining the policy or in pursuing a claim, a willful or grossly negligent act or a safety-law violation that materially increases the risk, a failure to do loss-control work you agreed to when that failure materially increases the risk, and a change in the activities or the property that materially adds to the risk and is not already on the policy. Two further grounds are a determination by the commissioner about the insurer’s reinsurance or about solvency. I have not sat across from an owner holding one of those commissioner letters. The statute is the list.

Section 677.2 is what the notice has to look like. It is in writing. It goes to the named insured at the mailing address shown on the policy, and to the producer of record, unless that producer is an employee of the insurer. It states the effective date and the reasons for the cancellation. It is given at least 30 days before that date. For nonpayment of premium, or for fraud, it is given no less than 10 days before. If the notice is mailed, the statute uses the time periods and procedures in Code of Civil Procedure section 1013(a). That section says service by mail is complete when the notice is deposited, and that a notice period set by statute is extended five calendar days when the place of mailing and the place of address are both in California. I have not taken your letter to court. If the notice was mailed, I count those five days before I tell you the printed date is the last day of coverage.

Read the mailing address on the declarations before you decide the letter never came. The statute uses the address shown on the policy. A closed box, or the landlord’s old office, is still that address until the policy is endorsed.

The sixty days belongs to the umbrella and the excess

People repeat “60 to 120 days” as if it were printed on every commercial policy in the state. The section they are reaching for is section 678.1. I opened it. Subdivision (a) says the section applies only to commercial insurance that is subject to section 675.5 and to section 676.6.

Section 676.6 applies to commercial umbrella liability, commercial excess liability, and commercial excess property. Excess property, in that section, is property coverage written over one or more underlying property policies, or over a self-insured retention. The building and the contents on a businessowners policy, or on a package, are the policy underneath. They are not the excess policy. On the words I read, the 60-to-120-day rule is the rule for the umbrella and the excess. I will not pretend it is the rule for the property page under them.

For a policy section 678.1 does cover, the notice is in writing, delivered or mailed to the producer of record and to the named insured at the mailing address on the policy. It goes out at least 60 days, and not more than 120 days, before the end of the policy period, and it includes the reasons. The same notice is required when the insurer will renew only by reducing limits, eliminating coverages, increasing deductibles, or increasing by more than 25 percent the rate on which the premium is based. If that notice is late, the policy continues, with no change in its terms or conditions, for 60 days after the insurer gives the notice. A mailed notice again follows section 1013. One situation where a nonrenewal notice is not required: the insurer has already made a written offer, inside that same 60-to-120-day window, to renew under changed terms or at a changed rate. The offer is the paper.

If the property policy underneath is the one leaving, section 676.6 says a cancellation of the excess policy can rest on cancellation or nonrenewal of an underlying policy that is not replaced without a lapse. The layer above can follow the property out the door. The layer itself is the umbrella note. Read both notices.

I looked for a different section that sets a 60-day nonrenewal notice on a primary commercial property policy. I did not find one. The department’s Commercial Insurance Guide, form 700, revised June 14, 2024, says the code has separate sections for different lines, and it points you to the department when a notice is unclear. For the property policy itself, I read the nonrenewal condition on the form that was issued. If that form gives you 30 days, 60, or something else, that sentence is the clock. Bring the form. I will not invent a day count the code does not print.

A steep renewal on that primary policy is often a new offer, not a non-renewal. Section 676.2’s 30-day notice for a rate increase, a limit cut, or a coverage change is a change during the policy period, after the policy has been in effect more than 60 days. It is not the quote that arrives at expiration. The 25 percent figure lives in section 678.1, on the umbrella and the excess. I will not tell you a larger increase on the property page is unlawful because someone quoted that 25 percent at lunch. Put the expiring declarations next to the quote. See what moved: the rate, the limit, the deductible, or a coverage that disappeared. Then we decide whether to take it, change it, or replace it.

The notice has to say why, on the policies the code names

On a cancellation covered by section 677.2, the notice includes the reasons. On a non-renewal covered by section 678.1, the notice includes the reasons. If the sentence is one line and it does not say which fact they used, I ask, in writing, for the specific reason. The brush, the roof, a loss, and a carrier leaving the class are four different next steps. I want to know which one is on your letter.

The privacy section people sometimes cite for a right to demand the reason is section 791.10. I opened the definition it uses. In section 791.02, an insurance transaction is insurance primarily for personal, family, or household needs, rather than business or professional needs. I will not hand a restaurant a personal-lines statute and call it the commercial rule. Ask anyway. The carrier can answer. The sentences I will rely on, for a reason in the notice itself, are 677.2 and 678.1, and only when the policy is one those sections cover.

Ask which fact they used. The brush, the roof, a loss, and a carrier leaving the class are four different files.

Bulletin 2026-11 is fifteen ZIP codes

Starting January 1, 2026, section 675.55 says an insurer shall not cancel or refuse to renew a policy of commercial property insurance for a property in any ZIP code within or adjacent to the fire perimeter, for one year after the declaration of a state of emergency, based solely on the fact that the structure is located in an area where a wildfire has occurred. The prohibition applies to policies of commercial property insurance in effect at the time of the declared emergency. The commissioner publishes the ZIP codes in a bulletin. For this section, a policy of commercial property insurance is a policy subject to section 675.5. The edges, including losses that have nothing to do with the fire, a willful or grossly negligent act that materially increased the risk, and a commercial policy with an annual premium of $25,000 or more where the insured employed at least 25 people on average over the prior 12 months, are already written out in the fall note. A restaurant is not on the habitational list that section pulls back in. The word that still matters is “solely.”

Bulletin 2026-11 is the Dove Fire bulletin. Commissioner Lara issued it on November 10, 2026. It is addressed to admitted and non-admitted insurers writing residential or commercial property insurance in California. It says Governor Newsom proclaimed a state of emergency for Tuolumne County on October 15, 2026, because of the Dove Fire. The department’s press release the same day describes the order as reaching policyholders in Tuolumne, Stanislaus, and Calaveras counties, and it says the residential piece is approximately 50,000 policyholders. That figure is the residential number in the release. It is not a count of restaurants.

The bulletin then prints the ZIP codes: 95222, 95228, 95247, 95251, 95305, 95310, 95321, 95327, 95329, 95346, 95361, 95370, 95372, 95379, and 95383. Every one of them starts with 952 or 953. The hold runs one year from October 15, 2026. Insurers also have to offer to rescind a cancellation or non-renewal issued for wildfire risk on or after that declaration, and to offer to reinstate or renew a policy that was in effect at the declaration, when the property is in one of those ZIP codes. The bulletin says the department may add ZIP codes in a supplemental bulletin if more of them are within or next to the perimeter.

The bulletin does not name the San Gabriel Valley. Monrovia, Pasadena, Arcadia, Alhambra, and Sierra Madre are not in it. If your ZIP is not on that list, and no later bulletin adds it, this moratorium is not your letter. A foothill address can still be a hard property placement. That is the market note. It is not Bulletin 2026-11.

One limit, because the bulletin is addressed to non-admitted insurers and the definition is narrower. Section 675.55 defines the commercial policy it covers as a policy subject to section 675.5, and section 675.5 says commercial insurance does not include surplus line insurance. I will not tell you a surplus-line property policy is inside this hold. If your ZIP is on the list and the stated reason is wildfire, bring me the form. I will read the definition against the policy you actually have.

What I want in the file

I open a restaurant property file 90 to 120 days before expiration. That span is the window section 678.1 prints for the umbrella and the excess policy. I use the same span for the property policy, because the form underneath may give a shorter notice, and because the photos and the loss runs are not a same-week project. This is how I work. It is not a statute that orders you to start on day 120.

  1. The letter, and the declarations. Which policy. The expiration. The mailing address on the policy. Whether the page is a cancellation, a non-renewal, or a renewal quote. The reason, if one is printed. If we wrote the policy, a copy of a statutory notice should also come to the producer of record. If someone else wrote it, send it the day you open it.
  2. Photos from this year. The roof, the electrical panel, the hood and the suppression tank, the sprinkler riser if there is one, the line, and the dining room. A picture from the opening does not answer a question about the building as it sits now.
  3. The hood file. The service tag, the inspection certificate, and the cleaning record. What the fire code asks of that system, and the policy symbol that can argue with you later, is the hood note. I want the paper that shows the work was done.
  4. The years on the building. Roof, wiring, plumbing, heating, with the invoices if you have them. The Hull supplement asks for the year the roof, the plumbing, the air conditioning, and the wiring were upgraded. If the year is a guess, say it is a guess.
  5. The sprinklers, in a sentence. The Societies supplemental asks whether the building is fully protected by automatic sprinklers. A system over the dining room and nothing over a storage mezzanine is a partial answer. Say which floors, and the last inspection if you have it.
  6. Loss runs, currently valued. The same Societies form asks for claims in the past five years, and it says an operating business needs currently valued loss runs or a signed no-loss letter. One of those may be required before coverage can be bound. I ask the carrier that wrote the expiring policy for the runs the week I open the file. They do not always arrive the afternoon I request them.
  7. Replacement cost, as of this year. The building, if you own it. Improvements and betterments, if you paid for a hood or a floor you cannot take with you. Business personal property. Business income. The construction index above is why a limit from several years ago is the wrong starting point. It is not the appraisal. Who insures the buildout is the lease note. I am not redoing that page.

When the admitted carriers decline

Two places are left, and they are not the same policy.

A surplus line policy is a policy from an insurer California has not licensed. Section 1763 says a surplus line broker may place that insurance for a home-state insured only when it cannot be procured from admitted insurers that actually write the particular type of insurance in this state. It is prima facie evidence of the diligent search if the filed form shows that three admitted insurers that actually write the type have declined the risk, or that fewer than three admitted insurers actually write it. The department’s commercial guide says the same thing in plainer words: three written declinations from licensed carriers, then the surplus line market. The statute is the one I follow when the real question is how many admitted carriers still write restaurant property. The guide also says that before you buy, the broker gives you the disclosure in section 1764.1. That notice says the insurer is not licensed by the state, is not subject to the financial solvency regulation that applies to California-licensed insurers, and does not participate in any of the insurance guarantee funds created by California law. Read it before you sign it. Section 675.5 leaves surplus line outside the cancellation and nonrenewal rules above. The notice period on the next one is the sentence in the policy you are buying. I read that page before I tell you how many days you will have next year.

The department’s surplus line insurer page describes two lists. Carriers on the List of Approved Surplus Line Insurers are preapproved under section 1765.2. Eligible carriers under section 1765.1 are carriers the department says it does not preapprove. Ask which list the company is on. I am not going to tell you a surplus line quote is a bargain. I can tell you what the disclosure is giving up, and what the form covers.

The other place is the California FAIR Plan, with a difference-in-conditions policy beside it. The plan is basic property insurance when the normal market will not write the building. It does not run the restaurant. Liability, liquor, the weeks you are closed, and what the difference-in-conditions policy is there to do: that is the FAIR Plan note. If we are heading there, read that note before you decide the building is insured.

The lease keeps its own clock

The coverage the lease requires has to be in force on the days the lease says so. A gap between the old policy and the new one is not a detail a certificate fixes the following week. Who has to be notified, and who insures the hood and the tile, are the lease note. Read the notice sentence in your exhibit before the expiration. If the landlord has to be told, I want that letter sent while the replacement is still a plan.

Pay the expiring policy while we shop. A cancellation for nonpayment, on a policy section 677.2 covers, can be noticed in no less than 10 days. A renewal you have not accepted yet is a different fact from a policy you stopped paying. I would rather remarket a policy that is still in force than explain a lapse to a landlord on Friday.

Bring the letter, the declarations, and the lease page that talks about insurance. If the photos are on your phone, bring those. I’ll buy lunch. Pick the restaurant.
