The FAIR Plan covers the building. It does not run the restaurant.

Fire, lightning, and an internal explosion are the policy. The guest, the liquor, the weeks you are closed, and a warm walk-in are other forms. Here is the commercial contract, what changed through this fall, and what I still place beside it.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. Written for owners, not for insurance people.

The question shows up with the non-renewal, or with a quote that never comes back. The carrier is done with the building. Somebody has said the words FAIR Plan. The owner wants to know whether that means the restaurant is insured.

It means the building can have basic property insurance when the normal market will not write it. It does not mean the Saturday night is insured. This note is the commercial policy itself, for a restaurant, bar, or café in the San Gabriel Valley. The wider fall market, including the one-year wildfire non-renewal rule, is the other note. A café on a flat block in Arcadia and a place against the brush above Sierra Madre can both end up here, and they did not get here the same way.

How a restaurant lands on it

The statute that created the plan has four purposes. Two of them matter at the table. One is to make basic property insurance available. The other is to push people toward the normal market, and the statute names that market: admitted insurers and licensed surplus line brokers. Basic property insurance means direct loss to real or tangible personal property at a fixed location, from the perils on a standard fire policy and an extended-coverage endorsement, plus vandalism and malicious mischief, plus whatever else the plan adds with the commissioner’s approval. It is not a package.

The Department of Insurance told brokers on March 11, 2022 that the normal market includes both of those places, admitted and surplus. The department’s view in that notice is that coverage can be “unavailable” when it is unaffordable. A quote that exists, and that you cannot pay, can still be the path here. The same PDF attaches the plan’s own bulletin from November 23, 2021. That bulletin says a non-renewal by your current carrier does not, by itself, qualify the risk. The broker certifies that a diligent search was made. The plan then tells the broker to search again before every renewal.

The commercial page calls this a temporary solution for a business that cannot buy coverage from a traditional company. The examples it prints are habitational buildings of five or more units, retail shops, manufacturing, offices, buildings under construction, and farms and wineries. The word restaurant is not on that list. A dining room is still a business at a fixed address, and the plan of operation approved in 2026 does not list restaurants as ineligible. What it does list as ineligible includes a building vacant or unoccupied for longer than a year, unless the vacancy is ongoing construction, and a building that has been condemned. A dark dining room that sits empty past a year is a different problem from a carrier that left.

The plan is small on the commercial side, and it grew, and then it flattened. Its commercial-line count by ZIP, figures as of September 30, 2026, shows 20,544 commercial policies statewide. A year earlier the same report shows 20,718, a 1 percent dip. On September 30, 2022 the count was 7,302. The key statistics page, through the same September, shows 698,307 policies of every kind and $773 billion of exposure. Commercial is the small pile inside that. The ZIP report says totals can vary by data source, so I am not going to divide one page by the other and call it a percentage.

In the ZIPs I actually drive, the commercial count is still a short list, and it moved.

A second file, commercial policies under $20 million of exposure, same date, splits the count into habitational, retail, manufacturing, office, agricultural, and other, across low, medium, and high wildfire scores. Habitational, on the plan’s own commercial page, means buildings with five or more units, including hotels and motels. Retail, on that same page, is shops: the examples are a boutique, a salon, a dry cleaner, a convenience store. A restaurant can sit in retail or in “other.” I will not assign it. The useful fact is the smaller one. Most of the commercial growth on my own ZIP is apartments and the like, not cafés.

What the commercial fire policy pays

The contract people mean is the commercial fire policy. The plan’s page says it is a named-peril policy: coverage only for the causes of loss the policy lists. The causes it prints without an extra charge are fire, lightning, and internal explosion. Vandalism and malicious mischief are optional, at an additional cost. There is a separate small-business form. I will come back to it. If the declarations say commercial fire, this is the one.

The sample they link is form CP 00 99 (01 05). It pays for direct physical loss to covered property at the described premises, caused by a covered cause of loss. Fire, lightning, and explosion apply on the form itself. Explosion includes gases or fuel inside a furnace or a flue, and it has its own carve-outs, including a rupture from water. Windstorm or hail, smoke, aircraft or vehicles, riot or civil commotion, sinkhole collapse, and volcanic action apply only if a premium is shown for extended coverage. Vandalism applies only if the declarations say included, and even then the form does not pay for theft, except building damage from the burglar breaking in or out. Sprinkler leakage applies only if the declarations say included. Read the premium column. A sample is not your page.

The plan also links a commercial amendatory endorsement, CFP 300 0725, and the endorsement says it changes the form. A few of the changes are the ones I want a restaurant to see.

  • Walks, patios, and other paved surfaces are property not covered. Outdoor furniture used to maintain the premises is listed with the building. The tables can be property. The slab is not.
  • A structure tied to the building only by a walkway, a wall, a fence, or a utility line has to be scheduled on its own. A detached prep kitchen, a storage container, a walk-in that stands outside: if it is not on the declarations, do not assume it is in the limit.
  • For a tenant, “building” in the vacancy condition means the unit you lease. For the owner, the building is vacant or unoccupied when 70 percent or more of the square footage is. The form still will not pay if that vacancy runs more than 30 days before a vandalism loss, or 60 days before any other covered cause. Buildings under construction or renovation are not treated as vacant. A long close, with the hood cold and the chairs stacked, is a conversation before day 31, not after.
  • The ordinance-or-law exclusion is in the endorsement, and the loss-payment section says the cost to repair, rebuild, or replace does not include the increased cost of complying with an ordinance. A kitchen that has to come back to the current hood and duct rules is the exclusion I do not want smoothed over. The burned wall and the code upgrade are not the same number.
  • Utility services are excluded when the failure of power, communication, water, or another utility starts away from the premises, or starts at the premises on equipment that brings the service in from off site. If a covered cause results, the form pays for that resulting loss. A fire that starts because the power failed can be a fire claim. The salmon that warmed up in the dark is the exclusion. I wrote the admitted-policy version of a shutoff in the walk-in note. On this form, start with the named perils.
  • Water is excluded: flood, surface water, sewer or drain backup, and water under the ground. A pipe that bursts is excluded unless a covered cause broke it. Sprinkler leakage is the exception only when that cause is included. Fourteen days of seepage is out on its own.

Covered property, if a limit is shown, includes the building and your business personal property. Business personal property includes stock, and stock includes merchandise held for sale. Food that a fire destroys can be property, up to the business-personal-property limit, subject to the valuation and the deductible. Food that spoils because the compressor quit, or because Edison shut the circuit off, is not a cause of loss on this form. Money, securities, and the cash in the till are on the property-not-covered list in both the sample and the endorsement.

The sample values the building with depreciation. It defines actual cash value as fair market value. The amendatory I read replaces loss payment and vacancy. It does not replace the valuation section. The how-to-apply page says the plan does not estimate the fair market value of the building, the cost to rebuild it, or the cost of labor and materials. The limit on the declarations is the number you and the broker put there. If a lender, or a lease, wants replacement cost, that is a separate grant. I will not pretend the sample commercial form gives it to you. A 2016 department order required a replacement-cost endorsement on eligible dwellings unless the owner declined it. That sentence is about dwellings. The commercial sample in front of me is the page I am using.

If a coinsurance percentage is on the declarations, and the limit is below that percentage of the value at the time of loss, the sample pays a fraction of the loss. The form walks the arithmetic. I would rather catch it at the quote than in the proof of loss. Tenant improvements, on the sample, are valued at the least of a depreciated repair cost, a share of your original cost based on the days left on the lease, or nothing if someone else pays to rebuild them. A restaurant that does not own the building needs that paragraph read against the lease, not against a hope.

A fire policy can put the wall back. It cannot seat Saturday.

The pages that are not in the form

I looked through the sample and the amendatory for a liability grant, a liquor grant, a business-income grant, and a spoilage grant. They are not there. Additional coverages on the sample are debris removal, preservation of property, a fire-department service charge, and pollutant cleanup. The amendatory rewrites debris removal and will pay up to an additional $25,000 for debris at each location, in one occurrence, when the loss plus debris blows through the limit or through the 25 percent cap. That is debris of covered property. It is not the gross you did not ring.

The plan’s difference-in-conditions page says a DIC policy provides coverages the plan does not, and it names water damage, theft, and liability. It says those policies are designed to combine with a FAIR Plan policy to look more like a comprehensive homeowners policy. The plan does not sell DIC. Its policies page says the same thing about flood and earthquake: consider them for perils the plan does not cover, and the plan does not offer them. The earthquake blurb on that page is the California Earthquake Authority, for individually owned residential property. I am not going to stretch a homeowners DIC into a promise about a dining room. The practical point is the one I can stand on. If the only property quote left is this fire policy, the rest of the restaurant is still uninsured until we place it.

The guest who falls
General liability. It is not in the commercial fire form. A certificate that names the landlord does not add it.
The drink, and the fight
Liquor liability, if you pour. I wrote the license side for a Type 41 or a Type 47. The FAIR Plan fire policy is not that form.
The weeks you are closed
Business income. The commercial fire sample pays for direct physical loss. It does not pay the seatings you missed while the hood was being rebuilt.
The food, when nothing burned
Spoilage, on a policy that actually grants it. Stock damaged by fire can be business personal property here. A warm walk-in is a different file, and the shutoff note is where I walk the admitted forms.
The code upgrade
Ordinance or law. The amendatory leaves the increased cost of complying with the building rules out of the repair figure.
The pipe, the patio slab, the till
Water, paved surfaces, and money. Named on the form as out, unless a covered cause or an included sprinkler-leakage grant says otherwise.

There is a second commercial product, and I do not want it confused with the first. The 2026 plan of operation calls Division II a businessowners policy: a combination of the ISO businessowners property form and the ISO businessowners liability form, for certain small business operations necessary and incidental to the occupancy. The limit in that plan is a combined $20 million for the building, business personal property, and associated coverages at one location, and business liability of $1 million per occurrence and $3 million aggregate. The application the plan posts, revised May 2022, lists business liability among the standard coverages and business income and extra expense among the additional coverages, and it says to see the policy for the limitations. It describes the program as eligible retail, office, service, and processing risks. I will not tell you a restaurant qualifies. The declarations tell you which contract you bought. If the form number is the commercial fire policy, none of that businessowners list is in it.

What actually moved, through this fall

The limit changed, and it has an end date. On March 28, 2025 the department approved Division I commercial limits of $20 million per building and $100 million per location. The 2026 stipulation says the plan began offering the high-value commercial policies on July 26, 2025. The plan of operation attached to that stipulation sets the sunset: the plan shall cease to offer high-value commercial policies after July 26, 2028. No new business and no renewal of that high-value policy may incept after that date. A policy already issued can run for one year from its inception. After the sunset, the commercial cap goes back to $20 million per location. High value, in that plan, means a commercial property policy with a limit over $20 million per location. A single-building restaurant under that number is on the ordinary commercial limit. A campus, or a building whose values were stacked past $20 million at one address, is on the clock.

In early March 2026, the same stipulation says, all but one of the member insurers voted to stop treating those high-value policies as a separate line when the plan calculates how members share an assessment. The commissioner approved that change. It changes the math among insurance companies. It does not add a coverage to your form.

The headline rate increase is a dwelling filing. I opened the department’s closed-filings list through September 30, 2026. The plan has two personal-lines entries. File 25-2174 is dwelling fire, a rate filing, 35.6 percent requested and 29.1 percent approved, closed April 6, 2026. File 25-2455 is dwelling fire and allied lines, approved at zero the same day. There is no commercial rate percentage for the plan on that list. The last commercial fire rate I can find on the department’s 2023 closed list is file 21-3484, commercial fire and allied lines, a 1.5 percent decrease requested and a 1.5 percent decrease approved, closed September 7, 2023. The 2026 plan of operation says a commercial rate application is due within 36 months after an approved commercial rate change, unless the commissioner directs otherwise. I am not going to put 29.1 percent on a restaurant. The list does not print an effective date for the dwelling change, and I will not borrow one from a write-up. Your commercial number is the one on the renewal quote.

The discount people quote has two numbers, and they are not the same document. The March 28, 2025 department release says residential and commercial policyholders can get a discount of up to 20 percent on the wildfire portion of the premium for hardening. The schedule the plan posted, last revised November 2025, is the one I use for a commercial policy with an effective date of November 15, 2025 or later. It lists up to 12 discounts, applied to the wildfire portion. A commercial policyholder who qualifies for all 12 may see up to 13.8 percent off that portion. The sheet says discounts are subject to change, and that “up to” is the plan’s word. Five are the immediate surroundings, five are the structure, one is for completing all ten, and one is the community: a Fire Risk Reduction Community on the Board of Forestry list, a Firewise USA site in good standing, or both for a larger percentage. The criteria are still written with the word dwelling. A strip-center café on Huntington and a building in the foothills are not the same worksheet. A Class-A roof, enclosed eaves, ember-resistant vents, and the five-foot zone can fit a restaurant building. A wood fence at the sidewalk may not. Nothing on the sheet applies itself. The broker has to put it on the quote.

The assessment is real, and it does not land where most owners expect. On February 11, 2025 the commissioner approved the plan’s request to assess its member insurers $1 billion, so the plan could keep paying claims from the January fires. Order 2025-1 is the order. The department’s recoupment FAQ, revised September 11, 2025, says the split of that assessment between personal and commercial lines is about 97 percent to 3 percent. Member insurers may ask to recoup 50 percent of an assessment up to $1 billion for a line, from their own policyholders, as a temporary supplemental fee, over 24 months, after a rule-change filing the commissioner approves. The FAQ says the fee is not premium. It also says FAIR Plan policyholders do not pay that fee. The insurers’ policyholders do. The same answer says the fee can be passed to an insurer’s DIC policyholders, and those people usually have a FAIR Plan policy too. On June 30, 2026 the Los Angeles Superior Court denied Consumer Watchdog’s petition challenging the recoupment bulletins. A denial is the ruling in that case. It is not a finding about the line on your bill. If a fee shows up on an admitted policy or on a DIC, I want the filing.

In January 2026 the plan said it had handled about 5,400 claims from the Eaton and Palisades fires and paid nearly $3.5 billion, and that it had a line of credit, a reinsurance program, and a $750 million catastrophe bond, to make another assessment less likely. That is the plan talking about its own financing. It is not a statement that your premium is frozen.

One more door back to the admitted market, and it is easy to ignore. Insurance Code section 10095(j) required a commercial clearinghouse on or before July 1, 2024, so admitted insurers can offer a commercial policy to someone already on the plan. Admitted insurers have the first option. The program may include nonadmitted insurers after that. The statute also requires a notice of how the plan shares your information for those offers, and a way to opt out. The plan’s own description says participating insurers get policy data monthly, without customer contact information, and that the broker of record is part of moving the policy. An offer from the clearinghouse is a reason to pick up the phone. It is not a reason to stop shopping.

What I do before the renewal

The plan of operation says a Division I or Division II policy is written for one year, and that a non-renewal notice goes out at least thirty days ahead, with the same exceptions the cancellation section uses for nonpayment, incendiarism, and material misrepresentation. Thirty days is a short window for a surplus quote. I start the remarket about ninety days out. That is my practice, not a statute.

  1. Read the notice for the reason and the date. A non-renewal is not an automatic FAIR Plan placement. The plan’s 2021 bulletin says so. If the only reason is the wildfire location, the one-year commercial moratorium is in the market note, and it has edges a restaurant can fall off.
  2. Shop the admitted market and the surplus-lines market before you accept the fire policy as the whole answer. The statute and the department both treat surplus-line brokers as part of the normal market. Call more than one broker. The plan says there is no additional cost for using a broker, and that not every broker is registered to place the plan. The department’s consumer page says the same thing: shop first, then a licensed broker, preferably one registered with the plan. You can also call the plan. I would rather you have a broker who can see both markets.
  3. If the property piece is the FAIR Plan, build the rest in the same sitting. General liability. Liquor, if you pour. Workers’ comp is its own policy either way. Business income for the weeks a fire would close you. A DIC, or whatever the surplus or admitted market will actually write, for the perils this form leaves out, water and theft among them. The plan does not sell that wrap.
  4. On the declarations, I want the form name, the causes that are included, the building limit, the business-personal-property limit, the tenant improvements, the deductible, and any coinsurance percentage. I want every structure scheduled. I want the patio understood as not covered. Bring the insurance section of the lease. A named-peril property policy does not, by itself, perform a list a landlord wrote for a package.
  5. Ask which of the twelve hardening discounts are on the quote, and ask for the November 2025 commercial schedule, not the “up to 20 percent” sentence from the March 2025 release. Document what the building actually has. A discount that is not on the quote is a brochure.
  6. Leave the clearinghouse opt-out alone unless you have a reason to use it. Then shop anyway. The plan’s instruction to its brokers is an annual search before renewal. If the limit at the location is over $20 million, put July 26, 2028 on the calendar. That policy does not renew past the sunset.

If the dec page is already a mystery, text me the photo. That part is free. If it all looks fine, I will say so. If you would rather do it over a plate of food than over a PDF, pick the restaurant. I’ll buy.
