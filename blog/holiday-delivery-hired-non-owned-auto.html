The Thanksgiving run is in somebody’s own car

The order that will not fit on the tablet is already going into a Civic, or into the owner’s own car. A crash on that drive is not a slip in the dining room. Hired and non-owned auto is the page to read before the trays leave.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. Written for owners, not for insurance people. I am not your lawyer.

It shows up the same way every November. A house in Arcadia wants the whole table. An office in Pasadena called at two. The DoorDash screen is a line of red, or the order is a stack of hotel pans and nobody on the app will take it. So a closer puts the food in their own car. Or you do, because the closer already left. Monrovia, Sierra Madre, Alhambra, San Gabriel, the rest of the valley. The drive is ten minutes until it is not.

If that car hits someone, three different people get named. The driver. The owner of the car, who may be the driver. And the restaurant, because the food had your name on the bag. Those are not the same insurance page. I would rather sort them while the book is still a list, not from a parking lot.

The dining-room policy is the wrong page

The liability form on a restaurant package is written for the guest, the floor, and the plate. It is not written for the drive. FC&S, walking through the businessowners liability form BP 00 03 07 13, quotes the aircraft, auto, or watercraft exclusion this way. There is no coverage for bodily injury or property damage arising out of the ownership, maintenance, use, or entrustment to others of any aircraft, auto, or watercraft owned or operated by, or rented or loaned to, any insured. Use includes operation and loading or unloading. The same paragraph says the exclusion applies even if the claim is framed as negligence in the supervision, hiring, employment, training, or monitoring of others, when the occurrence involved that auto.

The general liability form I can open, CG 00 01 04 13, starts that exclusion the same way. A carrier can file its own words. A later edition can move one. The form on your declarations is the one that counts. I am not going to pretend a missing page is a yes.

Read the exception people hope will save them, because it will not save this drive. On the wording FC&S quotes, the exclusion does not apply to parking an auto on, or on the ways next to, premises you own or rent, provided the auto is not owned by or rented or loaned to you or the insured. That is a parking exception, and only for a car that is not yours and was not rented or loaned to you. A Civic loaded with pans, on the way to a house, is operation. Loading the pans is use, on the sentence that says use includes loading or unloading. Calling it a hiring mistake, because you let that closer drive, is the negligent-supervision sentence. The exclusion still applies. It does not move the crash back onto the dining-room form.

What hired and non-owned actually is

The endorsement I can read in public is BP 04 04 01 10, Hired Auto and Non-Owned Auto Liability. It modifies a businessowners coverage form. Insurance is provided only for the coverages that show a specific premium in the declarations or in the schedule. Hired and non-owned are two lines. One of them can be blank.

On that edition, hired auto liability applies to bodily injury or property damage arising out of the maintenance or use of a hired auto by you or your employees in the course of your business. A hired auto is any auto you lease, hire, rent, or borrow. It does not include an auto you lease, hire, rent, or borrow from any of your employees, your partners, your executive officers, or members of their households.

Non-owned auto liability applies to bodily injury or property damage arising out of the use of any non-owned auto in your business by any person. A non-owned auto is any auto you do not own, lease, hire, rent, or borrow that is used in connection with your business. The definition then says this includes autos owned by your employees, your partners, your executive officers, or members of their households, but only while used in your business or your personal affairs.

That is the employee’s car, and it can be the car titled to you if the named insured is the company and you are an officer. It is not the van titled to the restaurant. An auto you own fails the first words of the definition, “you do not own.”

Two cuts, on the same page, matter more than the title.

The other-insurance clause says this insurance is excess over any primary insurance covering the hired auto or the non-owned auto. “Covering” is the word. If the driver’s own policy pays for that use, this endorsement is written to sit above it. If the driver’s policy declines the use, I will not promise you that the excess clause turns into a first-dollar policy. I want the denial in writing. I will not predict the claim from Huntington Drive.

And “who is an insured” is narrower than the restaurant. You are an insured. For a non-owned auto, a partner or executive officer is an insured, and an employee is an insured, but only while that non-owned auto is being used in your business. Then the endorsement lists who is not an insured. A partner or executive officer is not an insured for any auto owned by that partner or officer, or by a member of their household. The owner of a non-owned auto is not an insured, and neither is an agent or employee of that owner. The person who owns the Civic, and is driving the Civic, is the owner of the non-owned auto. On this edition, that person is not an insured. The business can be. The driver may be standing on their own personal policy, or on nothing.

If the declarations name you, the human, and the car is yours, it is not a non-owned auto at all. The definition requires an auto you do not own. Hired does not catch it either. You did not rent your own car from yourself. A sole proprietor who delivers in the car they drive to work is a different question from an LLC whose owner drives a car the LLC does not own. Read the named insured before you read the endorsement title.

The same endorsement replaces most of the liability exclusions with a shorter auto list. Bodily injury to an employee of the insured, arising out of and in the course of employment, or out of duties related to the business, is excluded, and the exclusion applies whether you are liable as an employer or in any other capacity. The closer who is hurt in the crash is not this page. Every employer, except the state, has to secure workers’ compensation, by Labor Code section 3700. How the comp premium moves is the note on the September benchmark. I am not re-teaching it here.

Property damage to property owned or being transported by the insured, or rented or loaned to the insured, is excluded, and so is property in the insured’s care, custody, or control. The other person’s car is the claim this endorsement is built for. The pans in the back are property being transported. The employee’s own bumper is the sentence I want you to read before you assume the endorsement fixes their car. Liberty Mutual, in a note dated March 19, 2020, says the business auto policy is not likely to cover physical damage to the employee’s vehicle. That is their wording. It is not a promise about yours.

The endorsement can insure the restaurant for the other person’s injuries. It does not, on the edition I can read, insure the person who owns the car they are driving.

The personal policy may not want this trip

California lets an auto policy say what use is out. Insurance Code section 11580.1(b)(3) says an automobile liability policy has to designate, by explicit description, the purposes for which coverage for those vehicles is specifically excluded. The code is not a delivery exclusion. It is the reason the exclusions, if they are there, are supposed to be written down. Read them. Do not guess from the insurance card.

The endorsement I can read for the app version is PP 43 33 01 23, Public or Livery Conveyance Exclusion (Including Delivery Network Platform). It adds a definition of a delivery network platform: an online-enabled application or digital network used to connect customers with drivers, or with local vendors using drivers, to provide prearranged delivery services, including courier services, for compensation. It says a delivery network platform does not include a transportation network platform. It replaces the liability exclusion so there is no liability coverage for ownership or operation of a vehicle while it is being used as a public or livery conveyance. It says that includes, but is not limited to, any time a vehicle is being used by an insured who is logged into a transportation network platform or a delivery network platform as a driver to provide delivery services, including courier services, whether or not the food, goods, items, or products to be delivered are in the vehicle. A share-the-expense car pool is not inside the exclusion. Neither is volunteer or charitable use. The same idea is written into the medical-payments part and into the part that pays for damage to the auto.

That endorsement is about being logged in. It is not stapled to every personal policy in the San Gabriel Valley. Your closer, on your clock, not logged into anything, is not the “logged into” sentence. The lead-in is still “public or livery conveyance,” and the form says the logged-in time is included but is not the whole exclusion. I will not finish that argument for a carrier I have not read. Liberty Mutual’s 2020 note draws the line I want the driver to ask about. Some personal insurance excludes coverage for for-hire deliveries, which that note says is different from business travel. Some companies, it says, might deny a claim if the business use was not disclosed or is specifically excluded. Have the driver ask their own insurer, in a way they can keep, whether delivering your food for the restaurant is a use the policy will take.

If the personal policy does answer, look at the limit before you relax. For a policy or bond issued or renewed on or after January 1, 2025, Vehicle Code section 16056(a)(2) sets the floor at $30,000 because of bodily injury to or death of one person in any one accident, $60,000 because of bodily injury to or death of two or more, and $15,000 because of injury to or destruction of property. Section 11580.1(b)(1) says the auto liability policy’s limits are not less than the limits in section 16056. For a policy or bond issued or renewed on or after January 1, 2035, section 16056(d) raises those floors by $20,000, $40,000, and $10,000. The floor is not a serious injury. Liberty Mutual’s note recommends, as its own recommendation, $100,000 per person and $300,000 per occurrence. That is not the statute. It is one carrier telling a restaurant what it wants to see on the driver’s policy. I will not turn either number into a price for your account.

One older number is still printed, and I do not want it misunderstood. Vehicle Code section 17150 makes every owner of a motor vehicle liable for death or injury to person or property from a negligent or wrongful act in its operation, by someone using it with the owner’s permission. Section 17151(a) then caps the liability of an owner imposed by that chapter, when it is “not arising through the relationship of principal and agent or master and servant,” at $15,000 for the death of or injury to one person, $30,000 for more than one person, and $5,000 for property damage. The legislative note on the section I read still shows a 1967 amendment. The cap and the 2025 insurance floor are different statutes. And the cap sentence itself steps aside when the liability comes through the employer relationship. Do not hear $15,000 as the most the restaurant can owe because a closer was on a delivery. I am not deciding that lawsuit. I am telling you not to skip the words “not arising through.”

The tablet is a different driver

A Dasher or an Uber Eats courier is not your closer. Proposition 22 put the app rules in the Business and Professions Code, and the section I am using was added by that initiative, effective December 16, 2020. I have not seen a later amendment on it.

Section 7463 defines a delivery network company as a business that maintains an online-enabled application or platform used to facilitate delivery services in California on an on-demand basis, and that keeps a record of engaged time and engaged miles. On-demand, in that definition, means the courier can accept or decline each request, and the company does not require the courier to accept a specific request in order to keep access to the app. Engaged time, subject to the exceptions in the section, runs from when the app-based driver accepts a delivery request to when they complete it. Time after the customer cancels, and time after the driver abandons the job, is not engaged time. The company may also exclude time when that is reasonably necessary to remedy or prevent fraud.

Section 7455(f)(1) says a delivery network company shall maintain automobile liability insurance of at least $1,000,000 per occurrence, for the benefit of the public, to compensate third parties for injuries or losses proximately caused by an app-based driver operating an automobile during engaged time, in instances where the automobile is not otherwise covered by a policy that complies with section 11580.1(b). Read the condition. The million is tied to engaged time, to an automobile, and to the instances where the car is not otherwise covered by a policy that meets that Insurance Code section. I will not turn it into “the app always pays the first million, for every minute the courier is online, and your restaurant is on the policy.”

The same section requires occupational accident insurance for an app-based driver who is hurt while online: medical expenses up to at least $1,000,000, and disability payments equal to 66 percent of average weekly earnings from all network companies, for up to the first 104 weeks, with the minimum and maximum weekly rates tied to Labor Code section 4453. Accidental death coverage, for spouses, children, or other dependents, is in the next subdivision. “Online” includes the time the driver is on the app and can receive requests, or engaged time. That is the app driver’s injury benefit. It is not workers’ compensation for your W-2 cook, and it is not hired and non-owned auto for your restaurant.

DoorDash’s own page on the auto insurance it maintains does not give California a separate section. California falls in the section headed “All Other US States,” which is the section I am using. There, DoorDash says it provides third-party auto liability insurance for Dashers in an accident during the “Delivery Service” period. That period begins when a Dasher accepts a delivery request and runs until the order is marked delivered, unassigned, or canceled. If the Dasher is online but has not accepted a request, DoorDash says their auto insurance is primary. It says Dashers must also maintain primary auto insurance at the minimum limits required by local law. It says damage to a Dasher’s vehicle is the Dasher’s responsibility. It says coverage is considered when the Dasher is considered liable for damages or injuries to another party during that delivery-service period. The page does not print a dollar limit in that “all other states” section. I will not borrow Boston’s number, or the statute’s number, and paste it onto DoorDash’s California paragraph.

Uber’s U.S. insurance page says the insurance described there refers exclusively to rideshare trips, and it points elsewhere for delivery. On that same page, Uber says it maintains commercial auto insurance on a delivery person’s behalf in case of a covered accident while they are making a delivery on the Uber app. I did not get a California delivery limit off the rideshare page, and I will not pretend the rideshare certificate is the delivery certificate.

The headline I do not want crossed with your catering book is the passenger one. Public Utilities Code section 5433, amended by Senate Bill 371, chapter 314 of the Statutes of 2025, is effective January 1, 2026. For a transportation network company, from the moment a passenger enters the car until the passenger exits, the company shall provide uninsured and underinsured motorist coverage of $60,000 per person and $300,000 per incident. That policy is primary over other uninsured or underinsured motorist coverage, and it is solely the company’s obligation. The $1,000,000 of primary liability for death, personal injury, and property damage, from the moment a ride is accepted until the ride is complete, is still in subdivision (b)(1). Section 5433 is the passenger statute. Section 7455(f)(2) tells a transportation network company to maintain the insurance that article of the Public Utilities Code requires. Section 7455(f)(1), the delivery-network sentence, is a different sentence, and the copy of it I read does not show the 2025 amendment. A story about Uber and Lyft insurance, from last October, is not a story about your server’s Civic, and it is not a repeal of the delivery rule.

I have not read a DoorDash or Uber certificate that names the restaurant as an insured. I will not treat the app as your hired and non-owned endorsement. If your own employee leaves in their own car because the tablet is backed up, the app’s period has not started, because nobody accepted the job on the app. That drive is yours to explain.

If the bag also has a cocktail in it, stop and read the other note. Delivery of the drinks covered by the to-go extension is a licensing problem before it is an auto problem. That one is cocktails to-go. This note is the crash.

The van with your name on it

A catering van the restaurant owns is not a non-owned auto. On BP 04 04 01 10, non-owned means an auto you do not own. Put the van on an auto policy that describes it. Tell the company when you buy it, before the first holiday run, not after. I am not quoting a symbol number I have not read off your declarations. I am quoting the definition.

A van you rent from a rental company, for the weekend the book overflows, can be a hired auto: an auto you lease, hire, rent, or borrow, and not one you are borrowing from an employee, a partner, an executive officer, or their household. Hired auto liability still has to be a line with a premium. The endorsement then excludes property damage to property rented to the insured. The bent fender on the rental van is that exclusion until some other coverage says otherwise. The contract the counter hands you is not my form. Bring both.

The turkey in the back is the transported-property exclusion on the same endorsement, whether the van is hired or the car is the closer’s. Liability for the people in the other car is the question this coverage is for. The food, the chafing dishes, and the sheet pans are a property question. Ask it as its own question. I will not stuff them into the auto liability sentence.

What I want on paper before the trays go out

This is the order, while the cars are still in the lot.

  1. Write down who is allowed to drive for the restaurant this month, and put yourself on the list if you drive. Liberty Mutual’s note says to look at the license, look at the insurance card and see that the liability insurance has not expired, and look at the car. I want the same three things. A card that expired in September is not a card. A car you would not let your own family leave in is not the holiday car. The list is a list. It is not coverage.
  2. Get a driving record on the people who will actually go out. Know which program you are in. The DMV’s Employer Pull Notice page says an employer must enroll a driver who needs a commercial Class A or Class B license, a commercial Class C with a hazardous-materials, passenger, or tank endorsement, a driver of passenger vehicles with a seating capacity of 10 or fewer, including the driver, for compensation by a charter-party passenger carrier, or a driver for a passenger stage corporation with a CPUC certificate or permit. A server in their own car, on an ordinary license, doing your catering run, is not that list. Do not enroll the whole dining room because a webpage said “pull notice,” and do not skip the record because the mandate does not name you. The DMV’s FAQ says the department permits an employer to enroll drivers who are not identified in Vehicle Code section 1808.1, if the driver consents on the authorization form, INF 1101, you keep that form, the information is for your business only, and you delete the driver when the employment ends. A volunteer who drives for you can be enrolled on those terms too. The same DMV page says that effective April 1, 2026, employers who are in the program have to conduct that business electronically, and it points to Title 13, section 350.47, of the California Code of Regulations. The record tells you who you are handing the keys to, in the loose sense of a car you do not own. It does not buy the insurance.
  3. One page, in writing, for the people on the list. Liberty Mutual’s note is the shape I recognize. Drivers follow the vehicle laws. Seat belts. No driving distracted or impaired, and no phone calls or texts, including while stopped at a light. No car that is not in safe condition. A crash gets reported to the driver’s own insurer and to you, and you decide the timeline before the week starts, not in the group text after. The note also says drivers should be told their personal insurance is primary. That is Liberty Mutual’s sentence, and it matches the excess clause on the endorsement only when there is primary insurance covering the car. Say it that way in your page, or do not say it. A written rule is not an endorsement. An endorsement is not a rule your closer has read.
  4. At renewal, ask the auto questions in a way you can keep. Is hired auto liability on the policy, and is a premium shown. Is non-owned auto liability on the policy, and is a premium shown. What limit is typed. Who is an insured, and is the owner of the employee’s car left off, the way BP 04 04 01 10 leaves them off. Does the other-insurance clause say excess. If you are a sole proprietor and the car is yours, where is that car insured, since it is not a non-owned auto on that definition. If the company owns a van, where is the van described, and was physical damage bought for it. Does any form exclude delivery, or food, or the use you are actually about to start for the holiday. If the answer is a shrug, that is not a yes. I will not promise that asking leaves the premium where it is. Coverage depends on the wording of the policy that is actually issued.

If the only driving all year is a bank deposit and a run for ice, say that. It is still a non-owned auto on the days it happens. If nobody who works for you drives, and every order leaves with an app, say that too, and then do not describe the app as your policy. If the Wednesday before Thanksgiving is going to be you, and four hotel pans, and your own back seat, I want to know that before you turn the key.

Lunch is still the easier way to lay the list on a table. Bring the declarations page. Bring the names. I’ll buy.
