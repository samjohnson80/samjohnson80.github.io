When Edison shuts the power off, the walk-in starts a clock

A Public Safety Power Shutoff is Edison turning the power off on purpose. The food, the night you closed, and the policy do not all start at the same minute.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. Written for owners, not for insurance people.

The question usually shows up with the chips. The wind was bad overnight. The walk-in is warm. Does the policy pay for the food, and does it pay for the night we closed?

The wind does not answer that. The endorsements do. This is the version I want a San Gabriel Valley owner to have before the next red flag: Monrovia, Arcadia, Pasadena, Sierra Madre, Duarte, Azusa, Glendora, the foothill side of the valley and the flat side. Edison describes both. The lines that feed a dining room can cross a windy stretch miles away, and a calm patio can still go dark. The October wind is the one I want the numbers from. The season does not end with it.

What Edison is doing, and why your block can be quiet

The National Weather Service describes Santa Ana winds as dry east-to-west winds through the mountain passes of Southern California, most common from September through May. They feel warm because the air compresses as it comes downhill. They can damage property. They raise wildfire risk because they are dry and they move a flame.

A Public Safety Power Shutoff, a PSPS, is the utility’s answer to that risk. Southern California Edison says it is a temporary shutoff to reduce the chance its equipment starts a fire, driven by strong winds, dry vegetation, and low humidity. On October 15, 2026, in the middle of the wind, Energized by Edison put the distinction in one sentence: a PSPS is a preventive safety measure, unlike an outage caused by damaged equipment or a repair. The California Public Utilities Commission calls it a last resort. The utilities, Edison included, may cut power when they reasonably believe there is an imminent and significant risk that strong winds will topple lines or blow vegetation into them. The commission says the authority sits in Public Utilities Code sections 451 and 399.2(a), from a 2012 decision. It also says, plainly, that a shutoff can leave communities and essential facilities in the dark.

The October event is the one I want the numbers from, because the headlines moved while the wind was still up. FOX 11, citing Edison’s figures as of 7 a.m. Friday, October 16, reported 34,946 customers shut off in Los Angeles County and 34,800 in Ventura County, 69,746 together. Malibu and Calabasas were among the communities. The station reported gusts up to 60 mph and a red flag warning through 8 p.m. Saturday, October 17. Later that same morning, CBS Los Angeles reported a higher count from the utility: 52,953 customers without power in Los Angeles County, and 57,120 customers in Ventura County facing a shutoff. I am not averaging those. The count was still moving. If your place was dark, the number that matters is the one on your alert.

The power does not return when the gusts do. Edison says crews inspect the lines from end to end, on foot, from a truck, by helicopter, or with a drone, and repair what they find before they re-energize. Inspections typically take up to eight hours and can take longer when they need daylight. Diane Castro, an SCE spokesperson, told CBS the same thing: once a circuit is cleared, restoration typically takes up to eight hours, with delays for helicopter or foot patrols, for damage, or because some circuits can only be inspected in daylight. Edison’s own page says a PSPS may last over 24 hours, and that a forecast can miss the moment the wind arrives, so there can be little or no notice before the shutoff.

Your patio can be still. Edison wrote this on October 15: the lines that serve you may run for miles through a windier, drier stretch, and if that stretch is unsafe, your power goes off. Underground lines on your street can still be tied to overhead lines farther along the circuit. A neighbor on a different circuit can be cooking while you are not. Customers in high fire risk areas are the ones most likely to see a PSPS, and so is anyone whose circuit only passes through one. When I checked Edison’s PSPS page on October 27, it showed zero customers in a shutoff and zero under consideration, out of SCE’s 5 million customers. That is a quiet afternoon. The Weather Service’s season runs through May.

Write the time on the log while the walk-in is still cold. By Thursday, Saturday is a story.

The food clock is the short one

If Los Angeles County Environmental Health inspects you, start with their power-outage sheet for food facilities, dated 10/2026, written for rolling blackouts and high fire danger. Write down the start time. Check temperatures. An outage of two hours or less is not considered hazardous to food that was being held safely when the outage began. After that, they want you acting, not hoping.

Keep the doors closed. A built-in digital thermometer may not work with the power out, so use a probe, and hold refrigerators at 41°F or below. Bagged ice is fine. Do not put dry ice in a closed walk-in. The county says it can build carbon dioxide to an unsafe level. Do not slide hot food into the cold box. It warms everything else. Group the cold food, and keep raw meat separate. If you move product, the county wants an approved cold-storage facility, and no more than 30 minutes away when the truck itself is not refrigerated.

You are in shape to stay open only if you still have hot running water, food at temperature, hot-holding at 135°F or above, a working hood and make-up air for the gas equipment, utensils you can wash and sanitize, and enough light in the kitchen and the restrooms. If you cannot, close yourself and reopen when you can. If the county finds you open without those, they will direct you to close, and you stay closed until they approve the reopening. Questions, during business hours, go to Environmental Health at (888) 700-9995.

When the power is back, probe the food. The county says to discard potentially hazardous food held above 41°F for more than four hours, or food with an internal temperature above 50°F. If nobody was there to watch the thermometer, discard it. Do not trust the smell. Their line, and it is the right one, is: when in doubt, throw it out. Do not refreeze what thawed. Ice cream that melted is trash. Before you reopen: electricity and gas restored, breakers reset, including the water heater and the gas solenoid, refrigerators at 41°F or below, freezers holding food solid, hot holding at 135°F or above, hot water at least 120°F for washing utensils and warm water at least 100°F for handwashing, ventilation and lighting working. If a boil-water notice or a do-not-drink notice is up, do not reopen.

The California Department of Public Health sheet, revised January 2025, is the statewide one. It says your local environmental health agency enforces the retail food code and may have more to say, so the inspector who walks your kitchen wins an argument with a state table. CDPH’s danger zone is 41°F to 135°F. It wants a calibrated thermometer, a battery clock, and a temperature log of the food and of the time it spent in that zone. Its table is tighter than the county’s four-hour line in a few places. On every row of the cold-food table, food at 50°F or above is a discard, including an outage under two hours. On the row labeled 4+ hours, cold food above 41°F is a discard. Hot food at 134°F or below can still be saved in the first two hours if you reheat it to 165°F and then hold it at 135°F, or if you cool it to 41°F within two hours after the power returns. Once the outage passes two hours, hot food at 134°F or below is a discard. If you cannot show how long the food sat in the danger zone, discard it.

I am not going to mash those two sheets into one rule for a lunch conversation. Follow the department that inspects you. Write the times and the temperatures down either way. You will want that page if you throw food away and later ask anyone to pay for it. If you are not sure which department that is, the city pages name the one I use for each town.

Spoilage is a form. The box has to be checked.

On the ISO causes-of-loss form, the dark walk-in is the loss the utility exclusion is written for. FC&S, in PropertyCasualty360, quotes the utility-services exclusion on the ISO causes-of-loss special form, CP 10 30. The insurer does not pay for loss caused by the failure of power, communication, water, or other utility service supplied to the described premises, however caused, if the failure starts away from the premises, or starts at the premises but involves equipment used to bring that service in from off site. A surge that would not have happened but for the power failure is out too. If the failure results in a covered cause of loss, a fire is the example they use, the policy pays for the damage that fire causes. The burned hood can be a claim. The salmon, sitting there warming up, is the exclusion.

The food needs its own grant. Spoilage coverage, CP 04 40, is the ISO endorsement FC&S describes for perishable stock: property kept under controlled conditions that is susceptible to loss if those conditions change. It applies to stock you own and stock of others in your care, at the described premises. Property on the building, in the open, or in a vehicle is not covered. The causes have to be marked on the schedule. If a cause is not marked, FC&S’s reading of the form is that it is not covered.

Two causes matter in a shutoff week. Breakdown or contamination is a temperature or humidity change from mechanical breakdown or mechanical failure of the refrigerating, cooling, or humidity equipment, only while that equipment is at the premises, plus contamination by the refrigerant. Power outage, in the 06 07 form language FC&S quotes, is a change in temperature or humidity from a complete or partial interruption of electrical power, either on or off the described premises, due to conditions beyond your control. FC&S’s 2020 article describes the 12 20 edition the same way: power outage on or off the premises, from conditions beyond your control. That sentence can reach a shutoff that starts on Edison’s side of the meter. It does not require the lines to be broken. It does require the box to be checked.

Read the rest of the schedule before you price the salmon at menu. The endorsement has its own limit and its own deductible. On the 06 07 form, no other deductible on the policy applies to it. If selling price is marked with an X, finished perishable stock is valued at the selling price as if nothing had happened, less discounts and expenses you otherwise would have had. If that box is empty, do not assume you are being paid the plate price. If breakdown or contamination is the cause you bought, and a refrigeration maintenance agreement is marked, you have to keep it. FC&S says that ending the agreement without telling the insurer suspends the spoilage insurance at that location.

The exclusions are the part I do not want smoothed over. On the 06 07 form, earth movement, governmental action, nuclear hazard, war, and water still apply, and then the form adds its own. Unplugging the refrigerator. Killing the power at a switch. The inability of the utility, or of another power source, to provide enough power because of lack of fuel or because of a governmental order. Your own generator being too small for the demand. Breaking the glass that is part of a refrigeration unit. A later edition can move a word. Read the edition on your policy.

A PSPS is Edison deciding to de-energize. The CPUC describes that as the utility’s own authority, used as a last resort, not as an order that names your restaurant. The governmental-order exclusion is a real sentence. I will not pretend the form says “PSPS,” and I will not pretend the sentence is decorative. This is why I want the page.

Off-premises power still wants something broken

Utility Services — Direct Damage, CP 04 17, is how ISO gives back physical loss from an interruption of water, communication, or power. FC&S is clear about the test. The interruption has to result from direct physical loss or damage, by a covered cause of loss, to the utility property you selected on the schedule. Power supply property means generating plants, switching stations, substations, transformers, and transmission lines. Overhead transmission lines are included only if the schedule says so. If a utility-services limit is shown, it is part of the property limit, not a second limit stacked on top. Electronic data is not covered property under the endorsement.

A planned shutoff does not meet that test. Nothing at the substation has to burn. Edison’s October 15 piece says the shutoff is preventive, unlike an outage from damaged equipment. FC&S looked at a cousin of this in 2021, an intentional load reduction by a utility, and said CP 04 17 did not respond, because there was no direct physical loss at the insured’s premises or at the supplier’s. Robins Kaplan, writing in 2017 about outage claims, points to a federal case in New York in which a utility shut power off ahead of Hurricane Sandy to protect its own system. The court found no coverage where the policy required physical loss at the utility. That case is not a California PSPS case. It is the published reading I will lean on, and it points the same direction.

If the wind actually breaks Edison’s equipment, or breaks yours, you are in a different claim. Read it that way. A preventive shutoff and a broken line are two files. If the wind damages the building, that is a property claim, and the market side of wildfire in California is a separate note.

ISO form names, as a map. A carrier can use its own form. The schedule on the policy that was issued is the one that counts.

The food in the walk-in
Spoilage, CP 04 40, with power outage marked. A temperature change from a power interruption, on or off the premises, beyond your control. Its own limit and its own deductible.
Other property hurt by the outage
Utility Services — Direct Damage, CP 04 17. Direct physical loss, by a covered cause, to the utility property marked on the schedule. Overhead lines only if they are marked.
The sales you missed
Business income, CP 00 30, or Utility Services — Time Element, CP 15 45. CP 00 30 wants direct physical loss at your premises, then 72 hours. CP 15 45 wants physical loss at the utility, then the waiting period written on its schedule.
The compressor itself
Equipment breakdown. A breakdown of the covered equipment. A planned shutoff is a different event.

Lost sales, and the hours you wait

Lost seatings are not spoilage. Spoilage, as FC&S describes CP 04 40, pays for the damaged stock. It does not pay for the income. Income is a business-income form, and then, if you bought it, a utility time-element endorsement on top.

FC&S walks through CP 00 30, the business income and extra expense form. The insurer pays the actual loss of business income you sustain because you had to suspend operations during the period of restoration. The suspension has to be caused by direct physical loss of or damage to property at the described premises, from a covered cause of loss. For business income, that period begins 72 hours after the direct physical loss. Extra expense begins immediately. The 72 hours is a time deductible. The first three days of lost income are yours.

Put a shutoff next to that sentence. The lights go out Saturday at 6. They are back Sunday at noon. The building is fine, the hood is fine, the only casualty is the food and a closed night. That fact pattern does not start the 72-hour clock, because the form is waiting for direct physical loss at the premises. The food, if anything pays for it, is the spoilage claim. The empty dining room needs a different grant.

The grant people mean is Utility Services — Time Element, CP 15 45. FC&S says you attach it only if you already have business income, extra expense, or both, and that it extends that coverage to a suspension caused by an interruption of service. The interruption still has to result from direct physical loss or damage, by a covered cause, to the utility property marked on the schedule: water, wastewater, communication, or power, and overhead transmission lines only if marked. An analysis of the 09 17 edition says a business-income waiting period is entered for each premises. The choices it lists are 0, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, 144, or 168 hours. A blank line is treated as zero. The wait starts when the interruption starts at your premises. Business income on the endorsement begins after the wait. Extra expense begins when the interruption does. The limit is part of the business-income limit, not a second pile of money, and coinsurance does not apply to the endorsement. For rating, that analysis treats 72 hours as the factor of 1.00. A shorter wait takes a higher factor. A longer wait takes a lower one. That is a rating factor, not a price. The number typed on your schedule is the number. A zero and a 72 are different policies that can both say “utility services” on a certificate.

Civil authority is the other place an owner looks, and the form is narrower than the phrase. On CP 00 30, FC&S quotes it this way: a covered cause of loss has to damage property somewhere else, a civil authority has to prohibit access to your premises because of that damage, and your premises have to sit in the prohibited area and within one mile of the damaged property. Business income under that coverage begins 72 hours after the authority first prohibits access, and then for up to four consecutive weeks. A dark dining room is not a deputy at the door. I will not stretch it into a shutoff.

A dead compressor is a different Tuesday

The Hartford, in a page updated August 8, 2025, describes equipment breakdown as coverage for mechanical, electrical, or pressure-system failures: the repair, the replacement, and sometimes the income while you are down. The examples are a generator that fails in a storm, a motor burnout, a surge into an electrical panel, an air conditioner that quits in a food warehouse. They describe the trigger as something inside the equipment. Wear and tear is not covered. Commercial property, in their telling, is the outside event: fire, a storm, a theft.

FC&S, on that 2021 load-reduction question, said the equipment-breakdown wording in front of them required a sudden and accidental breakdown of covered equipment that shows up as physical damage. A planned reduction was not sudden, and it was not accidental, and the loss of electricity was an indirect cause. The 2020 utility article adds the opening I do not want to hide. Under the 2013 ISO equipment-breakdown forms, spoilage and business income can be available when a service interruption results from a breakdown at a public or private utility. A breakdown. A bearing, an arc, a pressure failure. A decision to de-energize, made while the equipment is still intact, is the other file.

If the walk-in compressor dies on a quiet Tuesday, call me about equipment breakdown. If Edison draws a PSPS shape over your circuit, start with spoilage and the utility endorsements, and bring me the pages.

Do not plan on Edison paying for the salmon

Edison’s service guarantee lists the conditions the guarantee does not cover. The first one is a Public Safety Power Shutoff for wildfire mitigation. The 24-hour restoration promise lives inside that guarantee. A PSPS sits outside it.

On the claims page, Edison says it looks at whether the loss was its negligence, how it happened, the extent of the damage, and what the law treats as fair. The same page says that in many instances Edison is not responsible for power outages, voltage fluctuations, food loss, or property damage from forces outside its control, including wind and extreme heat, and that it is not responsible for an interruption that results from a cause not within its control. The full wording they point to is Tariff Rule 14.

If you file with them anyway, the page tells you what paper they want. For food, an itemized list of what spoiled, with receipts or other proof of cost. For a business loss, tax records, bank statements, payroll, revenue and expense statements, and sales receipts. Keep copies of what you send. They also ask you to limit the loss, and the example they give is getting ice so the food does not spoil. They say you can file with your insurer instead, and the insurer can decide whether to pursue Edison for what it paid you, less your deductible. A denial is not the end of a civil claim. Their page says the time limit they recite is three years for property damage and two years for bodily injury. I am not your lawyer. I am telling you what their page says so you do not wait a month to write anything down.

What I do before the next red flag

This is the order, while the walk-in is still cold.

  1. Put the restaurant’s address on Edison’s alerts, and your cell with it. Customers do it in My Account. Anyone, a tenant included, can sign up for alerts on a specific address, by text, email, or a voice call, and pick a language. The PSPS weather dashboard looks out up to seven days. The outage map is the live picture. Edison’s usual pattern is a notice up to three days out, then a tighter list, then a one-to-four-hour warning when they can send one, then a notice when they shut the power off, a notice when inspections start, and a notice when the power is back. Bookmark the map. A forecast can still miss the wind. Check it when a red flag goes up, not after the walk-in alarms.
  2. Know the ice, the coolers, and whether a generator you already own can run the refrigeration. The county’s prep list is a generator with enough capacity for refrigeration and lights, working thermometers, a source for block ice, enough ice chests, and an approved cold box somewhere else. If you run a generator, use Edison’s safety tips. They link them from the PSPS page. During a shutoff, Edison posts community resource centers on the outage map, usually up to a day ahead, and those centers can have ice. The insulated bags they describe are for medications. That ice is a help. It is not a walk-in.
  3. Tape a log to the cooler door. Time the power stopped. Temperature then. Temperature every hour with a probe, because the built-in display may be dead. Time you moved food, or decided to close. Time the power returned, and the temperature then. CDPH asks for the log. The county asks for the start time. A claim asks for both.
  4. If you throw food out, photograph it before it hits the trash, and keep the invoices. Edison’s food-loss list is the itemized food plus receipts. An adjuster on your policy is going to ask for the same kind of paper. Write down, the same day, what you could not sell. The POS knows. Your memory on Thursday does not.
  5. Save the SCE alert and a screenshot of the outage map for your address, with the time visible. A circuit “under consideration” and a circuit that was actually off are different facts. Keep both if both happen.
  6. On the policy, before you need it, I want the answers from the forms, not from the certificate. Is spoilage on the policy, is power outage checked, what is the limit, what is the deductible, is selling price checked. Is utility direct damage on it, and are overhead transmission lines included. Is CP 15 45 on it, and what waiting period is typed on the schedule. Is there equipment breakdown, and does any service-interruption wording require a breakdown. Is business income on the policy at all. If the dec page is a mystery, text me the photo. That part is free. If it all looks fine, I will say so.

Not every restaurant policy is an ISO form. Some carriers file their own. I am walking the ISO wording because it is the wording I can point at in public. The policy that was issued is the one that pays. If you would rather read it over a plate of food than over a PDF, pick the restaurant. I’ll buy.
