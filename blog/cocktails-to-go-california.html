Cocktails to-go run through 2029. They are not permanent.

On September 30, 2026 the state kept the to-go cocktail rules alive through December 31, 2029. For a San Gabriel Valley restaurant that pours, the pickup, the meal, and the seal did not get looser. The liquor policy may still be written for the dining room.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. I am not your licensing lawyer. I place the policy that has to live with the license.

The headline is easy to misread. Cocktails to-go, extended. An owner hears “extended” and hears “settled.” It is not settled. If you pour in the San Gabriel Valley, on a Type 41, a Type 47, or a close cousin, the useful question is narrower. What date is on the statute now, and which rules did not move with it.

What actually got extended

On September 30, 2026, Assembly Bill 2663 (Rogers, Chapter 829, Statutes of 2026) was signed. The Department of Alcoholic Beverage Control’s advisory says the bill extends the sunset on the cocktails-to-go laws from SB 389 (Dodd, Chapter 657, Statutes of 2021) and keeps Business and Professions Code section 23401.5 going until December 31, 2029. The same page says eligible licensees may keep exercising these privileges for three more years. The statute, as amended by that chapter, says the section is operative until December 31, 2029, and as of that date it is repealed. The legislative information site marks the amendment effective September 30, 2026.

That is a later sunset. It is not a permanent privilege. I would not print a 2030 to-go menu on the strength of it. When the date gets close, read the section again. Another bill could move it. I am not going to guess whether it will.

Who the department says can use it

Section 23401.5 applies to the holder of an on-sale license for a bona fide public eating place that has off-sale privileges, and to a licensed beer manufacturer, licensed wine manufacturer, or licensed craft distiller that operates a bona fide public eating place at its premises of production. The advisory names Type 41, Type 47, and Type 75 as examples of the eating-place side. The department’s page on sales of alcoholic beverages to-go also names manufacturer examples: Type 01, Type 02, Type 23, and Type 74, when that eating place is at the premises of production.

You still only sell, under this law, what the license already lets you sell on-sale. The advisory is plain about a Type 41: that license cannot sell distilled spirits under these laws, because it does not provide distilled spirits privileges. On the license-type page, a Type 41 is beer and wine for consumption on or off the premises, and distilled spirits may not be on the premises except brandy, rum, or liqueurs used only for cooking. A Type 47 may sell beer, wine, and distilled spirits on the premises, and beer and wine to go. A Type 75, the brewpub-restaurant, may sell beer, wine, and distilled spirits on a bona fide eating place, plus a limited amount of brewing of beer. If a condition on the license already restricts off-sale, the to-go page says you do not get to exercise a privilege that fights that condition. And the statute says nothing in the section requires you to sell alcohol for off-sale consumption. If you do not want the program, you do not have to run one.

The on-premises lawsuit, the obviously intoxicated minor, and the fight outside are a different conversation. That one is liquor liability for a Type 41 or a Type 47. This note is the drink that leaves.

The rules that did not loosen

Treat this as two cups. They are not the same sale.

The first is distilled spirits in a manufacturer-sealed container, the can or bottle the producer closed. If the license permits those spirits for on-sale consumption, section 23401.5 lets you sell them for off-sale consumption when the guest orders the drink and picks it up. The order may be online, by telephone, or in person. The person who placed the order is the person who picks it up, directly from you, at the premises, and that person presents a bona fide identification card so you can confirm age and identity. The statute says the alcoholic beverage shall not be ordered or picked up for delivery.

The second cup is the one your staff fills. Distilled spirits, a mixed drink, or single-serve wine, not in the manufacturer’s seal. Beer is out of this cup. The statute says you may sell the alcoholic beverages, except beer, when they are not in a manufacturer-prepackaged container, and only if the conditions are met. The advisory says the same thing in shorter words: beer cannot be sold in a nonmanufacturer-sealed container under these laws.

For that second cup, these are the conditions. They are the statute and ABC’s requirements and guidelines, not a house rule I wrote.

  • A bona fide meal. The statute says the meal has to conform to the department’s July 5, 2020 guidance, “What is required to be considered a ‘meal’?” The requirements page points to the same document. That page presumes snacks do not qualify, and it names pretzels, nuts, popcorn, pickles, and chips. It presumes a long list of appetizers do not, including chicken wings, pot stickers, and a cup of soup. It presumes side dishes such as fries and a small salad do not, along with a reheated refrigerated or frozen entree, and dessert. It also says many sandwiches and salads are substantial enough to be a real meal, and that a pre-packaged sandwich or salad generally would not. The page carries the department’s archive disclaimer. The statute still names it. If the to-go program is built on bar snacks, read that page before you call it a meal.
  • No more than four and one-half ounces of distilled spirits in a mixed drink or cocktail sold this way. The requirements page writes that limit as 4.5 ounces.
  • Two of these drinks per bona fide meal. The statute says two such drinks per meal. The requirements page says a maximum of two non-manufacturer-sealed drinks with each meal.
  • Wine only in a single-serve container. The statute defines that as a standard of fill between 187 milliliters and 355 milliliters that is authorized for wine under Section 4.72 of Title 27 of the Code of Federal Regulations. The requirements page states the same range and adds the conversion it is using: between 6.3 and 12 ounces.
  • A container with a secure lid or cap, sealed so the drink cannot be consumed without breaking the seal, and clearly labeled as containing alcohol.
  • Pickup at the premises, by the consumer who ordered it, with that identification. Someone else waiting at the curb is not the pickup the statute describes.
  • The open-container warning, posted so the buyer can see it, on the premises, online, or wherever it takes for that person to get the notice. The statute prints the sentence, including the reference to Vehicle Code section 23225. The requirements page says a non-manufacturer-sealed container is an open container if it is transported in a motor vehicle. Use the department’s wording.
  • Notice to ABC before you sell this cup. The statute says you notify the department in writing first. The Alcohol To-Go Notification page says use the department’s notification tool, that the notice is once and not an annual renewal, and that eligible licensees may begin after the notice is submitted and the warning sign is posted. The requirements page says a licensee selling only manufacturer-prepackaged beer, wine, or distilled spirits does not have to file that notice. If you are not sure which cup you are selling, ask your ABC district office before the first order.

One more restriction on the cup you fill, from the requirements page. If the guest opens that sealed to-go container before leaving, you are responsible for seeing that it does not leave. A drink partly consumed at the table cannot be packaged to go. The glass from dinner is not a to-go cocktail on the way to the car.

Delivery needs its own sentence, separate from the beer-and-wine habit you may already have. Section 23401.5 says a drink sold under that section shall not be ordered or picked up for delivery. The 2026 advisory says delivery of the alcoholic beverages authorized by these laws is expressly prohibited, whether by the licensee or by any third-party delivery service. The same page draws the line I do not want blurred. Existing law generally still allows an on-sale retailer to sell or deliver manufacturer-sealed beer and wine for consumption off the premises, and the advisory says AB 2663 and SB 389 do not change those privileges. What this law does not let you deliver is manufacturer-sealed distilled spirits, or distilled spirits or single-serve wine that you sealed yourself. A margarita in a delivery bag is not a creative reading of pickup.

What I want off the liquor form

Liquor liability is usually written for on-premises service. The guest, the bar, the sidewalk out front. A sealed container walking to a car is an off-sale style sale. That can sit outside what the carrier underwrote. I am not telling you your policy excludes it. I am not telling you it covers it. The declarations page will not answer this by itself. Read the liquor form. Ask, in a way you can keep, whether off-sale sales or sealed-container sales were contemplated when the account was written. If the answer is a shrug, that is not a yes. I will not promise a price, and I will not promise that asking leaves the premium where it is. Coverage depends on the wording of the policy that was actually issued.

The sunset moved. The seal, the pickup, and the liquor form did not become optional.

A delivery claim is a different mess. The ABC rules expressly prohibit delivery of the drinks this extension covers, by you or by a third-party service. If a cocktail leaves with a driver anyway, the claim starts with a sale the statute tells you not to make. Do not assume a liquor policy written for the dining room was also written for a delivery operation. Bring the license, the to-go menu if you have one, and the liquor forms. If you do not sell a single drink past the door, say that too. Then this extension is a calendar note, not a coverage argument.

One neighboring date, so the two clocks do not get stapled together. ABC’s summary of 2025 chaptered legislation says AB 592 (Gabriel, Chapter 469, Statutes of 2025) delayed the expiration of COVID-19 temporary catering authorizations from July 1, 2026 to January 1, 2029, and says the department may not issue any new ones on or after January 1, 2027. That is the outdoor catering permission. It is not section 23401.5, and it is not permission to deliver a cocktail.

Lunch is still the easier way to sort your own license from the headline. Bring the forms. I’ll buy.
