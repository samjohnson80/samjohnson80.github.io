The lease is not the endorsement.

The insurance paragraph in a commercial lease asks for an additional insured, a waiver, primary and noncontributory, a dollar limit, and somebody to insure the buildout. Those live on different pages. Here is how I read the paragraph before a San Gabriel Valley restaurant signs, or before Friday’s certificate goes out.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. Written for owners, not for insurance people. I am not your lawyer.

The PDF usually arrives from the landlord, and the deadline is Friday. Additional insured. Waiver of subrogation. Primary and noncontributory. A million, or two. Someone has to insure the hood and the tile. A certificate goes out. The Friday feels handled.

I have sat with that paragraph in Monrovia, Pasadena, Arcadia, and Alhambra. A dining room that pours. A café that spent the construction money on a bar it cannot take when it leaves. The sentence in the lease is the landlord’s ask. The endorsement is the page that answers. They are not the same piece of paper.

The short version, for an opening, is the note on what a new restaurant needs. The layer above the primary limit is the umbrella note. This one is the paragraph itself.

The paragraph has five asks

I cannot hand you a California statute that sets a general liability limit for a café. When a number exists, it is in the lease. I am going to show you the shape from a lease I can open in public. It is not a dining room. Do not borrow the dollars for Huntington Drive.

The lease is between CenterPoint Properties Trust and Romeo Systems, Inc., dated December 7, 2016, for the building at 4380 Ayers Avenue in Vernon. It is an exhibit in an SEC filing. The permitted use is battery assembly, warehouse, and office. I am using it because the insurance section is written out. A restaurant lease can put the same five asks on a shorter page.

On that lease, the tenant’s policies have to include the landlord, the landlord’s mortgagee, and other parties the landlord designates, as additional insureds, for ongoing and completed operations. The policies have to be primary and noncontributory. They have to provide a waiver of subrogation in favor of the landlord. The tenant has to furnish a certificate and a copy of the endorsements, and the lease names the waiver and the additional-insured endorsements in particular. The tenant also agrees to give the landlord thirty days’ notice of cancellation or nonrenewal, and ten days for nonpayment of premium. Read that last sentence on your own exhibit. On this one, the notice duty is the tenant’s.

The landlord, on the same lease, insures the building, including building fixtures, at full replacement cost. The tenant insures the tenant’s property, and the lease says that property includes alterations, at full replacement cost, with an agreed-amount endorsement that waives coinsurance. Business income has to cover continuing expenses, including rent, and extra expense, for at least one year. Those are two different property policies. The rest of this note is how the forms I can read answer, or fail to answer, each of those sentences.

The certificate can list the landlord. The endorsement is the page that adds them.

Additional insured is a schedule

The form I opened is CG 20 11 12 19, Additional Insured – Managers or Lessors of Premises, on a copy posted by Aon. A later edition can move a sentence. A carrier can file its own words. The form number on your declarations is the one that counts.

Section II of the general liability coverage part is amended to include the person or organization in the schedule as an additional insured, only for bodily injury, property damage, or personal and advertising injury caused, in whole or in part, by you or by someone acting on your behalf, in connection with the ownership, maintenance, or use of that part of the premises leased to you and shown in the schedule. The copy I opened had the schedule filled in as a blanket: all premises you lease, and any manager or lessor you agreed in a written contract to add, if that contract was executed before the loss. A different copy names one landlord and one address. If the schedule is blank, or it still says the old suite, I want that fixed before the certificate goes out.

Two exclusions sit on the same page. The insurance does not apply to an occurrence after you cease to be a tenant in that premises. It does not apply to structural alterations, new construction, or demolition performed by or for the person in the schedule. The buildout your contractor is doing for you is a different question from construction the landlord is doing for itself. Read which one the lease is talking about before you promise the landlord that Friday’s endorsement covers the crew on the sidewalk.

Two limiters follow, on the copy I read. The insurance for that additional insured applies only to the extent permitted by law. If a contract requires the coverage, it will not be broader than the contract requires. Section B then caps what the company will pay for the additional insured at the amount the contract requires, or the available limits, whichever is less. The endorsement does not increase the limits. A lease that asks for two million, stapled to a policy that carries one million, does not become two million because the landlord’s name was typed on this form.

The Vernon lease asks for additional-insured status for ongoing and completed operations, and it asks for a copy of that endorsement, not only a certificate. CG 20 11 is the lessor form I can read. If the quote uses a different number, I want the number. I will read that page before I tell you the landlord is on the policy.

Primary and noncontributory is a second page

Naming the landlord does not, by itself, say whose policy pays first. The endorsement I opened for that sentence is CG 20 01 04 13, Primary and Noncontributory – Other Insurance Condition. It modifies the commercial general liability coverage part and the products/completed operations liability coverage part. It adds a paragraph to the other-insurance condition and says that paragraph supersedes anything to the contrary.

The paragraph says this insurance is primary to, and will not seek contribution from, any other insurance available to an additional insured under your policy, provided two things. The additional insured is a named insured under that other insurance. And you have agreed in writing, in a contract, that this insurance would be primary and would not seek contribution from any other insurance available to the additional insured.

Both conditions are on the page. A landlord who has no policy of its own is not a named insured under “such other insurance,” which is the first condition. A lease that never says “primary” and never says the tenant’s policy will not seek contribution has not satisfied the second condition, no matter what someone typed in the description box of a certificate. The endorsement does not contain a sentence that adds an additional insured. That job is the previous page. This page only changes who pays first, and whether your insurer goes looking for the landlord’s insurer to share the loss, when those two conditions are true.

A waiver is two different recoveries

Owners hear “waiver of subrogation” as one box. On the forms I opened, the building and the lawsuit are different boxes.

Property first. CP 00 90 07 88, the commercial property conditions form posted by Nationwide Excess and Surplus, transfers your rights of recovery to the company after it pays, to the extent of the payment. You have to help it, and you must not impair those rights after the loss. Then it gives the permission a lease is trying to use. You may waive your rights against another party in writing prior to a loss to your covered property or covered income. After a loss, you may waive only as to someone already insured by the policy, a business you own or that owns you, or your tenant. The condition says this will not restrict your insurance.

That is the kitchen fire that burns the landlord’s roof and your hood. If the lease waived recovery in writing before the fire, the property form I read allows that waiver. Each insurer pays its own insured and stops, to the extent the waiver and the covered loss line up. If the waiver is signed after the loss, this edition allows it against your tenant, and against the short list above. It does not, on the words I read, invite a brand-new release of a stranger written the morning after.

The Vernon lease spends a section on that idea, and it is about property. Landlord and tenant agree to have the property policies they are required to carry either endorsed so the insurer waives subrogation against the other and the mortgagee, or written so the policy gives permission to waive recovery rights in writing before a loss. They agree to look only to their own carriers for a loss for which property insurance is carried, or is required to be carried. Each side waives claims against the other, and against the other’s agents, partners, and employees, for loss to property insured under the policies the lease requires. Deductibles on the tenant’s required policies are the tenant’s, and the lease says those deductibles count as insurance for this waiver. The section survives the end of the lease.

Liability is the other box. CG 24 04 05 09, Waiver of Transfer of Rights of Recovery Against Others to Us, is an endorsement to the general liability coverage part and the products/completed operations liability coverage part. The company waives any right of recovery it may have against the person or organization in the schedule, because of payments it makes for injury or damage arising out of your ongoing operations, or your work done under a contract with that person and included in the products-completed operations hazard. The waiver applies only to the scheduled person. It does not add the landlord as an insured. It does not pay for the roof. A property waiver that was never put in the lease is not cured by stapling CG 24 04 to the liability policy.

The case I will use is a restaurant company’s lease, and it is a liability claim, not a grease fire. In Fireman’s Fund Insurance Company v. Sizzler USA Real Property, Inc., the California Court of Appeal, Second District, decided December 18, 2008, that a waiver in the leases stopped the landlord’s insurer. Santa Monica Collection owned a shopping center at Fairfax Avenue and Santa Monica Boulevard in West Hollywood. Sizzler USA Real Property was the tenant, under two leases, and in August 1993 it subleased to a nightclub called Sky Sushi. On September 13, 2002, a patron, Heroico Aguiluz, was stabbed in the parking area. The landlord’s insurer, Fireman’s Fund, defended and settled, then sued the tenant to get the money back.

The leases required liability insurance with a minimum combined single limit of $1 million, and they required the landlord to be named as an additional insured. The opinion says the tenant’s policy with Federal provided $750,000 after a self-insured retention of $250,000, and that the landlord had not been named as an additional insured. Fireman’s Fund paid $300,000 to settle and $84,101 in defense costs. The court still enforced the waiver. Paragraph 20 released claims for damage to person or property caused by risks insured against under any insurance policies the parties carried, and it required each policy to waive the insurer’s right of recovery by subrogation. The court said that sentence followed the insurance the parties actually carried, including the landlord’s own policy. It was not a condition that failed because the tenant bought less than the leases described. The indemnity paragraph, the court said, was the allocation for liabilities the insurance did not cover. The two clauses did different jobs.

One caution, because I do not want the holding stretched into a dining room in Sierra Madre. The opinion says the failure to name the landlord was largely technical on these facts, because Federal’s policy did treat lessors as insureds. And the waiver was written for risks insured under any policy the parties carried. A lease that ties the waiver to the tenant actually buying the limit the lease describes can be a different paragraph. I have not read yours. The stabbing itself, and what a general liability or liquor form does with a fight, is the New Year’s Eve note. I am not retrying that coverage here.

A waiver in the lease is a sentence. The property form has to allow it before the loss.

The limit is the number the landlord typed

Back to Vernon, and only as that lease. Commercial general liability at a combined single limit of $1,000,000 per occurrence and a $2,000,000 annual general aggregate. The coverage description includes premises, products and completed operations, fire legal liability, host liquor, and contractual liability for insured contracts. Automobile liability, when motor vehicles are used in connection with the lease, at $1,000,000 combined single limit, including owned, non-owned, and hired autos. Umbrella liability, excess and follow-form over the general liability, the automobile liability, and the employer’s liability, on an occurrence form, at $5,000,000, with a self-insured retention no greater than $10,000. Workers’ compensation at statutory amounts, employer’s liability at $1,000,000, and a waiver of subrogation on that policy in favor of the landlord and the property manager. Property at the full replacement cost of the tenant’s property, including alterations. Business income for at least one year of continuing expenses, including rent, and extra expense.

If the tenant is in the business of manufacturing, selling, distributing, serving, or furnishing alcoholic beverages, the same section requires liquor liability and dram shop insurance in the amounts the landlord requires, and if the landlord names no amount, in amounts no less than the minimums required by applicable law. I am not going to invent a statutory liquor-liability minimum. The permitted use of this particular building was not a bar. The license, and when a California restaurant can still be sued over a pour, is the Type 41 and Type 47 note. Host liquor inside a general liability description, and a liquor policy for a place that pours, are two sentences. Read which one your exhibit uses.

The five million, and what “follow form” does and does not do, is the umbrella note. I am not repeating it. The point here is smaller. The number in the exhibit is the number the landlord wanted to see. It is not a price I can promise, and it is not a cap I will imply if your lease does not say so. A café in Duarte may be asked for one million and nothing above it. A bar that becomes a bar after ten may be asked for more. Bring the exhibit.

The Vernon lease also asks that defense expense sit in addition to the limit of liability. I want that sentence on the policy that is issued, if the lease you signed asks for it. A certificate that repeats the lease does not add the defense outside the limit.

Who insures the hood and the tile

The property form I opened is a sample of CP 00 10 10 90, the building and personal property coverage form. The copy is marked sample. IRMI’s October 25, 2024 commentary quotes the same use-interest definition from CP 00 10 10 12. Your declarations may show a later edition, or a businessowners form I have not opened. Bring that form. I will read the one you bought.

Your business personal property, on the sample, includes your use interest as tenant in improvements and betterments. The form defines those as fixtures, alterations, installations, or additions made a part of the building or structure you occupy but do not own, which you acquired or made at your expense but cannot legally remove. The test I care about is the last one, because it is the lease. If you can legally take the piece when you leave, it is not this definition. Furniture, fixtures, machinery, and equipment are already listed as business personal property on their own lines. A range you can roll out can sit there. A hood, a tile floor, or a bar that the lease says stays with the building, and that you paid for, is the use interest.

The building definition on the same form includes completed additions, and permanently installed fixtures, machinery, and equipment. IRMI’s point, which matches that definition, is that work you added can also fit the building the landlord insured. Two policies can see one hood. The tenant’s valuation clause then sorts the double claim. If others pay for the repair or replacement, the tenant’s form pays nothing for the improvements.

If you repair promptly, the sample values tenant’s improvements and betterments at actual cash value. If you do not repair promptly, it pays a proportion of your original cost: original cost, times the days from the loss to the end of the lease, divided by the days from the installation to the end of the lease. If the lease contains a renewal option, the end of the option period replaces the end of the current term. The optional replacement-cost coverage on the same sample says replacement cost, without deduction for depreciation, replaces actual cash value in the valuation condition. IRMI says turning that option on is how the prompt-repair number for improvements moves off actual cash value. The Vernon lease asked for full replacement cost of alterations, and for an agreed-amount endorsement waiving coinsurance. Actual cash value on an unendorsed valuation clause is a different number from the number that lease describes.

And the landlord, on that lease, does not pick up what you built. If the landlord chooses to rebuild after a covered fire, it restores the project to substantially the condition it was in before the fire, up to the insurance proceeds, with reasonable diligence. The lease says the landlord is not required to repair or replace any alterations or improvements the tenant made, or any of the tenant’s property. Rent abates only if the premises are unusable and the fire was not caused by the act or neglect of the tenant or the tenant’s agents or employees. If the landlord decides to demolish or not rebuild, it can terminate, with notice given within forty-five days after the casualty. If the landlord estimates the work will take more than one hundred eighty days, the tenant gets a window to terminate. The building coming back is a choice on the page I read. It is not a promise that your tile comes back inside the landlord’s check.

California Civil Code section 1933 is the older sentence underneath a lot of these clauses. The hiring of a thing terminates at the end of the term, by mutual consent, if the hirer gets a better title, or by the destruction of the thing hired. The Vernon lease does not leave a destroyed building as a one-line ending. It spends an article on who may terminate and who rebuilds. I am not your lawyer. If you are signing, both pages belong in the conversation. If you are already open, the insurance question I can answer is which policy is supposed to pay for the part you cannot take with you.

What I want on the table

This is the order, whether you are signing next week or the certificate is due Friday.

  1. The insurance exhibit, the whole page. Who must be an additional insured. Whether it says primary and noncontributory. Whether the waiver runs both ways, and whether it is property, liability, or both. The limits. Who insures the building, and who insures alterations. How much notice, and who has to send it. Whether the landlord can raise the numbers later.
  2. The additional-insured endorsement, not the certificate. The form number. The schedule: the premises, and the landlord’s name, or the blanket wording if that is what was issued. On CG 20 11 12 19, the grant is injury or damage caused in whole or in part by you, in connection with the part of the premises leased to you. It stops when you cease to be a tenant. It does not raise the limit.
  3. The primary and noncontributory endorsement. On CG 20 01 04 13, the written contract has to say the insurance is primary and will not seek contribution, and the additional insured has to be a named insured on the other insurance. The certificate box does not do that work.
  4. The waiver, on the right line. For the building, a written waiver made before the loss, and a property form that allows it. CP 00 90 07 88 is the permission I read. For the lawsuit, a liability waiver scheduled to the landlord, if the lease asks. CG 24 04 05 09 is that page, and it is not the roof. If the lease also asks for a waiver on the workers’ compensation policy, that is a third endorsement. The benchmark is the comp note.
  5. The buildout, in dollars and in the lease. What you paid for, and what you cannot legally remove. Whether the policy values a prompt repair at actual cash value or at replacement cost. Whether the landlord’s rebuild excludes your alterations, the way Vernon’s does. If both policies can see the same hood, I want the lease’s sentence about who pays, because the tenant’s form pays nothing for improvements when someone else pays to put them back.
  6. The way the room actually runs. Do you pour. Does anyone drive, including you. Is the business-income limit a year of rent and expenses, or a number that was left over from the old tenant. The opening note is the longer list. This note is whether the exhibit and the forms are talking about the same restaurant.

If the endorsements already match the exhibit, and the buildout is on the property limit at the valuation the lease asked for, I will say that. Asking the questions can move the premium. I will not promise that it stays put. Coverage depends on the wording of the policy that is actually issued.

Lunch is the easier table. Bring the exhibit and the endorsements, not only the certificate. Tell me what you built, and whether you can take it when you leave. I’ll buy.
