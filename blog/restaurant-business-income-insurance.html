The closed sign is not the claim.

A dark dining room is not, by itself, a business-income claim. The form pays the income you would have had, and only after a covered physical loss. December is expensive when December is the month on your books.

By Sam Johnson.

The question shows up the week the parties are already on the book. The kitchen is down, the sign is on the door, and you want to know whether the nights you cannot seat are on the policy. It is the same question in January, when the repair bill is the one on the counter and the lost December is the one nobody wrote down.

They are a different line from the repair. I already walked the hood, the compressor, the night Edison turns the power off, the health-department tape, and the shaking. This note is the income. The form I opened is CP 00 30 10 12, Business Income (and Extra Expense). A later edition can move a word. A carrier can type a different number of hours. The page on your declarations is the page.

What the form actually pays

Business income, on this edition, means the net income, net profit or loss before income taxes, that would have been earned or incurred, and continuing normal operating expenses incurred, including payroll. The insurer pays the actual loss of that business income you sustain because you had to suspend operations during the period of restoration. The suspension has to be caused by direct physical loss of or damage to property at premises described in the declarations, the ones with a business income limit shown. The loss has to be caused by or result from a covered cause of loss.

Suspension means the slowdown or cessation of your business activities. A dining room at half, because the line is down and you are running the window, can still be a suspension. It does not have to be a padlock.

If you occupy only part of the building, the form says your premises are the portion you rent, lease, or occupy, and any area in the building or at the described premises that services that portion, or is used to gain access to it. The shared electrical room is a sentence to read against the lease. I am not guessing which closet is yours.

The closed sign is not the claim. The claim is the physical loss, and then the income you would have had.

Extra expense is on this form only if the declarations show that business income applies at that premises. It means necessary expenses you incur during the period of restoration that you would not have incurred if there had been no direct physical loss from a covered cause. The form pays extra expense, other than the expense to repair or replace property, to avoid or minimize the suspension and continue operations, at your place or at a replacement or temporary location, including relocation and the cost to equip and operate that location. It also pays extra expense to minimize the suspension if you cannot continue. And it pays extra expense to repair or replace property, but only to the extent that expense reduces the amount of loss that otherwise would have been payable under this form.

Those payments do not stack a second limit on top. The most the form pays for loss in any one occurrence is the business income limit on the declarations. Payments for extra expense, for civil authority, for extended business income, and for alterations and new buildings do not increase that limit. A rented commissary and the lost seatings spend the same number. I want both invoices. I will not call the second one a separate pile.

The coinsurance condition, further down, does not apply to extra expense. The first hours of lost income can still be yours. The cost that lets you open the window can still be extra expense from the start, if it fits the definition and the physical loss is covered.

The period of restoration, and the 72 hours

Period of restoration, on this edition, begins 72 hours after the time of the direct physical loss for business income, and immediately for extra expense. It ends on the earlier of two dates. The date when the property at the described premises should be repaired, rebuilt, or replaced with reasonable speed and similar quality. Or the date when business is resumed at a new permanent location.

The expiration date of the policy will not cut that period short. A loss on December 28 does not die because the policy renews on January 1.

The period does not include any increased time required because of the enforcement of or compliance with an ordinance or law that regulates the construction, use, or repair of property, or that requires anyone to test for, monitor, clean up, or otherwise respond to pollutants. FC&S, on September 19, 2014, describes CP 15 31 10 12, Ordinance or Law – Increased Period of Restoration, as the endorsement that puts the added building-code time back into the period. The same description says it does not extend the period for a law about pollutants, or about fungus, wet or dry rot, or bacteria. I have not opened that endorsement. I am not adding the weeks a plan check takes unless the form on your policy does.

Set the clock on a covered loss at 6 p.m. on December 22. Business income, on this edition, begins 72 hours later, at 6 p.m. on Christmas Day. Christmas Eve sits in the wait. That is the form’s arithmetic. It is not a claim I handled, and it is not a promise that your declarations still say 72. FC&S describes CP 15 56 06 07, Business Income Changes – Beginning of the Period of Restoration, as an endorsement that can delete the 72-hour wait, so coverage is triggered immediately, or replace it with 24 hours. The paragraph I read ties that choice to the wait, including the wait after civil authority prohibits access. I want the copy on your policy before I tell you which clock moved.

I walked the same 72 hours, for a fire, in the note on the hood. A compressor is not this form’s 72 hours. The equipment-breakdown note has its own period of restoration, and I will not paste this one onto that one. A businessowners policy is a third page. I walked that version, including a 12-month outside date, in the note on the brick and the till.

If you can resume operations, in whole or in part, by using damaged or undamaged property at the premises or elsewhere, the form reduces the business-income loss to that extent. If you do not resume, or you do not resume as quickly as possible, it pays based on the length of time it would have taken to resume as quickly as possible. There is a duty, in the same form, to resume all or part of your operations as quickly as possible if you intend to continue the business. The window you can open is not a favor. It is part of the measurement.

Why a December closure is the one to measure

The form does not pay a national average, and it does not pay a flat month. The amount of business income loss is determined from the net income of the business before the direct physical loss, the likely net income if no physical loss had occurred, the operating expenses including payroll necessary to resume with the same quality of service that existed just before the loss, and other relevant sources. The form names your financial records and accounting procedures, bills, invoices and other vouchers, and deeds, liens, or contracts. It does not include net income that would likely have been earned because the covered cause itself increased the volume of business.

The check is the December you would have had. It is not a December someone invents, and it is not a slow February substituted in its place.

I opened the Census Bureau’s advance release for December 2025, release CB26-26, February 10, 2026. For food services and drinking places, the table is in millions of dollars. Not adjusted for season, it prints 99,628 for December 2025, 96,259 for November 2025, and 100,802 for October 2025. Adjusted for seasonal variation and for holiday and trading-day differences, but not for price changes, December is 100,229 and November is 100,374. The change table prints December against November as −0.1 percent, and December 2025 against December 2024 as 4.7 percent. The release’s own sentence on that year-over-year figure is 4.7 percent, plus or minus 1.8 percent. October, on the not-adjusted line, is higher than December. I am not calling December the biggest month in the country. The adjusted line is built to take the holiday out. Your Saturday is not.

The Census time-series page I opened, release date November 17, 2026, says an advance estimate is superseded by revised estimates from the larger monthly survey. The figures above are the advance release. I am not pretending they are the last word on 2025, and I am not turning a national table into a café on Huntington Drive.

What I will use the calendar for is narrower. The nights do not move. A buyout that was on the book for the 23rd is not a buyout in February. ezCater’s release of November 12, 2026, on its 2026 Workplace Holiday Party Report, says holiday-party budgets are projected to rise 13 percent on average this season, and 96 percent of planners expect to match or exceed last year’s spending. The survey was online in July 2026: 1,000 full-time employees and 605 workplace decision-makers involved in planning the party, nationwide. The same release says the platform brings restaurant partners new high-value customers and large orders. That is a catering season. It is not a census of the San Gabriel Valley, and it is not your gross. If the parties are on your book, they belong in the month the form will measure. If they are not, do not let a national press release talk you into a December your POS does not have.

The month on the form is the month on your books. A national table will not seat Saturday.

Payroll is a continuing expense, until a schedule says otherwise

On CP 00 30 10 12, payroll sits inside continuing normal operating expenses. It is part of business income. It is part of the coinsurance sum, unless an endorsement takes it out. Owners still say ordinary payroll. FC&S says the 2012 revision changed the name of CP 15 10 from Ordinary Payroll Limitation or Exclusion, because the endorsement is no longer limited to non-managerial employees, and the defined term ordinary payroll expense was removed. The endorsement applies to the job classifications and employees the schedule selects. That can be the line. It can be an officer, if the schedule says so.

Two choices, on that description. A limitation: business income includes that payroll only for the number of days shown. The days need not be consecutive, and they have to fall inside the period of restoration, or the extension of it. An exclusion: if the schedule indicates the payroll expense exclusion, that payroll is out of business income. FC&S says the point is payroll you would not keep paying through a long shutdown, hourly work you could replace, does not have to be insured for the whole period, and taking it out lowers the amount of insurance the coinsurance condition demands. The form itself says that, in determining operating expenses for coinsurance, you deduct all payroll expenses or the amount of payroll expense excluded, if CP 15 10 is attached.

The trade is the one I want said out loud before December. The people you limit are the people the form will not keep paying past the days on the schedule. A cook you cannot replace in the first week of January is not a savings. A Saturday crew you would lay off on day three might be. I will not pick that list from a blog. I want the schedule, and I want the names on it.

The businessowners edition I walked in the break-in note still uses the words ordinary payroll, and it limits that payroll to 60 days unless the declarations show more, with officers, executives, department managers, and employees under contract left off that limit. That is a different form. I am not pasting 60 days onto CP 00 30.

The sixty days after you reopen

Extended business income is already in CP 00 30 10 12. If the suspension produced a business-income loss payable under the policy, the form pays the actual loss of business income you incur during a further period. For business income other than rental value, that period begins on the date the property, except finished stock, is actually repaired, rebuilt, or replaced and operations are resumed. It ends on the earlier of the date you could restore operations, with reasonable speed, to the level that would have generated the business income that would have existed if no direct physical loss had occurred, or 60 consecutive days after the date you resumed.

Extended business income does not apply to loss from unfavorable business conditions caused by the impact of the covered cause of loss in the area where the premises are. The loss still has to be caused by direct physical loss or damage at the described premises from a covered cause.

Read that against December. The parties you missed while the kitchen was dark are inside the period of restoration, after the 72 hours, and only while you are still suspended. The 60 days start when you are open again. Reopen on January 3, and those 60 days are January and February. They do not reach back to Christmas Eve. They are the regulars who are slow to return. They stop at 60 days even if the regulars are slower than that. They do not pay a neighborhood that is quiet because of the loss itself.

Extended Period of Indemnity is an optional coverage in the same form, not a second name for the ordinance endorsement. Under the extended-business-income paragraph, the number 60 is replaced by the number shown in the declarations for this optional coverage. If the declarations show 180, the 60 is 180. If they show nothing, I am reading 60. I will not lengthen the ramp because someone said restaurants take six months to come back.

Civil authority, and the mile

Civil authority is an additional coverage, and it is narrower than the phrase. A covered cause of loss has to cause damage to property other than property at the described premises. The form then pays the actual loss of business income you sustain, and necessary extra expense, caused by action of civil authority that prohibits access to the described premises, if both of the following apply. Access to the area immediately surrounding the damaged property is prohibited by civil authority as a result of the damage, and the described premises are within that area but are not more than one mile from the damaged property. And the action is taken in response to dangerous physical conditions resulting from the damage, or from continuation of the covered cause that caused the damage, or the action is taken to enable a civil authority to have unimpeded access to the damaged property.

Business income under that coverage begins 72 hours after the time of the first action that prohibits access, and applies for a period of up to four consecutive weeks from the date on which that coverage began. Extra expense begins immediately after that first action, and ends on the later of four consecutive weeks after the date of the action, or when the business-income part of civil authority ends. The dollars spend the business-income limit. They do not raise it.

FC&S describes CP 15 32 06 07 as the endorsement that replaces the four-week civil-authority period with the number of days on its schedule, and that can also change the one-mile radius. The numbers on the endorsement are the numbers. A health order, with no damaged property inside a mile, is not this paragraph. A dark room because the utility turned a circuit off is not a deputy at the door. I said that in the note on Edison’s shutoffs, and I am not stretching it here.

Someone else’s building, and the power

Dependent properties are not in CP 00 30. A December 2025 analysis of the time-element dependent-property forms says the standard business income forms, without an endorsement, only cover a shutdown from a direct loss at the insured’s location. I opened CP 15 08 06 07, Business Income From Dependent Properties – Broad Form. It pays the actual loss of business income from a necessary suspension during the period of restoration, when the suspension is caused by direct physical loss or damage, from a covered cause, to dependent property at a premises described in the schedule.

Dependent property, on that edition, is property operated by others whom you depend on in one of four ways. To deliver materials or services to you, or to others for your account. That is a contributing location. To accept your products or services. A recipient location. To manufacture products for delivery to your customers under a contract of sale. A manufacturing location. To attract customers to your business. A leader location. The same definition says a contributing location does not include, for those services, water supply, power supply, or communication supply, including internet access or access to any electronic network. The utility is a different endorsement. Do not schedule the substation as a supplier and call it done.

On the copy I opened, the period of restoration for the dependent property begins 72 hours after the direct physical loss at that premises, and ends on the date the property there should be repaired, rebuilt, or replaced with reasonable speed and similar quality. The policy expiration does not cut it short. Ordinance or law time is left out, the same shape as on your own form. FC&S reads the period as the time it should take the dependent property to repair and resume. It does not add the further time it takes you to get back to normal after that property is back. The limit and the other business-income provisions apply separately to each premises on the schedule.

There is a small door for a dependent location that is not on the schedule. The 06 07 form calls it miscellaneous locations, and it will not pay more than .03 percent of the business income limit for each day’s suspension from any one location. A road, bridge, tunnel, waterway, airfield, pipeline, or similar structure is not a miscellaneous location. Three-hundredths of one percent of a $200,000 limit is $60 a day. I picked $200,000 so the percentage has a size. It is not your limit, and $60 does not replace a commissary.

FC&S describes the 10 12 editions, CP 15 08 and CP 15 09, as a broad form and a limited form. The broad form uses the same limit, coinsurance percentage, and coverage options as CP 00 30 or as CP 00 32, the business-income form without extra expense. The limited form is the one it describes for when you do not have direct business income at your own premises, or when the dependent limits differ from each other or from the direct limit. It says the 2012 revision added secondary contributing and secondary recipient locations, and that neither endorsement may be used with the agreed value option. I opened the 06 07 broad form. I have not opened the 10 12 pages. If your schedule is the later edition, I want that page before I import a word.

The December 2025 analysis uses a small breakfast and lunch spot next to a large office building as the picture of a leader location. That is the shape. The office park that fills a Tuesday lunch is a leader location only if it is scheduled, and only for a covered physical loss there. A slow December because the offices are on holiday is not damage. I will not call a calendar a dependent property.

Off-premises power is CP 15 45 10 12, Utility Services – Time Element. I opened that edition. It extends business income, extra expense, or both, to a suspension at the described premises caused by an interruption in utility service to that premises. The interruption has to result from direct physical loss or damage, by the covered cause indicated for that coverage, to the utility property marked with an X on the schedule. Water supply property means pumping stations and water mains. Wastewater removal property is a system for removing wastewater and sewage, other than one designed primarily for draining storm water, and the endorsement does not apply to an interruption from a discharge of water or sewage due to heavy rainfall or flooding. Communication supply property is property supplying telephone, radio, microwave, or television services, such as transmission lines including fiber, coaxial cables, and microwave radio relays except satellites. Power supply property means utility generating plants, switching stations, substations, transformers, and transmission lines supplying electricity, steam, or gas. Overhead transmission lines are not included unless the schedule marks them. The form says transmission lines include all lines that transmit communication or power, including lines that may be called distribution lines.

Coinsurance does not apply to that endorsement. The utility-services limit on the schedule is the only limit for this coverage, and it is part of, not in addition to, the business-income limit on the declarations. An interruption that only corrupts electronic data is excepted. The 10 12 copy I opened does not give the endorsement its own menu of waiting periods. FC&S, on August 10, 2018, describes the 09 17 edition as adding one. The choices it lists are no waiting period, or 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, 144, or 168 hours. The wait begins when service is interrupted at the described premises. On the wording FC&S quotes, business income begins after that wait, extra expense begins at the interruption, and the period-of-restoration definition in the coverage form does not apply to the endorsement. I want the edition on your policy before I tell you which clock is running.

A shutoff with nothing broken at the utility is the Edison note. Read it before you assume CP 15 45 answers a public-safety shutoff. Equipment breakdown has its own utility interruption, with the waiting period typed on those declarations. I am not mixing the two.

The worksheet, the coinsurance, and the monthly cap

If a coinsurance percentage is shown in the declarations, the form will not pay the full amount of any business-income loss if the limit is less than two numbers multiplied. The coinsurance percentage. Times the sum of the net income, net profit or loss before income taxes, and operating expenses, including payroll, that would have been earned or incurred, had no loss occurred, by your operations at the described premises for the 12 months following the inception, or the last previous anniversary date, of the policy, whichever is later.

The steps are printed on the form. Multiply that 12-month sum by the coinsurance percentage. Divide the limit by the figure from the first step. Multiply the total amount of the loss by the figure from the second step. The form pays that result, or the limit, whichever is less. For the rest, you rely on other insurance or you absorb it.

The form’s own example of being short uses net income and operating expenses of $400,000, a coinsurance percentage of 50 percent, a limit of $150,000, and a loss of $80,000. The first step is $200,000. The limit divided by $200,000 is .75. The payment is $60,000. The remaining $20,000 is not covered. The same example with a $200,000 limit pays the $80,000, and the form says no penalty applies. These are the form’s figures. They are not a restaurant in Monrovia, and they are not a price.

In figuring operating expenses for that test, the form deducts a list, if those expenses apply. The lines I care about in a kitchen are the cost of merchandise sold, including transportation charges, the cost of other supplies consumed, and, if CP 15 10 is attached, the payroll the endorsement takes out. Food you would not have kept buying while you were closed is not a continuing expense, and it should not be sitting in the coinsurance number either. Annual sales are not the worksheet. Net income before income taxes, plus the expenses that continue, minus the deductions the form names, is the shape of it.

The Business Income Report/Work Sheet is CP 15 15. FC&S says the 10 12 worksheet is how the insurable values are developed, and that it becomes part of the business-income policy provisions. The agreed-value option inside CP 00 30 tells you what has to be on the sheet before that option is activated. Financial data for your operations during the 12 months prior to the date of the worksheet, and estimated for the 12 months immediately following the inception of the optional coverage. The declarations have to show that agreed value applies, and an agreed value has to be shown. The agreed value should be at least the coinsurance percentage times the net income and operating expenses for the following 12 months that you report on the worksheet. FC&S says that when agreed value, or the premium-adjustment endorsement it discusses, applies, the worksheet has to be signed, certifying a true and correct report of values.

While agreed value is in force, coinsurance is suspended until 12 months after the effective date of the optional coverage, or the expiration date of the policy, whichever occurs first. The form reinstates coinsurance if you do not submit a new worksheet and agreed value within 12 months of that effective date, or when you request a change in the business-income limit. If the limit is less than the agreed value, the form will not pay more of any loss than the loss multiplied by the limit divided by the agreed value. The form’s example: a limit of $100,000, an agreed value of $200,000, a loss of $80,000, a payment of $40,000. The remaining $40,000 is not covered. Again, the form’s figures, not yours.

That is why the monthly records matter before the party season, not after the loss. The estimate is the December you expect, written down while you can still see last December. A flat one-twelfth of a year will understate the month the parties land and overstate a quiet one. The coinsurance test is still the whole year. Both numbers have to be honest. I want twelve months. I do not want a busy Saturday multiplied by 365.

The form has two other optional coverages that turn coinsurance off at the described premises, if the declarations show them.

Maximum period of indemnity. Coinsurance does not apply. The most the form will pay for the total of business income loss and extra expense is the lesser of the loss sustained and expenses incurred during the 120 days immediately following the beginning of the period of restoration, or the limit. A December fire that keeps you closed through spring can outrun 120 days. The days past that line are yours.

Monthly limit of indemnity. Coinsurance does not apply. The most the form will pay for loss of business income in each period of 30 consecutive days after the beginning of the period of restoration is the limit multiplied by the fraction shown in the declarations for this optional coverage. The form’s example uses a limit of $120,000 and a fraction of 1/4, which is $30,000 in each 30 days. In that example the actual loss is $40,000 in the first 30 days, $20,000 in the next, and $30,000 in the third. The payment is $30,000, then $20,000, then $30,000. The remaining $10,000 is not covered. The quiet second month does not lend its unused room to the heavy first month.

If your heavy 30 days are the stretch from the week of Christmas through New Year’s, the fraction has to be large enough for that stretch. A limit that looks comfortable across a year can still cut the only month that was going to carry the year. That is the sense in which a December closure costs the most. Not because the Census table said December was the biggest month. It did not. Because the cap does not move, and the form measures the month you actually lost.

Actual loss sustained is already the coverage grant. The form pays the actual loss of business income you sustain, and then the limit, the coinsurance math, the 120 days, or the monthly fraction can pay less than that loss. I did not find, on CP 00 30 10 12, an optional coverage that deletes the dollar limit and pays actual loss with no cap. If the declarations say actual loss sustained, or a number of months, in place of a dollar limit and a coinsurance percentage, that is a different entry. I want the page. On the businessowners form in the break-in note, business income is an additional coverage, it is not subject to the property limits, and it pays during the period of restoration and within 12 consecutive months after the date of the direct physical loss. That is a different sentence. I will not paste it onto this form.

What a closure is not

No direct physical loss from a covered cause, no business income, on this form. The California Supreme Court said so for the pandemic, and I am not going to smooth the holdings.

Another Planet Entertainment, LLC v. Vigilant Insurance Co., decided May 23, 2024, answered a question the Ninth Circuit certified. The court held that allegations of the actual or potential presence of the COVID-19 virus on an insured’s premises do not, without more, establish direct physical loss or damage to property within the meaning of a commercial property insurance policy. Under California law, direct physical loss or damage requires a distinct, demonstrable, physical alteration to property. The alteration need not be visible to the naked eye, and it need not be structural, but it has to result in some injury to or impairment of the property as property. The court also said it was not deciding that the virus can never cause direct physical loss or damage. It was deciding the allegations in front of it. A health order and an empty room were not enough, on those allegations. Another Planet’s policy included business income and extra expense. The trigger the court was reading is the same kind of trigger this form uses.

John’s Grill, Inc. v. The Hartford Financial Services Group, Inc., decided August 8, 2024, is a restaurant. The court says John’s Grill and John Konstin operate a restaurant in San Francisco. Sentinel denied the claim in part because the loss did not fall within a Limited Fungi, Bacteria or Virus Coverage endorsement. The court said the terms of that endorsement are clear. It provides virus-related coverage only if the virus results from certain specified causes of loss. The opinion quotes the endorsement: the coverage applies only when the fungus, wet or dry rot, bacteria, or virus is the result of one or more listed causes, and the first of those is a specified cause of loss other than fire or lightning. John’s Grill admitted it could not satisfy that limitation. The court enforced the words and reversed the Court of Appeal, which had treated the limitation as unenforceable. This is not the ISO virus exclusion. It is the endorsement on that policy. The shape I am keeping is that a virus endorsement with a specified-cause gate was enforced against a restaurant that could not pass the gate.

The ISO exclusion I opened is a specimen of CP 01 40 07 06, Exclusion of Loss Due to Virus or Bacteria. It says the exclusion applies to all coverage under the commercial property coverage part, including forms that cover business income, extra expense, or action of civil authority. The insurer will not pay for loss or damage caused by or resulting from any virus, bacterium, or other microorganism that induces or is capable of inducing physical distress, illness, or disease. The exclusion does not apply to fungus, wet rot, or dry rot, which the form sends to a separate exclusion. If CP 01 40 is on your forms list, the virus sentence does not wait for a specified cause the way the John’s Grill endorsement did. If it is not on the list, I will not pretend it is. Another Planet can still be the trigger problem, with or without the exclusion. Read the forms list.

A norovirus closure is a different file. I wrote it in the note on the sign on the door. The businessowners virus-or-bacteria exclusion, and the food-contamination endorsement that can turn a health order into its own small coverage, are in that note. FC&S describes a commercial-property cousin, CP 15 05 10 12, introduced in the 2012 revisions. If the business is ordered closed by the Board of Health or another governmental authority because of the discovery or suspicion of food contamination, that description includes cleaning, replacing the food, medical tests or vaccinations for employees, and the loss of business income from the suspension. I have not opened a dollar limit for that edition, so I will not invent one. Bring the endorsement if you bought it. Do not assume CP 00 30 pays for the tape on the door.

The computer sentence is already written, and it is easy to mix up with the disease exclusion. On CP 00 30, business income does not apply when the suspension is caused by destruction or corruption of electronic data, or any loss or damage to electronic data, except the additional coverage called Interruption of Computer Operations. The most that additional coverage pays, unless a higher limit is shown in the declarations, is $2,500 for all loss sustained and expense incurred in any one policy year, regardless of the number of interruptions or the number of systems. When the special causes-of-loss form applies, that additional coverage is limited to specified causes of loss and collapse, and the covered causes include a virus, harmful code, or similar instruction introduced into a computer system to damage it or disrupt it. That computer-code sentence is not a disease. CP 01 40 is the disease. There is no coverage, under the computer additional coverage, for an interruption related to manipulation of the system by an employee or by someone you retained to work on it. The cyber note is where I walked what the $2,500 does not do. I am not teaching a hack again.

Earthquake is not a covered cause on the special-form causes of loss I opened for the shake note. No covered cause, no business income, for the weeks the wall is cracked and nothing burned. A fire that follows is a different sentence, and I said so there.

Water, power, and communication are not a contributing location on the form I opened. Power is CP 15 45, and overhead lines only if the schedule marks them. Nothing broken at the utility is the shutoff note, not this one.
  8. The forms list, for the virus endorsement and the causes of loss. CP 01 40, if it is there, reaches business income, extra expense, and civil authority. Another Planet is the trigger even before that exclusion. John’s Grill is a different endorsement, enforced. A covered cause is still required. Shaking is the earthquake note. A taped door for a suspected foodborne illness is the norovirus note, unless a food-contamination endorsement is actually attached.
