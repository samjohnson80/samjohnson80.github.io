The employee claim is not on the general liability policy

Employment practices liability is the policy for a firing, a harassment claim, or discrimination. The missed break and the short paycheck usually stay with you. Here is what changed for a San Gabriel Valley restaurant, and what to read on the form.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. Written for owners, not for insurance people.

The letter usually comes out after we order. A former server. A cook who says the schedule was punishment. A guest who says a bartender crossed a line. Sometimes it is three years of meal breaks, with a page count. The owner wants to know which policy answers it. I would rather read the letter than guess.

A guest who slips is general liability. A burn on the line is workers’ comp, which is the other note. This one is the claim about how someone was hired, paid, scheduled, spoken to, or let go. The initials on the form are EPLI. Employment practices liability. If the dec page has no page with those words, that is the first thing I want to see.

What the policy is for

The International Risk Management Institute describes it as liability insurance for wrongful acts that come out of the employment process. The claims it says show up most often are wrongful termination, discrimination, sexual harassment, and retaliation. The same entry lists other workplace conduct the policies also pick up: employment-related defamation, invasion of privacy, failure to promote, deprivation of a career opportunity, and negligent evaluation. Directors, officers, managers, and employees are insureds. You can buy it by itself. IRMI says it is also often tucked into a management-liability package, next to directors-and-officers and fiduciary coverage.

Two clocks matter, and they are different from the slip-and-fall policy. IRMI says these forms are claims-made, and that they use shrinking limits. Claims-made means the policy that responds is the one in force when the claim is made. Shrinking limits means the dollars the insurer spends on defense come out of the limit. On a general liability policy, IRMI says, defense sits on top of the limit. Here, a long fight can spend the limit before anyone writes a settlement check.

IRMI’s definition of duty to pay is the other fork. A duty-to-defend form means the insurer takes the claim, picks the lawyer, and pays the bills. A duty-to-pay form, also called non-duty to defend, means you defend it and the insurer reimburses you. I want to know which sentence is yours before a letter arrives on a Friday.

I can talk about the lawsuit over how someone was treated. The paycheck you still owe is a different piece of paper.

What I do not want you to think it pays

Wage-and-hour is the gap I see owners miss. IRMI splits those claims in two. A misclassification claim is the salaried manager who was not actually exempt, so overtime never got paid. A pay-practices claim is the rest of it: a short check, off-the-clock closing, time spent changing into a uniform, a missed meal or rest break, wages paid late. IRMI says insurers universally exclude indemnity for both. Indemnity is the back wages, the settlement, the judgment. A handful of insurers will pay to defend the claim, IRMI says, under a sublimit typically of $100,000 or $250,000.

IRMI’s separate entry on the wage-and-hour endorsement says the same thing from the other side. The endorsement, when you can get it, covers defense costs. Coverage for the settlement or the judgment typically does not apply. A sublimit usually applies, and the example IRMI gives there runs from $100,000 to $500,000. Those two entries do not name the same cap. I am not going to average them. The number that matters is the one printed on your form, and it is a description from IRMI, not a price I can offer you.

A guest is the other gap. IRMI’s definition of third-party employment practices liability is a claim by a nonemployee, typically a customer, a client, or a vendor, against your people. It says that coverage is most often a separate insuring agreement, with a limit of its own, apart from the limit for claims by employees. A substantial minority of insurers add it by endorsement, and then the limit sits inside the employee-claim limit. A dining room, a bar, a café counter: the exposure IRMI describes is contact with the public. I look for that agreement, or that endorsement, before I tell you a guest’s complaint is covered. The same entry says a general liability policy does not cover a harassment or discrimination claim by a nonemployee, because general liability excludes those two causes of action. The slip-and-fall policy does not become the backup.

The common exclusions IRMI names on the employment-practices form itself are bodily injury, property damage, and intentional or dishonest acts. A cut, a burn, a fall on the shift is the workers’ comp conversation. Read the intentional-acts exclusion on your form before you assume a particular firing is still a covered wrongful act. The wording decides it. I will not decide it from the title.

Then the retention. The declarations will say retention or deductible. Those are not the same word. IRMI’s general explanation is the one I use at the table. Under a deductible, the insurer pays the loss and collects the deductible back from you. Under a self-insured retention, you pay until the loss reaches the insurer’s layer. Defense is its own question on the form. Ask two sentences, and make me answer them off the page: do defense costs count against that retention, and do they count against the limit. The shrinking-limits point above is the second one, on a typical employment-practices form. The first one is whatever your form says. I will not guess a dollar figure. There is not one number that belongs on every restaurant.

The wage that is about to move, and the wages that already did

The reason I am writing this in October is the payroll, not a new insurance form. A wage you pay wrong becomes the claim the employment-practices policy usually will not indemnify. The Labor Commissioner’s minimum-wage page says the California minimum, effective January 1, 2026, is $16.90 an hour for all employers, with a higher rate for covered fast food and for certain health-care workers, and with higher city and county rates where those exist. On August 13, 2026, the Department of Industrial Relations said the statewide rate becomes $17.40 an hour on January 1, 2027. The department said the Department of Finance certified that increase on July 31, 2026. The same release does the salary math for the overtime exemption: $17.40 times 2 times 40 hours times 52 weeks is $72,384. Beginning January 1, 2027, a person has to earn at least that much, and still meet the duties test, before you can treat the job as exempt. A kitchen manager under that salary is not exempt because the business card says manager.

Local rates sit on top of the state rate, and the department’s release says you pay the higher one when it applies. I opened the July 1, 2026 table on the UC Berkeley Labor Center inventory, which is the list the Labor Commissioner points employers to. Pasadena is $18.57. Unincorporated Los Angeles County is $18.47. The City of Los Angeles is $18.42. Monrovia, Arcadia, Duarte, Azusa, Glendora, Sierra Madre, and San Gabriel are not on that table. For an hour worked inside those city limits, I start with the state rate. If a later local ordinance shows up, the ordinance wins. If a shift is worked in Pasadena, in unincorporated county, or inside the City of Los Angeles, the local number is the floor for those hours.

The figures below are the rates on the pages I linked, for an hour worked in that place. Pasadena and the county are already above $17.40, so the January state increase does not lift those two floors. A Monrovia, Arcadia, or San Gabriel hour does move, from $16.90 to $17.40, unless a higher local rate applies to that hour. On a phone, the rate sits beside the place. Swipe the table for the last column.

Covered fast food is narrower than “we are quick.” The FAQ says the restaurant has to meet all three tests. It is a limited-service restaurant, meaning the guest orders and pays before eating, with limited or no table service. It is part of a chain of at least 60 establishments nationwide. It is primarily engaged in selling food and beverages for immediate consumption, which the FAQ treats as more than half of gross revenue. A franchisee is included. A restaurant that produces and sells bread as a stand-alone item of at least half a pound after cooling, and has done so since September 15, 2023, is one of the exemptions on that page. So is a restaurant inside a grocery establishment of more than 15,000 square feet, when the grocery employer is the one employing the people. A sit-down dining room in Arcadia is not in this bucket. A national counter concept in Pasadena may be, and then the floor is $20, not $18.57. The FAQ also says a salaried manager at a covered fast-food restaurant who is paid less than $83,200 does not meet the salary half of the exemption. That figure is the one the FAQ ties to the $20 wage. Duties still have to match.

There is no tip credit against any of these rates. The Labor Commissioner’s tips page says California does not let you count tips toward minimum wage. The guest’s tip is on top of the hourly rate. Pasadena’s notice says the same thing in its own sentence: $18.57 in addition to any tips received.

Tips, and who can now cite you for them

Labor Code section 351 says a gratuity is the sole property of the employee or employees it was left for. You do not take a part of it. You do not deduct it from wages. You do not credit it against the wage you owe. If the guest tips on a card, you pay the full amount written on the slip, with no deduction for the card-processing fee, no later than the next regular payday after the guest authorized the charge.

The section was amended by SB 648, effective January 1, 2026. The new sentence authorizes the Labor Commissioner to investigate and issue a citation, or file a civil action, for gratuities taken or withheld in violation of that section. The Labor and Workforce Development Agency flagged that citation power in its list of laws that took effect at the start of 2026. The rule about whose money the tip is did not start in 2026. The practical change is that the Labor Commissioner can cite you for it through the same path the statute points to in section 1197.1.

A tip pool can still be required. The Labor Commissioner’s tips page says involuntary tip pooling is allowed when it does not compensate an owner, a manager, or a supervisor, even if that person touches a table, and when the pool is fair and reasonable. The people in the pool have to be employees the tip was left for, in the chain of service. A manager in the pool is the version I ask about. The same page says a mandatory service charge is not automatically a tip, and it is not automatically outside the rule either. It cites O’Grady v. Merchant Exchange Productions, Inc. (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 771, where the court held a mandatory service charge on food and beverages could be a gratuity. If you add 20 percent for a buyout and keep it, read that question on the Labor Commissioner’s page before you decide the money is yours. I am not going to call it one way from a menu.

The break is a wage claim

Missed breaks are on IRMI’s list of pay-practice claims, which is the list sitting outside the indemnity. The rule is older than this year’s bills. It is also the one a letter loves, because a dining room runs through the rush and the break gets “made up later.”

The Labor Commissioner’s meal-period page says you may not employ someone for a work period of more than five hours without a meal period of at least thirty minutes. If the day is no more than six hours, the meal can be waived by mutual consent. A second meal is required after ten hours, with a waiver only if the day is no more than twelve hours and the first meal was not waived. The first meal has to come by the end of the fifth hour. Being relieved of all duty is the test. The person has to be free to leave. If you require them to stay on the premises, the meal is paid, even if they are not working. An on-duty meal, the kind a solo coffee window sometimes needs, requires that the nature of the work actually prevent relief, plus a written agreement the employee can revoke. The page’s examples are a sole worker in a coffee kiosk, a sole worker in an all-night convenience store, and a security guard alone at a remote site. A busy floor with three servers is not that example.

The page cites Brinker Restaurant Corp. v. Superior Court (2012) 53 Cal.4th 1004 for the part owners argue about. You do not have to police the parking lot to be sure nobody answered a text about a side of rice. You do have to relieve them of duty, give up control, give a real chance at an uninterrupted thirty minutes, and not impede or discourage the break. A policy in the handbook, plus a closing shift that cannot actually stop, is the fact pattern the case is talking about. If the person is truly relieved and then freely keeps working, you owe wages for that time, and the extra hour of premium is a different question. Skipping the meal so someone can leave thirty minutes early is not a waiver. The page says that outright.

Rest is separate. The Labor Commissioner’s rest-period page says you authorize and permit a net ten consecutive minutes for each four hours worked, or a major fraction of four. The Division treats more than two hours as a major fraction of four. A day under three and a half hours does not get a rest period. The ten minutes are paid. They are off duty. The page says a rest period is not the trip to the restroom, and the suitable resting place is separate from the toilet rooms. Insofar as practicable, the rest falls in the middle of the work period. The California Supreme Court case the page cites, Augustus v. ABM Security Services, Inc. (2016) 5 Cal.5th 257, says you relinquish control. An on-call rest, still tied to the radio, does not count.

The premium, if you do not provide the meal, is one extra hour of pay at the regular rate for that workday. The premium, if you do not provide the required rest, is also one extra hour for that workday, not one hour for every rest you missed inside the day. They are two different failures. Both are wages. Both are the kind of pay-practice allegation IRMI says the employment-practices indemnity does not pick up.

PAGA is the meter on that claim

The Private Attorneys General Act lets an aggrieved employee sue for civil penalties on behalf of the state, for Labor Code violations, including the wage rules above. The Department of Industrial Relations says Governor Newsom signed the reform, AB 2288 and SB 92, on July 1, 2024. The penalties themselves are in Labor Code section 2699, which I am using for the numbers below. The agency’s PAGA FAQ is the plainer version of the same reform.

For a provision that does not already name its own civil penalty, the statute sets $100 for each aggrieved employee per pay period, if you have one or more employees. It rises to $200 per employee per pay period if, in the five years before the violation, an agency or a court already found the policy unlawful, or if a court decides the conduct was malicious, fraudulent, or oppressive. A crew of twelve, paid every other week, is twenty-six pay periods. Twelve times twenty-six times $100 is $31,200, for one kind of violation, over one year, before anyone argues about a cap, a cure, or a lower award. That is arithmetic off the statute. It is not a forecast of what a judge will do. The letter usually names more than one kind.

The reform is the part that rewards a file you built before the letter. If, before you receive the PAGA notice, or before you receive a records request under Labor Code sections 226, 432, or 1198.5, you have taken all reasonable steps to comply with the provisions in the notice, the civil penalty shall not be more than 15 percent of the penalty sought. If you were not there yet, and within 60 days after the notice you take all reasonable steps to be in compliance going forward, the cap is 30 percent. The statute’s examples of reasonable steps are periodic payroll audits and action on what the audit finds, lawful written policies, training supervisors on the Labor Code and the wage order, and corrective action with supervisors. Reasonableness looks at the whole picture, including the size and resources of the employer and how serious and how long the violation was. A violation can still exist. The statute says the existence of a violation, by itself, does not prove you failed to take the steps.

Read the exceptions before you treat 15 percent as a promise. The 15 percent and 30 percent caps do not apply to that $200 penalty. A court may award less than the maximum, and it may also go above the caps, if sticking to them would be unjust, arbitrary and oppressive, or confiscatory. If you satisfy the 15 percent section or the 30 percent section and you cure the violation, the statute says you are not required to pay a civil penalty for that violation. Cure, for wages, means each aggrieved employee is made whole: unpaid wages going back three years from the notice, 7 percent interest, any liquidated damages the underlying statute requires, and reasonable attorney’s fees and costs. Paying the missed break premium on the next check, and calling it cured, is not the sentence in the statute.

For a case under the amended statute, 65 percent of the civil penalties go to the Labor and Workforce Development Agency and 35 percent to the aggrieved employees. The code says those amendments apply to a civil action brought on or after June 19, 2024, and not to a case whose notice was filed before that date. The agency’s FAQ describes the same 65/35 split, and the same 15 and 30 percent caps, for notices filed on or after June 19, 2024. The penalty is not a bonus paid to one plaintiff. Most of it, on a reformed case, is the state’s. It is still your check. And it is still the kind of wage exposure the employment-practices indemnity, as IRMI describes it, does not pay.

The notice you were supposed to hand out in February

Labor Code section 1553 is the Workplace Know Your Rights Act, SB 294, effective January 1, 2026. On or before February 1, 2026, and every year after, you give each current employee a stand-alone written notice. You also give it to each new hire. You give it the way you normally communicate: in person, email, or text, if you can reasonably expect the person to have it within one business day. If the employee has an authorized representative, that person gets it annually too, by electronic or regular mail.

The notice has to cover workers’ compensation, the right to notice of an I-9 inspection, protection against unfair immigration-related practices, the right to organize, and constitutional rights when law enforcement is at the workplace. It also has to include new legal developments the Labor Commissioner decides belong there, and a list of the agencies that enforce the rights. You give it in the language you normally use with that employee, if the Labor Commissioner’s template exists in that language. If it does not, English is permitted. You keep a record of compliance for three years, including the date each notice was provided or sent.

The Labor Commissioner’s page on the new laws says the template is posted, and that it is available in English, Spanish, Chinese (simplified and traditional), Vietnamese, Korean, Tagalog, Hindi, Punjabi, Arabic, and Urdu. Use the template. Do not draft a prettier one and hope. The same page describes the other half of the law, which is in the statute’s neighboring sections and on that page in the agency’s words. If an employee has asked you to notify a designated emergency contact, you notify that person if the employee is arrested or detained at the worksite, or, when you actually know, if the arrest or detention happens during work hours off the worksite. The page says that duty started January 1, 2026, and that by March 30, 2026 you had to give employees the chance to name the contact and to say whether you should make that call. If the February notice never went out, send it, note the date, and keep the note. The next annual date is still coming. A new hire still gets it on the way in.

January 1, and a comment about someone’s papers

The bill I would put in a pre-shift meeting before New Year’s is AB 2495, Chapter 748, approved by the Governor on September 29, 2026. It amends Labor Code section 1019. It is not an urgency statute. The chaptered text has no urgency clause. Article IV, section 8 of the California Constitution says a statute enacted at a regular session goes into effect on January 1 next following a 90-day period from enactment, and that urgency statutes are the ones that take effect immediately. For this bill, that date is January 1, 2027.

The amended section makes it unlawful to use an unfair immigration-related practice to retaliate against a person for exercising, or trying to exercise, a right under any local, state, or federal statute or regulation that applies to employees. The listed practices, when done for that purpose, are asking for more or different documents than federal law requires, or refusing documents that reasonably appear genuine on their face; using E-Verify at a time or in a way federal law does not require; threatening to file, or filing, a false police report or a false report to a state or federal agency; and threatening to contact, or contacting, immigration authorities. Conduct at the express and specific direction or request of the federal government is not included.

The new subdivision reaches further than that list. It also makes it unlawful to engage in any other conduct, related to a person’s perceived immigration status, that would reasonably tend to dissuade them from doing something they have a legal right to do, or to push them into something they have a legal right to refuse. Actual immigration status does not matter. “Employee” includes an applicant and a former employee. A violation within 90 days of the person exercising a protected right raises a rebuttable presumption that it was retaliation.

The civil penalty is up to $10,000 per employee or person for each violation, awarded to the person who suffered it, on top of other remedies, including damages and attorney’s fees if they prevail. A court can also order agencies to suspend licenses required to operate the business at that location. The statute says a professional license is not included. A first violation can be a suspension of up to 14 days, a second up to 30, a third or later up to 90. The court has to weigh whether the employer knew, the good-faith effort to fix it after notice, and the harm a suspension would do to other employees. I am not going to tell you which of your permits a judge would pick. I am going to tell you the statute gives the court that tool, at the address where it happened.

In a restaurant, the fact I worry about is ordinary. Someone asks why Friday’s check was short, or why the break never came, and a manager answers with a comment about papers. That comment is now the kind of conduct this section is written to reach, once January 1 arrives. It is also the kind of fact that rides along with a wage claim. The wage piece is the claim your employment-practices indemnity, as IRMI describes the market, does not pay. The retaliation piece is the claim that form is actually for. They arrive in the same envelope.

What I would do before the next payroll

None of this requires a new handbook company. It requires the pages you already have, read against the hour the person worked.

  1. Pay the rate for the address. State $16.90 until January 1, then $17.40, unless the hour was in Pasadena at $18.57, unincorporated county at $18.47, the City of Los Angeles at $18.42, or a covered fast-food restaurant at $20. Tips on top. A salaried manager has to clear the salary test and the duties test. On January 1 the state salary number DIR published is $72,384. On a covered fast-food job, DIR’s FAQ still states $83,200.
  2. Pay the card tips in full, on the next payday, and keep owners, managers, and supervisors out of the pool. The Labor Commissioner can cite you for a tip that was taken or withheld. If a service charge is mandatory, read the Labor Commissioner’s question on O’Grady before the money lands in the house account.
  3. Relieve people for the meal by the end of the fifth hour, and authorize the ten-minute rest. Write down that it happened. A rush is not a waiver. A one-person coffee window is the narrow on-duty case, and it wants a written agreement. The extra hour of pay, if you miss it, is cheaper than the letter, and the letter is the part the employment-practices indemnity is built to decline.
  4. Build the PAGA file while nobody is suing. A periodic payroll audit you actually act on. Written policies that match the wage order. Supervisors trained. Corrective action when a closer keeps people through the break. The statute says those are the kinds of reasonable steps that can cap the penalty at 15 percent if they were already happening before the notice or the records request, or 30 percent if you start within 60 days after the notice. Size counts. A twelve-person café is not held to a hotel company’s program. Write down what you did. The cap is not the $200 penalty, and a judge can depart from it. Cure means making people whole, including interest and fees, not a promise to do better next Friday.
  5. Hand out the Know Your Rights notice, and keep the date. Use the Labor Commissioner’s template, in the language you actually use on the floor if that template exists. New hires get it. The record lives three years. If you never offered people the chance to name an emergency contact for an arrest or detention at work, the agency’s page says that chance was due March 30, 2026. Offer it now, in writing.
  6. Before January 1, tell every person who schedules, hires, or checks documents what AB 2495 covers. A comment about someone’s papers, tied to a complaint about wages, breaks, or the schedule, is the fact pattern. The penalty in the bill is up to $10,000 per person per violation, and a court can suspend licenses required to operate at that location. Actual status does not matter. Conduct the federal government specifically directed is the carve-out in the statute. A joke on the line is not that carve-out.
  7. Then read the employment-practices page, if you have one. Is third-party coverage on it, as its own agreement or as an endorsement, and is the limit separate. Is wage-and-hour defense even offered, and what is the sublimit. Do defense costs shrink the limit. Does the form say retention or deductible, and do defense costs count against it. Is it duty to defend or duty to pay. What is the retroactive date, if there is one. IRMI’s buyer’s checklist from March 1, 2000, still on its site, is where I send people for the shape of that last question: a claims-made form can cut off an act from before the retroactive date even when the claim is made during the policy. I am using that checklist for the question. I am not using any premium in a 2000 article. If the dec page has no employment-practices coverage at all, say so before you answer the letter.

If you would rather do this over a plate of food than over a PDF, pick the restaurant. Bring the employment-practices page if you have one. If you are not sure you have one, bring the dec page. I’ll buy.
