The shake is not the fire.

Before dawn on November 20, the catalog put a magnitude 3.9 a kilometer northwest of Commerce. That is not a claim. The property form a San Gabriel Valley restaurant already has excludes the shaking. The fire that follows is a different sentence.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. Written for owners, not for insurance people. I am not your lawyer.

The entry I opened is reviewed. The U.S. Geological Survey event record for ci41364111 lists magnitude 3.94, moment magnitude, one kilometer northwest of Commerce, 15.45 kilometers down, at 11:25 UTC on November 20, which is 3:25 a.m. Pacific. The record’s own title rounds it to M 3.9. It shows 14,820 felt reports. I am not going to tell you from Huntington Drive who in Alhambra woke up. I am going to tell you the question the number produces the next time an owner sits down.

The plates. The hood you paid for and cannot take when you leave. The walk-in. The rice. The week the room cannot open. The property form already on the shelf was not written to pay for the shaking. A carrier can file its own words. A later edition can move a sentence. The form on your declarations is the one that counts.

The faults are not the Whittier story people tell

Commerce is the other side of the river from the west end of the valley. The faults I will name are the ones the data center puts next to the cities where the restaurants are. I am using the Southern California Earthquake Data Center, the U.S. Geological Survey’s open-file report on the 1991 earthquake, and the 1991 network bulletin. If a page and a memory disagree, I am keeping the page.

The Sierra Madre fault zone, on the data center’s page, is a reverse fault. It dips north. The nearby communities it lists are Sunland, Altadena, Sierra Madre, Monrovia, Duarte, and Glendora. The page’s probable magnitude is 6.0 to 7.0, with a question mark. The same page says this zone was not the fault responsible for the 1991 Sierra Madre earthquake. At the junction of two segments, the Clamshell–Sawpit Canyon fault splays off toward the northeast. The data center says that fault, not the Sierra Madre fault zone itself, ruptured in 1991. The network bulletin says the same attribution: Hauksson and Jones put the June 28, 1991 shock on the Clamshell–Sawpit Canyon fault, a branch of the Sierra Madre system. The bulletin puts the epicenter 11 kilometers, 7 miles, north of Monrovia, at 7:43 a.m. Pacific, local magnitude 5.8.

The open-file report is preliminary, and it says it was not reviewed for the Survey’s editorial standards. I am using the numbers it prints. Caltech’s local magnitude was 5.8. The report counts one death, notes that another death by heart attack may have been from the stress of the shaking, counts 107 injuries, and says the greatest damage was in Monrovia and Sierra Madre, with similar but less extensive damage in Arcadia, Pasadena, and San Gabriel. It assigns those five communities a Modified Mercalli intensity of VII. The dollar figure it attributes to the Governor’s Office of Emergency Services, as of August 9, 1991, is $33.5 million, split $26.0 million private and $7.5 million public. In Pasadena it describes a large portion of the south wall of the restaurant it calls the Chicago for Ribes, at 90 West Colorado Boulevard, collapsed. In Monrovia it describes houses shifted off their foundations, and water and gas lines broken. I was not adjusting those claims. The valley has already had the morning when a dining room’s wall came down. The policy question is the same one.

The Raymond fault, on its own page, is left-lateral, with only minor reverse slip, about 26 kilometers, dipping about 75 degrees north. The nearest communities listed are San Marino, Arcadia, and South Pasadena. The page’s probable magnitude is 6.0 to 7.0. The Whittier fault is a different page: right-lateral strike-slip with some reverse slip, about 40 kilometers, dipping northeast, nearest communities Yorba Linda, Hacienda Heights, and Whittier, probable magnitude 6.0 to 7.2. I am not going to merge that fault into the 1987 earthquake because the names share a word.

The Whittier Narrows earthquake, October 1, 1987, is its own page. Local magnitude 5.9. Depth 14.6 kilometers. Eleven kilometers, 7 miles, southeast of Pasadena. Thrust faulting, on a previously unknown concealed thrust about 20 kilometers east of downtown Los Angeles. Eight fatalities. $358 million in property damage. The page says the most severe damage was in the Uptown district of Whittier, the old downtown section of Alhambra, and the Old Town section of Pasadena, where unreinforced masonry was concentrated. Wood-frame houses, it says, sustained relatively little. A brick storefront in the old downtown the page names is the shape of that sentence. It is not a sentence about the Whittier fault’s page.

The exclusion does not sort the hood from the rice

The special-form causes of loss I opened are CP 10 30 10 12. When Special is shown in the declarations, covered causes of loss means direct physical loss unless the loss is excluded or limited. Then the lead-in. The insurer will not pay for loss or damage caused directly or indirectly by any of the following. Such loss or damage is excluded regardless of any other cause or event that contributes concurrently or in any sequence to the loss.

Earth movement is the second exclusion. Earthquake, including tremors and aftershocks and any earth sinking, rising, or shifting related to such event. Landslide. Mine subsidence. Earth sinking, other than sinkhole collapse, rising or shifting, including the soil conditions the form lists. If earth movement as described in those four paragraphs results in fire or explosion, the form pays for the loss or damage caused by that fire or explosion. Volcanic eruption is a fifth paragraph, with its own giveback for fire, building-glass breakage, or volcanic action. The exclusion applies whether any of that is caused by an act of nature or is otherwise caused.

The businessowners copy I opened is BP 00 03 07 02, inside a policy packet whose earth-movement page is marked © ISO Properties, Inc., 2001. The lead-in is the same shape, and it adds that the exclusions apply whether or not the loss event results in widespread damage or affects a substantial area. Earthquake, on that edition, includes any earth sinking, rising, or shifting related to the event. I did not find the words “tremors and aftershocks” in that 07 02 wording. A June 2026 analysis of the 08 24 edition says that later edition does include tremors and aftershocks inside the earthquake paragraph. I want the edition on your declarations before I import a word from an analysis. On the 07 02 copy, if earth movement as described in paragraphs (1) through (4) results in fire or explosion, the form pays for the loss or damage caused by that fire or explosion.

The exclusion is the cause. It does not become smaller because the declarations call the property a building, business personal property, or stock. It does not become smaller because the thing that fell was expensive.

The building, if a building limit is on the declarations and you are the one insuring it, is the cracked wall and the shifted foundation. The fire that starts afterward is the giveback. The shaking that only cracks the wall is the exclusion.

Improvements and betterments are the hood, the tile, and the bar you paid for and cannot legally remove. What that phrase means, and how a tenant’s form values a repair if you do repair, is the lease note. I am not walking the actual-cash-value formula again. The cause still has to be covered. Calling the hood an improvement does not move it out of earth movement.

Kitchen equipment is a line I will not pick in order to dodge the exclusion. A range you can roll is business personal property. A walk-in that is permanently installed machinery can sit in the building definition if you insure the building, or in the improvements sentence if you paid for it and cannot take it. I will read the invoice and the lease before I pick the line. I will not pick the line and then tell you the quake is covered. A quake is not a mechanical breakdown. The compressor, and the equipment-breakdown form that can still carry an earth-movement exclusion of its own, is the equipment note and the note on Edison’s shutoffs.

Inventory is stock. Bottles on the floor because the shelf walked are this exclusion. Food that spoils because the power is off, with no direct damage from the shaking, is the utility and spoilage question I already walked in the Edison note. I am not stuffing a warm walk-in into an earthquake form you never bought, and I am not stuffing a fallen shelf into a spoilage endorsement.

Business income, on the 07 02 businessowners copy, pays the actual loss of business income you sustain from the necessary suspension of your operations during the period of restoration. The suspension must be caused by direct physical loss of or damage to property at the described premises, from a covered cause of loss. That copy also says it will only pay business income during the period of restoration that occurs within 12 consecutive months after the date of the direct physical loss, and ordinary payroll for 60 days unless the declarations show more. Shaking is not a covered cause. The weeks you are closed because the wall is cracked, and nothing burned, are not that sentence. If the fire is the covered cause, the income question is the one I already walked on the business-income form, including the wait, in the hood note. I am not pasting the 72 hours again. A later businessowners edition can type a different wait. The number on your form is the number.

The fire that follows can be the policy you already bought. The shaking is a page you have to add.

The statute kept the fire, and it did not keep the shelf

Insurance Code section 10088 applies notwithstanding sections 530 and 532. In the absence of an endorsement or an additional policy provision specifically covering the peril of earthquake, no policy which by its terms does not cover earthquake shall provide, or be held to provide, coverage for any loss or damage when earthquake is a proximate cause. That is so regardless of whether the loss or damage also directly or indirectly results from, or is contributed to, concurrently or in any sequence, by any other proximate or remote cause, whether or not that other cause is covered. The section says “policy” includes all policies of any nature, including business and commercial forms providing coverage against loss due to damage to the property of the insured.

The last sentence of 10088 is the one I do not want widened. Nothing in the section affects section 2071, and nothing in it precludes an insurer from specifically providing coverage for direct loss caused by explosion, theft, or glass breakage resulting from an earthquake. “Does not preclude” is not “your form already pays.” The earth-movement paragraphs I read give back fire and explosion. They do not, in those sentences, give back theft, and they do not give back glass, except the volcanic paragraph’s building-glass breakage. We are not adjusting a volcano.

Section 10088.5 is the fire. Notwithstanding section 2081, nothing in section 10088 exempts an insurer from its obligation under a fire insurance policy to cover the losses of a fire which is caused by or follows an earthquake. The cracked plaster, the shelf, the walk-in that walked, are 10088 if you did not buy earthquake. The fire the quake starts is 10088.5, on a fire policy. The form’s giveback and the statute are the same split. I will not let a cracked wall ride into the fire sentence, and I will not let a fire get talked out of a fire policy because the ground moved first.

Sprinkler leakage is not in that giveback. On both forms I opened, the earth-movement exception I could read is fire or explosion, not the water from a head that sheared. The sprinkler giveback I walked in the rain note sits on the water exclusion. A head that opens because the building shook is not flood, and it is not that paragraph. A June 2022 analysis of the earthquake endorsements describes a schedule choice called earthquake–sprinkler leakage only. When that is the only choice selected, the analysis says the coverage applies only to sprinkler leakage caused by or resulting from earthquake or volcanic eruption, and the deductible is the fire deductible, not the percentage deductible. I have not stapled that analysis to your policy. I want the schedule, and I want to see whether anyone bought the leakage by itself and called it earthquake.

The page you buy is an endorsement, or it is a second policy

I am not going to price either one. I am going to tell you the shape, from pages I could open, and then I want the declarations.

The endorsement is not a policy by itself. The June 2022 analysis says the earthquake endorsements do not eliminate the earth-movement exclusion. They cover earthquake, and volcanic eruption, and when that coverage conflicts with the exclusion, only the earthquake and volcanic-eruption part is overridden. Landslide stays out. The analysis says none of them is a stand-alone causes-of-loss form. CP 10 10, CP 10 20, or CP 10 30 still has to be on the policy. CP 10 40, on that analysis, is earthquake and volcanic eruption coverage with a percentage deductible, and the schedule has to show a percentage and a limit for each building or premises, or the sprinkler-leakage-only choice. All earthquake shocks, or volcanic eruptions, that occur within any 168-hour period are one occurrence. One deductible for the cluster, not a new deductible for every aftershock. CP 10 45 is the sub-limit version with a percentage deductible. The analysis says that sub-limit is not the building limit on the rest of the policy, and that it can be an annual aggregate reduced by each earthquake in the policy year. CP 10 28 and CP 10 29 are the flat-dollar versions, and the analysis says that flat deductible applies per location, to all the property scheduled there. A percentage is not the only shape. A flat dollar is a different edition. I will not assume which one is on your declarations.

On CP 10 40, the analysis says the property deductible is a percentage of the limit of insurance for the covered property, not a percentage of the loss, and it is calculated separately for each building, for personal property in each building, and for personal property in the open. The illustration uses 5 percent. The same section says that deductible provision does not apply to the business-income forms CP 00 30, CP 00 32, and CP 00 50. I will not invent the deductible that does apply to the weeks you are closed. The analysis also says the ordinance-or-law exclusion still applies unless CP 04 05 is attached, and that exterior masonry veneer on a wood frame is not covered unless the declarations include it or the veneer is less than 10 percent of the exterior wall area. It says stucco is not masonry veneer. If the front of the room is brick on wood, I want that schedule read. I have not looked at your wall. The analysis is not your form.

The second policy I opened is a difference-in-conditions form, UND 1211 (08-15), posted by ICW Group. It says it includes Insurance Services Office material, with permission, and material of Insurance Company of the West. It pays for accidental direct physical loss at the locations in the declarations, from a covered cause, unless the loss is excluded. The exclusion list is long, and it is written to sit beside the policy you already keep. One exclusion is any cause of loss covered under the policies the form’s all-risk warranty requires you to maintain. It is the difference. It is not a second copy of the fire policy.

Earthquake, on that form, means a sudden movement of the crust of the earth caused by the release of stress accumulated along geologic faults. Earthquake is then listed inside earth movement, and the exclusion applies only if earthquake contributes in any way with one of the events on a list: fire, explosion, volcanic eruption, tsunami, a utility failure, sprinkler leakage unless an earthquake-sprinkler-leakage endorsement is attached, and the others printed there. Sprinkler leakage is also its own excluded cause, unless that endorsement is on the policy. A fire is written back toward the policy that already covers fire. The shaking that arrives by itself is the gap this form is built to leave open. I am not going to summarize that as “earthquake is covered” and stop. I am telling you to read which events the exclusion turns back on.

Business interruption is a different sentence on the same form. Delay, loss of market, loss of use, interruption of business, and consequential loss are excluded except when specifically covered by endorsement. The deductible example uses a business-income line, and the form says the coverages and amounts in the example may not be applicable to your policy. A sample that shows business income has not sold you the weeks you are closed.

The deductible on that form is the percentage or the amount in the declarations. If per unit is indicated, the percentage is multiplied separately by the stated value of each coverage item. The example uses 10 percent, and it walks a building, contents, and business income as three deductibles. If per location is indicated, the percentage multiplies the values at that location, whether or not every item was damaged. If per policy is indicated, it multiplies all the values on the schedule. Ten percent is the illustration. It is not a market I am willing to announce. Covered property, when the cause is covered, includes the building, contents (furniture, fixtures, machinery, and equipment, including property of others in your care if you are liable), stock, and tenant’s improvements and betterments. The improvements definition matches the lease note: made a part of the building you occupy but do not own, acquired or made at your expense, and you cannot legally remove.

People say the deductible is a percentage. On the endorsement analysis and on this DIC form, the percentage runs against a limit or a stated value, not against whatever the loss happens to be, and it can apply separately to the building, the contents, and, if that line was actually bought, the income. The percentage itself is the one typed on the declarations. I am not going to average a 5 and a 10 into a price for a dining room in Duarte.

The CEA is a house

Section 10081 says no policy of residential property insurance may be issued or delivered in this state unless the named insured is offered earthquake coverage. The offer can sit in the residential policy, by endorsement, or in a separate policy. Section 10087 defines that residential policy. It is a policy insuring individually owned residential structures of not more than four dwelling units, individually owned condominium units, or individually owned mobilehomes, and their contents, used exclusively for residential purposes, or a tenant’s policy on the personal contents of a residential unit. It does not include insurance for real property or its contents used for any commercial, industrial, or business purpose, except a structure of not more than four dwelling units rented for individual residential purposes.

A dining room is the commercial sentence. The offer in 10081 is not an offer your businessowners carrier has to make you. The California Earthquake Authority’s own page on how to buy says it works through partners who sell residential earthquake policies for homeowners, condo unit owners, mobilehome owners, and renters. It says the CEA does not offer stand-alone policies. The residential property policy and the CEA policy have to be issued by the CEA partner. A businessowners policy on a café is not that residential policy. I will not decide a flat above the restaurant from the edge of section 10087. If you live upstairs, bring both policies. The house may be a CEA question. The hood is not.

The FAIR Plan note already caught the plan pointing at the CEA for a house, and saying the plan itself does not sell earthquake. I am not repeating the FAIR Plan. If the only property quote left is a fire policy, the shaking is still unbought until a form specifically covers it. Section 10088 is that sentence, for a commercial form as much as for any other.

The lease does not buy the peril

I am not going to invent a statute that assigns earthquake to the landlord or to the tenant. Section 10088 reaches business and commercial forms either way. The lease is who agreed to insure the building, and who agreed to insure the alterations. That reading is the lease note. This note is whether the policy that was supposed to do it specifically covers earthquake.

“Special form,” “all risk,” and “the businessowners policy” are the forms with the exclusion I just walked, until an endorsement or a second policy says otherwise. A certificate that repeats the building limit has not added the peril. A lease that says the tenant carries property insurance has not added it either. If the exhibit requires earthquake, or requires it when it is available, that is a promise you made to the landlord. The promise is not the endorsement. Bring the exhibit. I have not read yours. The Vernon lease in the other note is not an earthquake exhibit, and I am not borrowing it.

What you can do in the room is not a policy

None of this puts the shaking on the form. It is what I want done before the form is the only plan.

The Earthquake Country Alliance starts with the things that move. Heavy items go to the floor or a low shelf. Top-heavy furniture and appliances get secured to the studs. Where a gas line meets an appliance, including an oven, the connection should be flexible. In a dining room that is the shelf of glasses over the pass, the rice, and the range the gas line actually reaches. The Alliance is not writing you a coverage opinion. It is writing about what falls.

The same site’s gas page says to find the meter, know the valve, and keep a wrench where you can reach it. Shut the gas off only if you smell it, hear it escaping, or suspect a broken gas pipe, appliance, vent, or flue. Once it is off, the page says service comes back only from the utility or a qualified plumber, and that after a quake the wait for that person can be long. Do not shut the meter because the room shook and you are being careful. You may be closed until someone the utility will accept turns it on. Automatic valves exist. The page says some cities require one at a sale or a significant renovation, and that some installations need a permit. Ask the building department in the city where the meter is. I have not read Monrovia’s ordinance into Pasadena, or the other way around. The page also says about one in four fires after an earthquake is related to natural gas. That is their sentence. I am not dressing it up as a study of restaurants.

The Ready.gov QuakeSmart toolkit says to attach tall shelving to the wall with brackets or flexible straps, and once it is braced, to put lip guards on so the stock does not walk off. It also says to keep a current photo or video inventory of the premises, the equipment, the inventory, and the supplies, and to look once a year at property limits, deductibles, and coinsurance. Take the pictures on a Tuesday, when the shelf is full and the health department is not in the parking lot. The pictures do not create coverage. They are what you have when a covered fire, or an earthquake form you actually bought, asks what was there. The file name on that site is misspelled. The page I opened is that file.

What I want on the table

This is the order, whether the only excitement was a 3.9 before dawn or the lease is due Friday.

  1. The causes of loss, not the title of the policy. Is earthquake a covered cause, or is earth movement excluded. The edition. On CP 10 30 10 12 and on BP 00 03 07 02, earthquake is inside that exclusion, and the giveback I could read is fire or explosion. Tremors and aftershocks are named on the 10 12 special form. They are not named on the 07 02 businessowners wording I opened. A June 2026 analysis says the 08 24 businessowners edition names them. Your edition decides.
  2. Which page was added, if any. An endorsement number, or a second policy. On the analysis of CP 10 40, the schedule needs a limit and a percentage for each building or premises, or the sprinkler-leakage-only choice, and 168 hours is one occurrence. A sub-limit is not the building limit. A flat dollar per location is a different edition. Landslide is still out. Ordinance or law is still out unless that endorsement was bought. Masonry veneer is a schedule question if the front is brick on wood.
  3. The deductible, in words. Percentage of what: the limit, or the stated value. Per building, per unit, per location, or per policy. Whether business income has its own deductible or is not on the form at all. The DIC form I opened excludes interruption of business unless an endorsement specifically covers it, even though the illustration shows an income line. Five percent and ten percent are illustrations. The declarations are the number.
  4. The five piles, each with a limit or a blank. The building, if you insure it. The improvements you cannot remove. The equipment you can roll. The stock. The business income, and the wait, and the 12 months on the 07 02 copy if that is your edition. A blank income line is not a week of receipts.
  5. Sprinkler leakage, as its own question. The earth-movement giveback I read is not the water from a broken head. If the only earthquake grant is leakage, the analysis says the fire deductible applies, and the building is still excluded. I want that sentence read before anyone hears “we have earthquake.”
  6. The lease, one page. Who insures the building. Who insures the alterations. Whether the exhibit actually says earthquake, or only says special form. The certificate is not the endorsement. The CEA page is a house, or a condo, or a renter’s contents. It is not the dining room. If you live upstairs, bring that policy too, and I will not pretend one of them answers the other.

If the endorsements are already there, and the deductible is a number you can stand, and the income line is either bought or honestly absent, I will say that. Asking the questions can move the premium. I will not promise that it stays put. Coverage depends on the wording of the policy that is actually issued.

Lunch is the easier table. Bring the property declarations and the insurance page of the lease. Tell me what you built that you cannot take with you, and whether the shelf of glasses is strapped to anything. I’ll buy.
