Liquor liability, if you are getting a Type 41 or a Type 47

Two owners can have the same square footage and a completely different insurance conversation. The license is the first thing I want to see.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. I am not your licensing lawyer. I am the person who has to place the policy that matches the license.

One of you has a beer and wine license. The other can pour a martini. Both of you might be opening on Colorado Boulevard or on Myrtle Avenue, and both of you have been told, by someone, that “California doesn’t really do dram shop.” That sentence is half true, which is the dangerous half.

What the ABC actually printed

The Department of Alcoholic Beverage Control describes a Type 41 as an on-sale beer and wine license for an eating place. You may sell beer and wine to drink there or to take home. Distilled spirits may not be on the premises, except brandy, rum, or liqueurs used only for cooking. You have to run the place as a bona fide eating place, keep a suitable kitchen, and make actual and substantial sales of meals. Minors are allowed on the premises. The license is subject to Responsible Beverage Service rules: the people serving alcohol, and the managers of those people, have to be RBS certified.

A Type 47 is the on-sale general eating place. Beer, wine, and distilled spirits on the premises, and beer and wine to go. Same eating-place rules, same kitchen, same rule that minors may be there, same RBS requirement.

The fees are not in the same zip code, which matters when you are building an opening budget. On the ABC fee schedule effective January 1, 2026, a general license from the priority drawing, and Type 47 is in that group, carries an application fee of $19,840, and the annual fee is due with the application. A non-general license, the beer-and-wine side that includes a Type 41, carries an application fee of $1,135, again with the annual fee due at application. Those are ABC’s numbers, not a quote for insurance, and not the whole cost of getting the license. A Type 47 on the open market is its own negotiation. I am only flagging the application fee so nobody budgets a spirits license like a beer and wine license.

“Bona fide eating place” is not a slogan on a menu. The ABC’s quick summary of retail laws says Type 41 and Type 47 licensees have to make actual and substantial sales of meals during normal meal hours, at least five days a week if they are open that often. Sandwiches or salads alone do not count as compliance. The department will presume you are operating as a bona fide eating place if the gross sales of food prepared and sold to guests on the premises exceed the gross sales of alcoholic beverages. Some licenses are conditioned on food being half or more of sales. If the concept is really a bar that happens to own a fryer, say so out loud. The license on the wall and the license the floor actually is are both questions an underwriter will ask. I would like to hear the honest version at lunch, not after a claim.

The lawsuit, the misdemeanor, and the hearing

California is not a wide-open dram shop state. It is also not a free pass. The ABC’s own laws-and-liability page, which says it is not legal advice, splits the risk into three rooms: criminal court, an ABC hearing, and a civil lawsuit. One bad sale can visit all three. Administrative penalties on that page run from fines to suspension to revocation of the license.

Business and Professions Code section 25602 makes it a misdemeanor to sell or give alcohol to a habitual drunkard or to an obviously intoxicated person. The same section is where the Legislature said the seller is generally not civilly liable for injuries inflicted as a result of the drinker’s intoxication. The drinking, not the pouring, is treated as the cause. Section 25602.1 cuts a hole in that immunity. A cause of action may be brought against a licensee who sells or gives alcohol to an obviously intoxicated minor, when that sale is the proximate cause of the injury or death.

Section 25658(a) is the other misdemeanor owners know by heart and still get wrong on a Saturday: selling or giving alcohol to anyone under 21.

Then the ABC adds the part that the “we’re immune” story leaves out. Under common law, a licensee has a duty to exercise reasonable care for the safety of patrons. The ABC says that if the sale is negligent, common law liability may result no matter the guest’s age. If the conduct is reckless, the ABC says the drinker, minor or adult, can recover for self-inflicted injuries. Reckless, in their words, is an unreasonable act, a disregarded risk, and a conscious indifference to the consequences.

Insurance does not make the misdemeanor go away. It is there, if the policy says so, for the lawsuit and the lawyers.

Being shielded from a certain kind of suit does not stop someone from filing one. Defense is the part of the policy people forget until the complaint arrives. I have a shorter version of this on the restaurant page, under the over-service scenario, if you want it in six lines.

General liability is usually the wrong drawer

If you are in the business of selling alcohol, the general liability policy is generally written to send alcohol claims somewhere else. I say this so often I put it on the restaurant page and sourced it: general liability usually does not cover alcohol-related incidents. Liquor liability is the coverage built for them. Beer and wine count. A Type 41 is not “too small” for this conversation. A café that adds a short wine list has crossed the line from “we don’t need that” to “we need to read the exclusion.”

Two quotes with the same headline limit can be different policies. When I send a file to a carrier I am asked, in one way or another, what share of sales is alcohol, what time you close, whether there is a dance floor or live music, and whether anyone is there as security. I ask you those questions because they change which companies will even look. I am not going to publish a standard price for a Type 47 in Pasadena. There isn’t one I would put my name on. The only honest price is a quote for your room, your hours, and your loss runs.

The fight on the sidewalk

The claim that keeps bar owners up is not always the driver. It is the fight outside, and the employee who steps in to break it up.

ISO’s January 1, 2026 filing, as FC&S described it in October 2025, includes an assault-or-battery exclusion for the liquor liability coverage part, form CG 35 09 01 26, and a companion for general liability, CG 40 51 01 26. Attached, they take away coverage for injury arising out of an assault or battery committed by anyone, for trying to prevent or stop it, and for negligence in hiring, training, supervision, and keeping the person involved. The words “assault” and “battery” are not defined on the forms. FC&S notes they take their ordinary meaning, and that adding the endorsement to a policy that did not have the exclusion reduces the coverage.

Your policy may not be an ISO policy. It may have had a version of this exclusion for years. It may cover the fight only up to a lower limit than the rest of the liquor liability. I will not guess which one you have, and I will not invent a dollar cap and call it typical. Bring the forms. We will read the exclusion page, not the certificate. The certificate is a one-page proof that coverage exists. It does not show you what was carved out.

What I want on the table

  • The license, or the application if you do not have it yet. A Type 47 that is still in process is not a Type 41 “for now” if you plan to pour spirits on opening night. The policy has to match the operation you will actually run.
  • A rough split of food sales and alcohol sales. Label it as an estimate if you have not opened. The ABC presumption and the underwriter are both going to ask.
  • Who is RBS certified, and who manages them. That is an ABC requirement on both of these license types. It is also the first fact I want to be able to tell a carrier. I am not claiming a carrier will discount you for it. I am claiming they will ask.
  • Last year’s incident log, if you have one. “We walked someone out and the police were not called” still belongs in the conversation. A surprise later is how a claim gets into trouble for a reason other than the exclusion.

If you are opening, read what a new restaurant actually needs before you buy the first policy you see online. The liquor line is one item on that list, and the landlord’s certificate will not wait for you to figure the rest out. The broader market, including why this exclusion is showing up now, is in the fall market note.

Lunch is still the easier way to do this. Bring the license application and the lease. I’ll bring the questions, and I’ll buy.
