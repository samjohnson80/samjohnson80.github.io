The January wage is a payroll number

On January 1, 2027 the California minimum becomes $17.40. Workers’ comp premium is built on payroll, so an estimate that still uses $16.90 can come back as an audit bill. Here is what to send over before then.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. Written for owners, not for insurance people.

The text usually lands after the lunch rush. This week it was about January. An owner in Arcadia had seen the wage number and wanted to know if the workers’ comp policy was going to send a second bill. Sometimes it does. The premium is a payroll figure with a rate attached. If the estimate on the policy still assumes the old wage, and the checks after New Year’s are higher, the audit is where those two numbers meet.

This is the version for a restaurant, bar, or café in the San Gabriel Valley. The class codes, the September 1 benchmark, and the audit rules are the workers’ comp note. The employment-practices form, and the wage claims it usually does not indemnify, are the other note. This one is the estimate sitting on the policy between now and the auditor.

What the state certified

On July 31, 2026, the Department of Finance certified the 2027 adjustment. Labor Code section 1182.12, as that letter describes it, tells the director to use the lesser of 3.5 percent or the change in the U.S. CPI-W. The average index from July 1, 2025 through June 30, 2026 was up 2.99 percent from the year before. The letter says the minimum wage, which has been $16.90 an hour for all employers since January 1, 2026, therefore increases by 2.99 percent, to $17.40 an hour, for all employers, on January 1, 2027.

The Department of Industrial Relations said the same thing in its August 13, 2026 release. Beginning January 1, 2027, the statewide minimum is $17.40 an hour. The release also does the salary math for the overtime exemption: $17.40 times 2 times 40 hours times 52 weeks is $72,384. A person has to earn at least that much, and still meet the duties test, before you can treat the job as exempt from overtime. A kitchen manager under that salary is not exempt because the card says manager. The Labor Commissioner’s minimum-wage page still shows the rate in force today, $16.90 an hour for all employers, effective January 1, 2026, and it points employers to the city and county list when a local rate is higher.

DIR’s release says you post the statewide minimum-wage order, and the industry wage order, where employees can read them. The pay stub has to list the rates. When a city or county rate is higher, you pay the higher one for the hours it covers. A short check is back wages, penalties, and liquidated damages. That last sentence is the claim I come back to at the end. It is not a workers’ comp claim.

I can tell you the floor. The invoice is the payroll you actually run.

Where this valley already sits higher

January 1 does not lift every hour in the San Gabriel Valley. Some floors are already above $17.40, and they stay there until the city, the county, or the fast-food rules say otherwise. The Labor Commissioner’s page points to the UC Berkeley Labor Center inventory. On the July 1, 2026 table I opened, Pasadena is $18.57, unincorporated Los Angeles County is $18.47, and the City of Los Angeles is $18.42. Monrovia, Arcadia, Duarte, Azusa, Glendora, Sierra Madre, San Gabriel, Alhambra, and South Pasadena are not on that table. For an hour worked inside those city limits, I start with the state rate. If a local ordinance shows up later, DIR says the higher rate is the one you pay.

The figures below are the rates on the pages I linked. “Moves on January 1” means the state step from $16.90 to $17.40 actually raises the floor for that hour. An hour you already pay above the floor does not have to move just because the statute did. On a phone, swipe for the last column.

Pasadena’s official notice says $18.57 applies to adult and minor employees who work two or more hours a week in the city, in addition to any tips. The city’s minimum-wage page posts that notice for July 1, 2026. The handout dates the rate from July 1, 2026 through June 30, 2027. A Pasadena hour does not pick up the January state step. The next city number, after June 30, is not in the notice I read.

The county’s notice for fiscal year 2026–27 says the Chief Executive Office set the unincorporated rate at $18.47, up from $17.81, effective July 1, 2026, a 3.7 percent step off the November 2025 Los Angeles-area CPI-W. The employer page says anyone who works at least two hours in a week in unincorporated county gets that rate for those hours. Where you live, and where the business is headquartered, do not decide it. The address where the hour was worked does. If you are not sure the block is unincorporated, the county says to look the address up on the Registrar-Recorder’s site. I would rather you check the address than guess from the city name on the awning. The county page says the rate increases each July 1. It does not publish the July 1, 2027 number. January 1 is not that adjustment.

Covered fast food is a different floor, and it did not move on the pages I opened this week. The Labor Commissioner’s FAQ still says $20.00 an hour, effective April 1, 2024, for a restaurant that meets all three tests: limited service, where the guest orders and pays before eating; part of a chain of at least 60 establishments nationwide; and primarily engaged in selling food and beverages for immediate consumption. A franchisee is included. A sit-down dining room in Arcadia is not this bucket. A national counter in Pasadena may be, and Pasadena’s own notice says the state’s fast-food rate supersedes the city rate when the employer meets the definition of a national fast-food chain. The FAQ also says a salaried manager at a covered fast-food restaurant who is paid less than $83,200 does not meet the salary half of the exemption. That figure is the one the FAQ ties to the $20 wage. I do not see a 2027 revision of it on that page.

The Fast Food Council’s page still describes the $20 rate from AB 1228 and says the council can set later wages. The meetings page lists council and subcommittee dates through February 26, 2025, and the page is marked April 2025. I do not see a 2026 or 2027 meeting there, and I do not see a wage order above $20. The FAQ says the council may raise the hourly rate by no more than the lesser of 3.5 percent or the CPI-W change, and that no council increase could take effect before January 1, 2025. Authority is not a new number. Until DIR posts one, the fast-food floor I can cite is $20. If you run a covered counter, do not build a January estimate off $17.40. You are already above it.

Why the workers’ comp policy cares

The Department of Insurance guide, revised June 23, 2025, walks the arithmetic in plain order. Estimated payroll in a classification, per $100, times the rate the carrier filed for that class, is the base premium. Experience modification, and usually a schedule or judgment factor, moves that base. The product is an estimate. The final premium waits until the term is over and the payroll records have been audited. If payroll ran above the estimate, you owe the difference. If it ran below, the company owes you a return premium. The guide says some carriers will take monthly payroll. If yours will not, you can tell the broker or the underwriter when the number moves during the year. A corrected estimate during the term, in the department’s words, can help minimize a large premium audit bill or a large return premium, either of which can hit cash flow.

That is the whole January problem. Fifty cents an hour is the state step, for an hour that was sitting on $16.90 and has to come up to $17.40. A person at 30 hours a week, 52 weeks, is 1,560 of those hours. Fifteen hundred sixty times fifty cents is $780 of extra payroll for that one person, across a full year at the new rate. Your policy may cover only the months after January 1. Your week may not be 30 hours. People you already pay at $17.40 or more, in a city that follows the state rate, do not have to move because the floor moved. The auditor uses the payroll records, not this sketch. I am showing the shape of the number so the estimate you send me has one.

I will not turn $780 into a premium. The rate on your policy is the one your carrier filed. The statewide advisory figures, and the restaurant classes, live in the September note. Updating the estimate does not freeze a price, and it does not hold the old price. A higher payroll is a higher base, at that carrier’s rate, before the modification. What the update does is put the difference into the deposit you already expect, instead of into a bill that arrives after the auditor is done. If the policy is already at the company’s minimum premium, a small payroll change may not move the deposit at all. The department’s guide describes a minimum premium as the smallest amount the company will charge to issue the policy. I will not guess whether you are in that bucket. The page with the estimate on it will say.

Build the estimate from the payroll the bureau actually uses. The WCIRB’s guide to remuneration counts gross wages, salaries, commissions, bonuses, most profit sharing, vacation, holiday and sick pay, and the straight-time portion of overtime. It leaves out tips. It also leaves out the premium portion of overtime, which is the amount above the regular rate. The California Basic Underwriting Manual effective September 2026 is the longer version of that rule. Tips received from someone other than the employer are not payroll. The premium portion of overtime comes out only when the records show it separately, by employee, and in summary by the type of operation: either regular-rate pay for all hours plus the premium portion, or regular-rate pay for the non-overtime hours plus the total paid for the overtime hours. A swing-shift or graveyard differential is not overtime under that rule. It stays in. If the records do not split the overtime premium, the exclusion is not available and the overtime payment stays in the payroll. The manual says it is guidance for the bureau’s members and is not itself a Department of Insurance regulation. I still want the records kept the way it describes, because that is the page an auditor is holding.

The salary test is a payroll event even when the hourly crew is already above $17.40. If you raise a manager to $72,384 so the job can stay exempt, that new salary is payroll. The bureau’s guide counts salaries. If you leave the salary where it is and the person starts earning overtime, the straight-time portion of those hours is payroll, and the premium portion depends on the records. Fast-food managers are on the FAQ’s $83,200 figure, not on $72,384, for as long as that FAQ still says so. Duties still have to match. The longer version of the exemption, the breaks, and the penalties is the employment-practices note. For this page, the point is simpler. A salary you lift in January belongs in the estimate you send in November.

What I want in a text before the new year

None of this requires a new payroll company. It requires the address, the hours, and the estimate that is already on the policy.

  1. Name the address where the hour is worked. State rate, Pasadena at $18.57, unincorporated county at $18.47, or a covered fast-food restaurant at $20. Two hours in a week is enough to pull that hour onto the Pasadena or county rate. Check the block if the awning and the jurisdiction might not match.
  2. Count only the hours that have to rise. An hour already at or above the floor that applies to it does not move because the state number moved. An hour on $16.90 in Monrovia, Arcadia, San Gabriel, or any other city that is following the state rate does move, by fifty cents, on January 1.
  3. Add the salary changes. Anyone you are raising to $72,384 to keep the state exemption, or anyone who will start earning overtime instead, changes the payroll. On a covered fast-food job, use the FAQ’s $83,200 until DIR changes that page.
  4. Leave the tip jar out of the workers’ comp number. Gross wages and salaries in. Tips from the guest out. Keep the overtime premium visible in the records if you want it excluded. A night differential stays in.
  5. Send the revised estimate for the rest of this policy term, and for the next one if you are already in renewal. A policy that started in September does not reset on New Year’s Day. Only the hours from January 1 through the expiration pick up the new wage. If the carrier takes monthly payroll, January’s report is the one that should show it. If the carrier does not, the department’s guide says to tell the broker or the underwriter during the term. I would rather have the number in December than explain it in April.
  6. Post the new order, and put the rate on the stub. DIR’s release says both. A wage you pay under the floor that applies to that hour is the claim in the next section.

Deliberate under-reporting is insurance fraud. The department’s guide says so. I want the payroll you are actually going to run. A clever estimate is a worse conversation than a high one.

The short paycheck is a different policy

Workers’ comp pays a work injury. It does not pay the fifty cents you missed. General liability does not pay it either. The form owners hope will answer a wage letter is employment practices liability, and the market for that form usually leaves the wages themselves with you.

The International Risk Management Institute says insurers universally exclude indemnity for wage-and-hour claims under employment-practices policies. Indemnity is the back wages, the settlement, the judgment. IRMI says a handful of insurers will pay to defend the claim, under a sublimit typically of $100,000 or $250,000. IRMI’s separate entry on the wage-and-hour endorsement says the endorsement, when you can get it, covers defense costs, that coverage for the settlement or the judgment typically does not apply, and that a sublimit usually applies. The example range in that entry runs from $100,000 to $500,000. Those two pages do not name the same cap. The number that matters is the one printed on your form. Read it before you assume a January wage claim has a policy behind it.

The questions on that form, the third-party guest coverage, the retention, and the 2026 and 2027 wage rules that sit next to this increase are already written up in the employment-practices note. That note is the letter. This one is the estimate. If the dec page has no employment-practices coverage at all, say so while we are still talking about payroll, not after a former opener files the claim.

If you would rather do the arithmetic on a napkin than in a spreadsheet, pick the restaurant. Bring the workers’ comp page, or a photo of the payroll estimate. I’ll buy.
