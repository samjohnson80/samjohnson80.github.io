Note · November 10, 2026 · Monrovia

The storefront is 120 days. The kitchen is still limited.

Assembly Bill 1679 gives a San Gabriel Valley chef or caterer a short path into an eligible commercial space. It starts in 2027. It does not hand you a dining room, a liquor license, or a certificate the landlord can treat as the policy.

By Sam Johnson. I sit down with people trying a residency and people opening for real, from Monrovia out through the valley. I am not your land-use lawyer, and I am not ABC.

The link going around is Eater LA’s October 15 rundown of bills the governor signed on September 30. The one chefs are circling is Assembly Bill 1679. A dark bay, a short clock, a way to test the food before a long lease. I like the instinct. I want the chapter on the table before anyone calls the bay a restaurant, and I want the insurance conversation to start with the license or the lease the landlord is actually going to send.

What was signed, and when it starts

Assembly Bill 1679, lead authors Mark González and Wicks, is Chapter 825 of the Statutes of 2026. The status page says the governor approved it on September 30, 2026, and the Secretary of State chaptered it the same day. It adds Chapter 5.9, starting at Government Code section 51028, and it amends the definition of a food facility. The status page marks the measure non-urgency, non-appropriation, and non-tax levy. The chapter itself does not name an effective date.

Article IV, section 8(c)(1) of the California Constitution says a statute enacted at a regular session goes into effect on January 1 next following a 90-day period from the date of enactment, except for the cases in the next two paragraphs. Paragraph (3) is the immediate list: statutes calling elections, tax levies, appropriations for the usual current expenses of the State, and urgency statutes. This bill is outside that list. A 90-day period measured from September 30, 2026 ends in December 2026. The January 1 next following that period is January 1, 2027. Eater’s rundown says most of the laws from the session take effect that day. I am using the constitution for this one, not the roundup.

The governor’s September 30 release describes the bill as requiring cities to create a new permit so pop-up businesses can operate in vacant stores for up to 120 days. Read section 51028.5(f) next to that sentence. A city, county, or city and county may meet the chapter through an existing permitting framework, or by creating a new authorization, if what it issues satisfies or exceeds the chapter. And the definition of an eligible commercial space, in section 51028.3, does not use the word vacant. The findings do. The press release does. The tests in the definition are the ones I would walk the space against.

The clock, and which space counts

Section 51028.5 says each local jurisdiction shall allow a temporary commercial activation authorization for a pop-up small business to operate in an eligible commercial space for a period not exceeding 120 days. A local jurisdiction may allow a renewal or an extension. It may require an application for permanent occupancy if the cumulative time of an eligible temporary use in a single space exceeds 12 months within any 24-month period. The chapter says that sentence is not a ban on requiring the permanent application, and it is not a ban on your applying for permanent occupancy whenever you are ready. A renewal is something the city or county may allow. It is not a second 120 days you can assume.

An eligible commercial space, in section 51028.3, is an existing building or tenant space that meets four tests. It was granted a certificate of occupancy, or it passed a final inspection, that permitted commercial use, or the jurisdiction already allows commercial use there. It meets the health and safety standards for the use you are proposing. It is not currently undergoing a structural alteration. It does not require modification of fire-resistant elements. For a food facility, the space also has to be fully enclosed by floors, walls, and a ceiling. A patio with a tent is not that sentence. If the bay fails one of the tests, the chapter is not your permission slip. The city pages are where I keep who signs off locally. The opening checklist is the longer sequence if this residency turns into a real opening.

The kitchen the chapter actually describes

Section 51028.3 limits an eligible temporary use to commercial activity in a Group B or Group M occupancy, as those groups are defined in the California Building Code, with an occupant load not exceeding 49. The same section says an eligible temporary use does not include Assembly, Institutional, Residential, or High-Hazard occupancies. The parentheses in the chapter call that building code “Part 7 of Title 24.” On the Building Standards Commission’s list for the 2025 Title 24, effective January 1, 2026, Part 2 is the California Building Code and Part 7 is the California Wildland-Urban Interface Code. The same chapter cites the Fire Code as Part 9, which matches the commission’s list. I am reading the occupancy words in Part 2. I would ask the counter which book they have open before I argued with the parentheses.

In the 2025 California Building Code, section 303.3, Group A-2 is the assembly use intended for food or drink consumption. The list includes restaurants, cafeterias and similar dining facilities, including the associated commercial kitchens, and taverns and bars. Section 303.1.1 says a building or tenant space used for assembly purposes with an occupant load of less than 50 persons shall be classified as a Group B occupancy. The chapter’s cap is 49, which is under 50. Section 51028.11 says the authorization shall not allow a change to a higher-risk occupancy. It may allow a limited temporary change between Group B and Group M if the occupant load does not exceed 49, no structural alteration is required, and the fire, health, and safety standards for the temporary occupancy are met. Section 309 is Group M, mercantile: display and sale of merchandise the public can walk into, including markets and retail or wholesale stores. A retail bay that becomes a full dining room is the change I would not assume the authorization allows. Whether 49 people and a hot plate land you in Group B, or in A-2, is the building official’s call. I will not make it from Huntington Drive.

Food has a second fence. Section 51028.3 says an eligible temporary use for a food facility is limited to activities consistent with limited food preparation under the Retail Food Code. Health and Safety Code section 113818 is that list. It includes heating, frying, baking, roasting, cooking and seasoning to order, and holding, portioning, and dispensing food a catering operation prepared. It includes hot and cold holding, and reheating, of food that was prepared at an approved permanent food facility and held at the temperatures the chapter requires. It does not include thawing. It does not include cooling cooked potentially hazardous food. It does not include washing food. It does not include cooking potentially hazardous food for later use. It does not include grinding raw ingredients or potentially hazardous food. Slicing and chopping potentially hazardous food, other than produce, is out unless it happens on the heated cooking surface. Tomorrow’s menu, prepped tonight in the pop-up, is the excluded side of that section. Food that was made at the permitted kitchen and is finished here is closer to the included side. I am not the inspector.

Section 51028.17 says you do not begin until the local enforcement agency has done a field evaluation and determined the space can support limited food preparation. The local environmental health agency may add temporary food-safety requirements that stay consistent with the Retail Food Code. Section 2 of the act puts a pop-up small business that has been approved for that limited preparation into the definition of a food facility in Health and Safety Code section 113789. Approval is a food facility. A hopeful lease is not.

Two more limits I do not want discovered on a Saturday. Section 51028.13 says the authorization has to comply with the health and safety standards for temporary uses or temporary structures in the Building Code, and with the temporary-events provisions of the Fire Code, including fire department access. It does not limit a local fire official who needs more conditions to protect people. Section 51028.15 says the authorization does not exempt the space from state or federal accessibility requirements. The jurisdiction has to give you written accessibility guidance, and it may require you to acknowledge the obligation. The authorization is not a finding that the space is fully accessible for a permanent occupancy. Section 51028.21 says the authorization does not confer a vested right, does not count as permanent occupancy, and does not establish a legal nonconformity. The jurisdiction may revoke it for a violation. It may charge a fee, not above the reasonable cost of running the program.

The chapter gives you a clock. It does not give you a dining room.

The license or the lease, then the certificate

The chapter does not print the insurance requirements. The landlord’s paper does. Sometimes that paper is a lease. Sometimes, for a short residency, it is a license to occupy. Either way, read the insurance paragraph before you buy anything. The longer version of that habit, for a place you are actually opening, is what a new California restaurant actually needs. This note is the short run.

What the landlord usually asks to see is a certificate, and behind it an additional-insured endorsement. Those are two different pieces of paper. Insurance Code section 384 says a certificate or verification given in place of the policy has to say, in substance, that it is not an insurance policy and does not amend, extend, or alter the coverage. The ACORD 25 (2001/08) reproduced by Fresno State says the certificate is issued as a matter of information only, confers no rights on the certificate holder, and does not amend, extend, or alter the coverage. The same form’s note says that if the holder is an additional insured, the policy must be endorsed, and a statement on the certificate does not confer those rights in place of the endorsement. A later ACORD edition changes wording. The sentence I care about does not: the certificate is not the endorsement.

The lessor endorsement I can point at in public is ISO CG 20 11 01 96, on that same Fresno State page. The schedule has three lines: the part of the premises leased to you, the name of the person or organization, and an additional premium. It adds the scheduled person as an insured only for liability arising out of the ownership, maintenance, or use of that leased part of the premises. It then says the insurance does not apply to an occurrence after you cease to be a tenant there, or to structural alterations, new construction, or demolition performed by or for that additional insured. A later edition can add a word. Your forms list is the edition that counts. If the paper in your hand is a license and the endorsement says “leased to you” and “tenant,” I want the carrier to read the paper before we call you a tenant. I will not decide that property question at lunch. I also will not type a liability limit the lease has not shown me. The number in the license or the lease is the number they asked for. The number on the declarations is the number you bought.

The guest, and the equipment you roll in

A guest who slips, and a guest who says the food hurt them, are general liability questions. I am not re-teaching that list here. The pop-up version is the address. On CG 20 11 01 96, the landlord is an additional insured for the premises in the schedule, not for whatever bay you found on Friday. If the endorsement still describes the commissary and not the storefront, the certificate can name the landlord all day and still be describing the wrong room.

The equipment is a property form, and the edition I can show you is ISO CP 00 10 10 12. It pays for direct physical loss of or damage to covered property at the premises described in the declarations, caused by a covered cause of loss. Your business personal property, on this form, is the furniture, fixtures, machinery, equipment, stock, and other personal property you own and use in the business, located in or on the building described in the declarations, or in the open or in a vehicle within 100 feet of that building or of those premises, whichever distance is greater. The fryer at a second address is not “within 100 feet” of the first one. Leased personal property for which you have a contractual responsibility to insure is included in your business personal property, unless it is otherwise provided for under personal property of others. Borrowed equipment, and leased equipment the contract does not require you to insure, is personal property of others when it is in your care, custody, or control and at the described building. Payment for that property is for the account of the owner. If the declarations show no limit for it, this edition’s extension for personal property of others is $2,500 at each described premises. A borrowed oven does not become a $2,500 problem on purpose. Tell me about it while it is still on the truck.

Two extensions people lean on, and that I would not use as the plan for a 120-day run. The off-premises extension on this form pays up to $10,000, and one path is covered property temporarily at a location you do not own, lease, or operate. A storefront you are running is a place you operate. The newly acquired extension can pick up business personal property at a location you acquire, other than a fair, trade show, or exhibition, up to $100,000 at each building. It ends when the policy expires, when 30 days expire after you acquire the property, or when you report the values, whichever happens first. Thirty days is not 120. Put the address on the policy. A carrier’s own form can read differently. I will read the one that was issued.

The first person you pay

Labor Code section 3700 says every employer except the state shall secure the payment of compensation. The Division of Workers’ Compensation FAQ puts it in shorter words: if a business employs one or more employees, it has to satisfy the law. One hire is the line. The class codes, the September 1 benchmark, and what you can still change before an audit are the workers’ comp note. I am not repeating them. If the residency is truly only the owners and nobody else is paid, the same FAQ is where the sole-proprietor conversation starts, and how the company is formed changes the answer. I would rather look at the entity with you than have you guess from the group text. The FAQ also says what can follow if the coverage was required and was not there. I am not going to retype that schedule.

Liquor, only if ABC actually said yes

Section 51028.9 says the city or county shall consider suspending, deferring, or modifying certain standards that a permanent occupancy would trigger, including discretionary land-use procedures. The exception in that section is cannabis, adult businesses, and alcoholic beverage sales, unless the establishment is a bona fide public eating place as defined in Business and Professions Code section 23038. “Shall consider” is not “shall waive.” And a city land-use conversation is not an ABC license.

The ABC food-service page prints section 23038. A bona fide public eating place is a place regularly and in a bona fide manner used and kept open for serving meals to guests for compensation, with suitable kitchen facilities, conveniences for cooking, and an assortment of foods required for ordinary meals. The kitchen has to be sanitary, with the proper refrigeration, and it has to comply with the local health department. “Meals” means the usual assortment of foods commonly ordered at various hours. Sandwiches or salads alone do not comply. I will not tell you that limited food preparation under section 113818 is that kitchen. The two statutes use different words. Ask the city and ask ABC before you print a wine list.

If alcohol is part of the residency, I want the ABC paper, not a reading of the pop-up chapter. The department’s caterer’s permit page says a Type 58 lets certain on-sale licensees, including a Type 41 or a Type 47, cater alcoholic beverages off site, and that each event needs its own catering authorization. It also says a Type 58 is limited to 36 catered events per year at any given location, unless the district office grants the exception that page describes. The daily-license instructions are Form ABC-221. A general daily license on that page is for an organization formed for a specific charitable or civic purpose, a fraternal organization in existence for over five years, a religious organization, or a political organization. A beer and wine daily license is for an existing nonprofit of the kinds the page lists. The fees printed there are $50 a day for beer and wine, $75 a day for general, and $100 an event for a special temporary license. The form has to be in the district office at least 10 days before the event, and not more than 30 days ahead. A chef serving dinner for four months is not a description I found on either page. Call the district office. If what you actually need is a Type 41 or a Type 47 on the premises, that conversation is the liquor note. If you already hold an on-sale eating-place license and the drink is leaving in a sealed cup, that is the cocktails-to-go note. Liquor liability belongs on the policy when one of those permissions is real. It does not belong there because a group text said pop-ups can pour.

Ninety days of service, and the premium

The policy period is whatever the declarations say. Ask whether that period is the 90 or 120 days, or a year you are hoping to cancel. I will not promise that a short run prices itself as a fraction of a year.

IRMI’s definition of a minimum premium is the least amount to be charged for a particular coverage. It may apply per location, per coverage, or per policy. That is a definition, not your quote. A minimum-earned endorsement can go further. One wording I can point at is Berkley Life Sciences GL COND 5003 0308. The percentage on that form is a blank. Whatever percentage is filled in is fully earned at inception, and the first named insured is not entitled to a return of that minimum earned premium if the first named insured cancels, or if the company cancels for nonpayment of premium or for fraud or misrepresentation. I will not write a number into a blank I have not seen. IRMI’s definition of short-rate cancellation is the case where you cancel before expiration and the insurer keeps a greater share of the unearned premium than a pro rata return. The method depends on the policy. Your cancellation clause is the one I will read.

Workers’ compensation has a plainer version, and it is still not a price. The Department of Insurance guide, in the workers’ compensation brochure, describes a minimum premium as the smallest acceptable premium the company is willing to charge in order to accept a risk. Each company files its minimum with the department. The same guide says that if you cancel a workers’ compensation policy mid-term, the company returns unearned premium pro rata, unless it has disclosed, under Insurance Code section 481(c), that cancellation will be on a short-rate basis. It may charge the minimum premium if the short-rate amount is less than that minimum. A 90-day payroll does not automatically prorate past the minimum. The payroll arithmetic, and what to send before the audit, is the January wage note. Bring the declarations. The page with the policy period and the minimum on it will answer this. I will not guess it, and I will not promise a price.

What I want on the table

  • The city’s or county’s authorization, or the health department’s field-evaluation note, and the date you want to start. The food side waits on that evaluation.
  • The license or the lease, the insurance paragraph, and the landlord’s name the way it has to be typed.
  • What you own, what you are borrowing, and what a rental contract says you must insure, and which address it will sit at.
  • Whether anyone is paid. One employee is the workers’ compensation question.
  • Whether alcohol is in the plan, and the ABC paper if it is: a license for the premises, a catering authorization, a daily license, or nothing yet.
  • The day someone wants the certificate. I can issue a certificate from a policy that already has the endorsement. I cannot invent the endorsement the carrier never quoted.

If the 120 days go well and you want the space for real, we start over with the permanent list. Until then, bring the paper you have. A thin file is a normal way to begin a residency. A guessed file is worse.
