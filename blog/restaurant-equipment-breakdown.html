The compressor is not the shutoff.

Thanksgiving is Thursday. A walk-in, a reach-in, an ice machine, a combi, a panel, or the HVAC can quit in the middle of a service, and the property form I opened is written to leave that failure with you. Equipment breakdown is the page that puts a mechanical or electrical failure back on the policy, if you bought it. The food and the lost seats still have to be on that page.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. Written for owners, not for insurance people. I am not your refrigeration tech, and I am not your lawyer.

It is Thursday, November 19. Thanksgiving is a week from today. The walk-in is holding the birds and whatever the prep list still has to fit. The reach-in at the line is opening every time a ticket fires. The ice machine is the thing the bar notices first. The combi is the thing the kitchen notices when it will not come back up between parties. The panel is in a closet nobody opens until a breaker is warm. The HVAC is whatever is keeping the dining room from feeling like the kitchen.

I would rather read the form while those machines are still running. Monrovia, Arcadia, Pasadena, Alhambra, the rest of the valley. A full room does not sort a dead compressor into the right policy.

Three bad nights get told to me as if they were one story. Edison turns the power off and the food warms up. A hood fire puts the kitchen out. A compressor, a motor, a board, or a breaker dies while the lights are still on. The first one is the note on the shutoff. The second is the note on the hood. This one is the machine.

The compressor is not the shutoff. A fire in the hood is not a dead motor.

Wear is one exclusion. The breakdown is the next one.

People say the policy covers a sudden and accidental breakdown, and that wear and tear is the part you keep. The Insurance Information Institute’s small-business guide is close to that sentence. It says wear and tear is not covered because it does not meet the basic test of being accidental and unpredictable, and that regular maintenance is your responsibility. The same guide lists mechanical breakdown as something the basic businessowners policy leaves out, and that you can add. It lists spoilage as a different option. I am using the guide for that map. It is not your policy.

The forms I opened are plainer, and a little colder. Wear and tear is its own exclusion. Mechanical breakdown is the next one. A compressor that seizes on a Thursday service is the second exclusion. It does not have to have been dying for a year.

On CP 10 30 10 12, the causes-of-loss special form, the insurer does not pay for loss caused by or resulting from wear and tear. In the same list it does not pay for mechanical breakdown, including rupture or bursting caused by centrifugal force. The elevator-collision exception is the one I read. A walk-in is not an elevator. The same list excludes, for personal property, dampness or dryness of atmosphere, and changes in or extremes of temperature. Food in a box is personal property. A warm walk-in is that sentence, even before anyone argues about the motor.

If one of those excluded causes results in a specified cause of loss, or in building glass breakage, the form pays for the loss that specified cause or that glass breakage causes. Specified causes of loss, on this edition, start with fire. They include lightning, explosion, windstorm or hail, smoke, and the rest of that list. The form pays for the fire. It does not go back and pay for the mechanical breakdown that led there. A grease fire is the hood note. I am not teaching the suppression tag again.

Electrical damage is a separate exclusion on the same form. Artificially generated electrical, magnetic, or electromagnetic energy that damages or disrupts an electrical or electronic wire, device, appliance, system, or network is out. The form’s own examples include electrical current, including arcing. If fire results, the insurer pays for the loss or damage caused by that fire. A panel that arcs and burns the closet can be a fire claim. A panel that dies, and does not burn, is the exclusion.

The businessowners edition I opened for this question is BP 00 03 01 10. The notes on the hood and the till used the 07 13 edition. I am not going to pretend a word survived the trip unless I read it here. On 01 10, other types of loss include wear and tear, and then mechanical breakdown, including rupture or bursting caused by centrifugal force. If an excluded cause in that list results in a specified cause of loss or building glass breakage, the form pays for that resulting loss. Fire is a specified cause on this edition too.

Two sentences on 01 10 are narrower than the property form, and I want them on the table before anyone tells you a point-of-sale terminal is “just equipment.” The mechanical-breakdown exclusion does not apply to the breakdown of a computer. And the electrical-apparatus exclusion, which is the same artificially generated energy exclusion, including arcing, will still pay for loss or damage to a computer from that energy if the occurrence took place within 100 feet of the described premises, or if a power interruption, surge, blackout, or brownout had a cause within those 100 feet. A terminal that fails as a computer is a question I will read on your edition. A surge that starts past that 100 feet is not the sentence I just read. The cards in the terminal, and a register that is locked by someone else, are the note on the register. I am not teaching them again.

The Hartford, on a page updated November 17, 2026, describes the split the way an owner hears it. Commercial property, in their telling, is the outside event: fire, storms, burglary, theft. Equipment breakdown is an internal mechanical, electrical, or pressure-system failure. The examples include an air conditioner that stops blowing cold air, an electrical panel or circuit breaker that stops working after a power surge, and a motor burnout. They say normal wear and tear is not covered. They name restaurants and coffee shops among the businesses that may need the coverage. Their own answer on a businessowners policy is the one I will steal, because it is the honest one: equipment breakdown may be included, or it may have to be added, and you ask. I have not counted how many valley policies already have it.

A compressor can be sudden and still be excluded. The property form does not wait to see if it was tired.

What the equipment-breakdown form adds

The equipment-breakdown form I opened is EB 00 20 01 13. The covered cause of loss is a breakdown to covered equipment. Each coverage below is provided only if a limit, or the word INCLUDED, is shown for it in the declarations. If neither is shown, that coverage is not provided. A blank line is not a favor. A carrier can file its own form. A later edition can move a word. The declarations that were issued are the page that counts.

Breakdown, on this edition, means a direct physical loss that causes damage to covered equipment and necessitates its repair or replacement. The loss has to be one of three kinds: failure of pressure or vacuum equipment; mechanical failure, including rupture or bursting caused by centrifugal force; or electrical failure, including arcing. It does not mean a malfunction, and the form’s examples of a malfunction include adjustment, alignment, calibration, cleaning, or modification. It does not mean leakage at a valve, fitting, shaft seal, gland packing, joint, or connection. It does not mean defects, erasures, errors, limitations, or viruses in computer equipment and programs. Cleaning a coil is not a breakdown. A virus in the point-of-sale system is not a breakdown. A failure that damages the machine, and that requires a repair or a replacement, is the definition.

Covered equipment means equipment built to operate under internal pressure or vacuum, other than the weight of its contents; electrical or mechanical equipment used in the generation, transmission, or utilization of energy; and communication equipment and computer equipment. That is the shape of a walk-in compressor, a reach-in, an ice machine, a combi, an HVAC unit, a panel, and a terminal, if the machine meets the definition and is not on the “does not include” list. That list is real. It includes a cabinet or compartment that supports or contains the equipment. It includes felt, wire, a belt, a clutch plate, a brake pad, a nonmetal part, and any part or tool subject to periodic replacement. I will not decide from Huntington Drive whether a heating element is that last item. Bring the invoice and the manual.

Except for the utility and contingent-income pieces, the covered equipment has to be at a described premises and owned, leased, or operated under your control. Covered property is property you own, or property in your care, custody, or control for which you are legally liable.

Depletion, deterioration, corrosion, erosion, or wear and tear are still excluded. If a breakdown occurs, the form pays the resulting loss or damage. The wear is yours. The breakdown that follows is the grant, if the rest of the form is met. I will not call a noisy compressor a breakdown because the dining room is full. I will not call it wear because someone hopes the claim goes away.

Fire is excluded on this form, including a fire that results in a breakdown, occurs at the same time, or ensues from a breakdown. Read that next to the property form. The property form can pay the fire that follows a mechanical breakdown, because fire is a specified cause of loss. The equipment-breakdown form I opened does not pay that fire. A hood fire stays on the property policy, and in the hood note, even if a motor was in the story. A motor that stops, and does not burn, stays here.

These are the coverages an owner means, and each one has to be on the declarations.

  1. Property damage. Direct damage to covered property at the premises. The value is the cost to repair, rebuild, or replace with property of the same kind, capacity, size, or quality, on the same site or another, whichever is less costly, or the cost you actually and necessarily spend, on the same comparison. If the damaged equipment is obsolete and useless to you, the form says it will not pay for it. If you do not repair or replace within 24 months after the breakdown, the form pays the smaller of what the repair or replacement would have cost, or actual cash value at the time of the breakdown. A compressor you never replace becomes a smaller conversation.
  2. Expediting expenses. With respect to that direct damage, the extra cost you necessarily incur to make temporary repairs, and to expedite the permanent repairs or the replacement. Thanksgiving week is when a shop charges the holiday rate and a part comes on a plane. The regular repair is property damage. The extra is this line, if the line is there. I will not call a holiday invoice two compressors.
  3. Business income and extra expense, or extra expense only. Actual loss of business income during the period of restoration, and the extra expense you necessarily incur to operate during that period. If the declarations say extra expense only, business income is not provided. Business income means net income, net profit or loss before income taxes, that would have been earned or incurred, plus continuing normal operating expenses, including payroll. Extra expense means the additional cost to operate during the period of restoration, over what you normally would have spent. The form looks at the experience of the business before the breakdown, and the probable experience without it.
  4. Spoilage damage. Spoilage to raw materials, property in process, or finished products, if they are in storage or in the course of being manufactured, you own them or are legally liable for them under a written contract, and the spoilage is due to the lack or excess of power, light, heat, steam, or refrigeration. The form also pays necessary expenses you incur to reduce that loss, to the extent they do not exceed the loss that otherwise would have been payable. Ice, if it actually saves food, is the shape of that sentence. A guess is not.
  5. Utility interruption. Only if you already have business income and extra expense, extra expense only, or spoilage. It extends that coverage to an interruption of utility service when three conditions are met. The interruption is the direct result of a breakdown to covered equipment owned, operated, or controlled by the local utility or distributor that directly supplies you. The equipment is used to supply electric power, communication, air conditioning, heating, gas, sewer, water, or steam. And the interruption lasts at least the consecutive period shown in the declarations. Once that waiting period is met, coverage starts at the beginning of the interruption, and the deductibles still apply. If the outage is shorter than the hours typed on the schedule, this extension does not apply. If this coverage is on the form, the definition says utility-owned equipment used only to supply your premises is not brought in as covered equipment under that paragraph. Separately, for covered equipment a utility owns, used only to supply your premises, not in your care, custody, or control, and for which you are legally liable, the property-damage limit is replaced by one dollar. I am not going to pretend those two sentences are simple. The reading I will use until I see your declarations: the form is not buying Edison a transformer. The grant, if you bought it, is the spoilage or the income that follows a breakdown at the utility.

The period of restoration on this edition begins at the time of the breakdown, or 24 hours before the insurer receives notice, whichever is later. It ends five consecutive days after the damaged property is repaired or replaced with reasonable speed and similar quality. If a number of days is shown for an extended period of restoration, that number replaces the five. If a time deductible is shown, the insurer is not liable for loss under that coverage during the stated period immediately after the breakdown. A day, if the deductible is in days, means 24 consecutive hours. I will not paste the property form’s 72 hours onto this one. I will not pretend the five days are a waiting period. The number on your declarations is the number. There may be no time deductible at all. There may be one that eats the holiday.

Deductibles are on the declarations, by coverage. The insurer pays the amount of covered loss that exceeds the deductible, up to the limit. If more than one covered equipment item is in one breakdown, only the highest deductible applies for each coverage. One breakdown includes an initial breakdown that causes other breakdowns, and breakdowns at one premises that show up at the same time from the same cause. A surge that takes the panel and the combi can be one occurrence. I will still want both invoices.

Five sublimits sit at $25,000 each, unless a higher limit or the word INCLUDED is shown. They are part of, not in addition to, the property-damage limit or the limit per breakdown. Ammonia contamination, which is spoilage of covered property contaminated by ammonia, including salvage expense. Consequential loss, a reduction in value of undamaged stock that becomes unmarketable because another part of the product was damaged. Data and media, the cost to research, replace, or restore them, including reprogramming. Hazardous substance, the additional cleanup cost over what would have been paid if no hazardous substance were involved. Ammonia is not a hazardous substance for that last limit. Water damage, including salvage expense. If the refrigerant in your coil is ammonia, the spoilage from that contamination has a cap unless the declarations raise it. I am not diagnosing the gas. The nameplate is the fact. The data sublimit is not a cyber policy. A board that fails, and takes the local data with it, is this paragraph, inside $25,000 unless the declarations say otherwise. A guest’s card, and a vendor who changed banks, stay on the register note.

Spoilage is valued on its own scale. Raw materials at replacement cost. Property in process at the replacement cost of the raw materials, the labor expended, and the proper proportion of overhead. Finished products at the selling price, as if no loss had occurred, less discounts you offered and expenses you otherwise would have had. A raw case of turkeys is not a finished pie. I will read the invoice before I pick the line. I will not price the walk-in at menu because Thursday is Thanksgiving.

A holiday rate is the extra. It is not a second compressor. The line has to be on the declarations.

The businessowners option is a smaller door

On BP 00 03 01 10, optional coverages apply if shown as applicable in the declarations. Equipment breakdown protection coverage pays for direct loss of or damage to covered property caused by or resulting from a mechanical breakdown or electrical failure to pressure, mechanical, or electrical machinery and equipment. That phrase does not mean a malfunction, including adjustment, alignment, calibration, cleaning, or modification. It does not mean leakage at a valve, fitting, shaft seal, gland packing, joint, or connection. It does not mean damage to a vacuum tube, gas tube, or brush, or the functioning of a safety or protective device.

For this optional coverage, three exclusions do not apply: electrical apparatus, steam apparatus, and the mechanical-breakdown exclusion. Wear and tear stays. The temperature-change exclusion, the one aimed at personal property, is not on the list of exclusions this option turns off. I will not tell you the food came back because the compressor did. The Institute’s guide treats spoilage as its own option. The causes it names are a mechanical breakdown of your refrigeration or humidity-control system, and a power outage from conditions beyond your control. That is a different grant from the repair of the machine. Your schedule is the one that says whether you bought it.

Business income on this businessowners form pays the actual loss from a necessary suspension during the period of restoration, caused by direct physical loss at the described premises from a covered cause of loss. The period begins 72 hours after the direct physical loss for business income, and immediately for extra expense. If the declarations amend that 72-hour period for the equipment-breakdown optional coverage, the form calls the amendment a time deductible, and it supersedes the time deductible that otherwise applies to business income. Each day means 24 consecutive hours. A compressor that is back Friday can still sit inside 72 hours, and the income line has nothing to pay for the seats you missed. Extra expense can still be there, because it begins immediately. I want both sets of invoices. I will not call the rush part the repair.

If the property policy is the California FAIR Plan, stop and read the note on the plan. I am not laying this businessowners form, or EB 00 20, on top of it.

The shutoff is a different file

I wrote the Edison version already. A public-safety shutoff is the utility deciding to de-energize. The food, the power-outage box on a spoilage endorsement, and the hours you are dark are that note. I am not reprinting the county’s discard table or the state’s. Follow the department that inspects you. Write the times down either way.

What I want kept straight, so the two notes do not collapse:

The compressor, the motor, the board
Equipment breakdown, if it is on the policy. A breakdown of covered equipment. Repair or replacement of the machine. The property forms I opened exclude the mechanical breakdown itself.
The food, when the machine fails
Spoilage, and only if that coverage is on the declarations. On EB 00 20 it has to result from the breakdown, and from a lack or excess of power, light, heat, steam, or refrigeration. On the spoilage option the Institute describes, and on the endorsement in the shutoff note, a breakdown of the refrigerating equipment can be one marked cause, and a power outage can be another. The shutoff note already says the box has to be checked, and that the endorsement pays the stock. It does not buy the compressor.
Edison turns the power off, and nothing in the machine is broken
The shutoff note. Utility interruption on EB 00 20 wants a breakdown of the utility’s covered equipment, and the interruption has to last at least the hours typed on the declarations. The same form excludes deliberate load-shedding by the supplying utility. A planned shutoff, with the equipment still intact, is the other file. I will not rename a shutoff to make it fit.
The seats you cannot sell
Business income, if you bought it, and then the time deductible that is actually on the schedule. Spoilage does not pay the income. I walked that split for a dark dining room in the shutoff note.
A fire
The property form, for the fire. The hood note, if it is the kitchen system. EB 00 20 excludes fire, including fire that ensues from a breakdown.

If both happen in the same week, they are still two files. A shutoff on Wednesday and a seized compressor on Friday do not become one story because the walk-in was warm both times.

What I want done before Wednesday

Thanksgiving is Thursday, November 26. This is the week. The code and the manuals I opened do not all use the same clock, and I am not going to mash them into one.

Health and Safety Code section 113996 says potentially hazardous food is held at or above 135°F, or at or below 41°F, except during preparation, cooking, cooling, a trip of less than 30 minutes, when time is the control, or as the rest of that section allows. The exceptions are real. Roasts at 130°F or above, after the cook the statute points at. A list that may be held at or below 45°F, including raw shell eggs and unshucked live molluscan shellfish. A serving line or a salad bar that may sit above 41°F and not above 45°F for no more than 12 hours in 24, if the unused food is discarded by the end of that period. A display case is not a serving line. Forty-one and 135 are the numbers I want a line cook to be able to say. The exceptions are on the page if you are using one.

Section 114157 requires a thermometer for each refrigeration unit, located to show the air temperature in the warmest part, and, except for vending machines, affixed so it is readily visible. Cold or hot holding equipment for potentially hazardous food needs at least one integral or permanently affixed temperature device that is easy to read, with the exceptions the section lists, including a salad bar. The scale is in increments no greater than 2°F. Section 114175 says equipment and utensils shall be kept clean, fully operative, and in good repair. The statute does not name your refrigeration company, and it does not require the notebook I want. The claim will.

The reach-in manual I opened is Beverage-Air 809-175A, revision D, dated March 10, 2026, for its hydrocarbon undercounter refrigerator-freezer models. The manual says the unit is for a commercial application, and that includes bars and restaurants. The cleaning schedule says condenser coil, quarterly. The condenser section says the coil should be inspected once a month and cleaned as required. It says failure to keep the coil clean will lead to poor performance, excessive power consumption, and compressor failure, and may result in loss of property, and that it may void the limited warranty. The warranty page says condensing coils must be cleaned at regular intervals, that failure to do so is subject to a voided warranty, and that Beverage-Air recommends a minimum monthly cleaning, because requirements vary. I am not averaging quarterly, monthly, and “as required” into a rule of my own. Read the manual for the machine you own. Unplug it before you open the grille. The same section says a dusty coil gets a brush and a vacuum, up and down, so the fins survive. A greasy coil gets coil cleaner, and that should be left to an experienced technician. The warranty I read says no claim can be made against it for spoilage of food, loss of sales, or consequential damages. That is the manufacturer’s warranty. It is not your insurance policy, and it is not a promise that a clean coil is covered either way.

The ice-machine manual I opened is True’s TCIM service manual, TEC_TM_198, revision A. It says the maintenance schedule is a guideline, and that more frequent maintenance may be required depending on water quality, environment, and local sanitation rules. In areas with airborne contaminants, and grease is the example, chemical cleaning of the air-cooled condenser should be done only by a service professional. The manual’s own reminder to clean the condenser air filter is every 3 months, and that line also says to check or clean the condenser coil. If your ice machine is not that family, I am not handing you True’s clock. The code sentence is the one that still applies: clean, operative, in good repair.

I did not open a manual that sets one interval for every walk-in compressor on a valley roof. The roof unit is the company that services it. If the box is already warm, loud, or icing up, I want that company on site before Wednesday. A combi that is slow to recover, and an HVAC unit that is already losing the dining room, are the same kind of call. I am not picking the vendor.

On EB 00 20, if covered equipment is found in, or exposed to, a dangerous condition, a representative of the insurer may suspend the breakdown insurance on that equipment by written notice, to your last known address or to the address where the equipment is. Coverage comes back only by an endorsement. A dirty coil is not automatically that letter. If the letter arrives, I want it the same day. The form also reimburses unused costs of a nonrefundable, nontransferable warranty or service contract that becomes void or unusable because of a breakdown. A service contract is not a condition I read on this form. If the spoilage page in the shutoff note marks a refrigeration maintenance agreement, that note already says what happens when you drop it.

The list for this week

  1. Write the temperatures down, starting this morning. Every cold box, including the lowboy and the bar cooler, and the hot holding if you are already running it. Time, air temperature, who looked. Again at close. Do it through Wednesday. The thermometer has to be there. The notebook is what I want when someone later asks how long the food was cold. Your memory on Thursday night is not the log.
  2. Look at the reach-in coil, with the power off. Use the manual for that machine. On the Beverage-Air manual I opened, a monthly look is in the condenser section, the schedule also says quarterly, and the warranty page recommends a minimum of monthly. Dust can be a brush, up and down. Grease is a technician. The same manual says not to clean a dirty condenser with a sharp object. It also says air filters are not recommended on that equipment, because they restrict the cooling air. If your manual says the opposite, follow yours.
  3. Put a card by the panel. The refrigeration company, the ice-machine company, HVAC, an electrician, and the person who works on the combi and the point-of-sale hardware. Their names, in your handwriting. I am not putting a vendor’s phone number on this page. Thursday service is a bad time to search for one. Section 114175 wants the equipment operative. It does not introduce you to the tech.
  4. If a box is already in trouble, call that company before Wednesday. Warm, noisy, iced over, or a condenser you cannot see through. The same for an ice machine that is behind, and for an HVAC unit that is not holding the room. A service call this week is maintenance. It is not, by itself, a claim. EB 00 20 says cleaning is not a breakdown.
  5. Photograph the declarations. I am looking for whether equipment breakdown is shown, as the businessowners optional coverage or as its own form. For EB 00 20, whether each of property damage, expediting, business income, spoilage, and utility interruption has a limit or the word INCLUDED. The deductible, and whether a time deductible is typed. The utility-interruption waiting period, in hours. The ammonia, data, and hazardous-substance numbers, if you are on the $25,000 defaults or something higher. On a businessowners policy, whether the 72 hours were amended for equipment breakdown. Whether spoilage is a separate endorsement, and which cause is marked. That last question is the shutoff note. A photo is enough to start. If the pages look fine, I will say so.
  6. If it dies on Thursday anyway, write the time and the temperature before the door stays open. Keep the door shut as much as you can. Photograph the thermometer, and photograph the food before it hits the trash. Call the name on the card. Then call me. The discard rules are the shutoff note, because they follow temperature and time. I am not reprinting them. If there is smoke, or the suppression system trips, stop and use the hood note. A fire is a different form.

On EB 00 20, the duties I read are prompt notice, with a description of the property, and as soon as possible how, when, and where. You have to allow a reasonable time to examine the property and the premises before repairs are undertaken or physical evidence of the breakdown is removed. You still have to protect the property from further damage. At their request, a signed, sworn proof of loss, within 60 days after the request. They supply the forms. Cooperate. Neglect, after the loss, to use all reasonable means to save the property is its own exclusion. A walk-in left standing open is a fact I do not want to explain later.

If the compressor is already loud, do not wait for a table. Text me today. If it is still running, pick the restaurant. Bring the declarations, and the name of whoever services the walk-in. I’ll buy.
