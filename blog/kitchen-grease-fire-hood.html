A current hood tag did not put the fire out

Last week a grill fire stayed on the grill, and the system in the hood never tripped. The tags were current. Here is what I want checked in a San Gabriel Valley kitchen before the holiday parties.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. Written for owners, not for insurance people.

The booking usually lands before the chips. A company dinner. A family that wants the back room. The fryer is going to stay hot longer than a Tuesday. The question I want answered before that night is a practical one. If the grease lights, does the hood put it out, and if you close, which page of the policy is supposed to care?

Last week the version I did not want was in Pocatello, not on Myrtle Avenue. The Idaho State Journal reported, on October 27, that crews were called to Oliver’s Restaurant at 130 S. Fifth Ave. at 7:12 a.m. Monday. Kim Stouse, a spokesperson for the Pocatello Fire Department, said the fire came from a buildup of grease, that nobody was hurt, and that it stayed on the grill. She also said the suppression system in the hood did not deploy. Staff tried an extinguisher. The crew put the fire out with a Class K extinguisher, the kind made for cooking oil. The tags on the hood system were up to date, she said, and the inspection records were current. An employee told the paper the restaurant stayed closed all day Monday and opened again around 1 p.m. Tuesday, after the fire department cleared it. The usual hours are 8 a.m. to 9 p.m., Monday through Friday.

I am not moving an Idaho kitchen into Monrovia. I am keeping one sentence from it. The tag was current. The system did not trip. That is the sentence I do not want to learn about your place from a fire captain. The first week of November is when I want it said out loud, because the party book is the stretch when the fryer works hardest, and a dark Monday is a room you may already have sold.

The restaurant number is older than I want

The detailed count of fires in eating and drinking places, the one that separates a restaurant from a warehouse, is NFPA’s February 2017 report. It uses 2010 through 2014. U.S. fire departments responded to an estimated average of 7,410 structure fires a year in eating and drinking establishments. Those fires caused an average of three civilian deaths, 110 civilian injuries, and $165 million in direct property damage a year. Cooking equipment was involved in 61 percent of the fires. Failure to clean was a factor in 22 percent. Deep fryers were involved in 21 percent, and ranges or cooktops in 14 percent. Sixty-eight percent of the fires never spread beyond the object where they started. That last figure is why “contained to the grill” is not a small story. It is the common shape. The hood is what is supposed to keep a grease fire in that shape. The dollars are the dollars of those years. I am not inflating them.

The newer count is wider than a dining room. The U.S. Fire Administration, on a page last reviewed February 14, 2025, estimates 33,300 cooking fires in nonresidential buildings in 2023, and $50.3 million in dollar loss. That is not a restaurant-only number. It is every nonresidential building where cooking was the cause. I am not going to average it with the NFPA report, and I am not going to pretend either figure is the San Gabriel Valley. The local fact I can hand you is the code on the wall, and the tag on your hood.

What the California code asks of the hood

The book in force is the 2025 California Fire Code, Title 24, Part 9, based on the 2024 International Fire Code. The code book says it took effect January 1, 2026. I am using the text of that book, and the same sections as posted for the 2025 code’s chapter on building services and for section 904.

Section 606.2 says a Type I hood goes at or above commercial cooking appliances, and domestic appliances used for commercial purposes, that produce grease vapors. The exceptions are narrow. Certain factory-built hoods listed to UL 710, and recirculating systems listed to UL 710B, skip specified mechanical-code sections when they are installed the way the mechanical code says. A listed down-draft can take the place of a hood over that appliance. An electric appliance does not need a Type I hood when an approved testing agency documents that the effluent contains 5 mg per cubic meter of grease or less, tested at 500 cubic feet per minute under UL 710B. A fryer, a griddle, a charbroiler, a wok, a range that throws grease is not that last exception. If a countertop unit is the thing you are unsure about, ask the fire code official before you decide the hood does not see it. I will not make that call from a menu.

Section 904.14 is the extinguishing system. Equipment that produces grease-laden vapors gets the Type I hood, under the California Mechanical Code, and an automatic system listed and labeled for the use. The code names three: a wet-chemical system complying with UL 300, a carbon dioxide system, and an automatic sprinkler system. It also says existing dry-chemical and wet-chemical systems shall comply with UL 300. The exception is a public school kitchen without deep-fat fryers, and only when a state-funded modernization is under the Division of the State Architect. A café on Huntington Drive is not that kitchen. A factory-built recirculating system tested to UL 710B, and listed, labeled, and installed under the mechanical code, is the other exception.

The manual pull is in section 904.14.1. It sits at or near a way out of the cooking area, not less than 10 feet and not more than 20 feet from the kitchen exhaust system, and not more than 48 inches or less than 42 inches above the floor. It has to identify the hazard. The pull takes no more than 40 pounds of force and no more than 14 inches of movement. An automatic sprinkler system does not have to have that manual pull. If a spot between 10 and 20 feet will not fit, the fire code official may accept another location at or near the way out, unobstructed and in view from it. A pull you cannot reach because the sheet pans live in front of it is not a location.

When the system trips, section 904.14.2 shuts off the fuel or the electrical power to the cooking equipment, and the reset is manual. You do not relight the line because the smoke looks gone. Oliver’s stayed closed until the fire department cleared it. The manual reset is why a kitchen can stay dark after a dump of chemical, even when the flames are out.

Service is section 904.14.5. The system is maintained under Title 19, Division 1, Chapter 5, of the California Code of Regulations, and under the three subsections that follow. It is serviced at least every six months, and again after it activates. A qualified person inspects it, and a certificate of inspection goes to the fire code official. Fusible links and automatic sprinkler heads are replaced annually. Frangible bulbs are the exception: the code does not require those to be replaced every year. Anything else is serviced or replaced the way the manufacturer says.

I am not going to mash that sentence with the national standard. NFPA’s Jonathan Hart, writing on April 27, 2021, about restaurant protection and NFPA 96, says the extinguishing system is maintained at least every six months, the parts that actuate it are tested, and fusible links of the metal-alloy type are replaced at that six-month service. California’s sentence on the link is annual, with the manufacturer’s instructions sitting beside it. If the code, the standard’s public overview, and the manual do not name the same month, I want the link changed on the sooner date, and I want the invoice to say which instruction was followed. I will not pick the looser reading for a kitchen I have not stood in.

The hood is on a different clock from the tank. Section 606.3.3.1 says the hood, the grease-removal devices, the fans, the ducts, and the other appurtenances are inspected on the schedule below, or on an interval the fire code official approves. A qualified person does the inspection. If the inspection finds an accumulation of grease, those parts are cleaned to ANSI/IKECA C10. The schedule is when someone looks. The cleaning is what the look finds. A tag from a visit that never opened the duct is not the sentence in the code. These are the rows in Table 606.3.3.1.

Every 3 months
High-volume. The code’s examples are 24-hour cooking, charbroiling, and wok cooking.
Every month
Solid fuel.
Every 12 months
Low-volume. The code’s examples are places of worship, seasonal businesses, and senior centers.
Every 6 months
Everything else.

A busy December does not, by itself, move a kitchen from the six-month row to the three-month row. The examples are the examples. What a busy December does is lay more grease in the duct between the inspections you already owe. If you charbroil, or you cook on a wok, you are already on the three-month row. If you roll a solid-fuel grill out for the parties, you are on the monthly row. If you added that station for the season, the next section is the one that applies, not a wish that the old tag still covers the new line.

The records have to name the person and the company. An inspection record describes the inspection and says when it happened. A cleaning record says who cleaned and when. They are filled out after the visit, and kept. When the hood or the duct is inspected, the tag shows the company’s name, address, and telephone number, and the date of service, where a person can see it. The old tag is covered or removed. Section 606.3.1 says the ventilation runs at the rate the hood requires, and that grease filters listed to UL 1046 are in place while the equipment under the hood is used. Pulling the filters because they roar is how a rush becomes a duct fire.

Move the equipment, and the system has to be looked at again. Section 904.14.5.1 says that where the cooking media changes, or the equipment is repositioned or replaced, the automatic system has to comply with sections 904.14 through 904.14.4. The fryer that slid eighteen inches for a holiday menu is a repositioning. Hart’s NFPA piece says the same thing from the design side: the system is laid out for the equipment it was laid out for, and changing that equipment without looking at the system can mean it does not extinguish. He also flags the caps on the discharge nozzles. They keep grease out of the nozzle, and they are meant to blow off when the system fires. Caps taken off for day-to-day cooking are a way to clog the nozzle that was supposed to open.

The portable extinguisher is separate, and it is state regulation, not a suggestion from a salesperson. Title 19, section 573, says a Class K extinguisher is provided where the hazard is vegetable or animal oil and fat. The travel distance is no more than 30 feet. A placard by the extinguisher says the fire protection system is activated before the extinguisher is used. A solid-fuel appliance with a firebox of 5 cubic feet or less gets at least one 1.6 gallon wet-chemical extinguisher listed for Class K, in the immediate vicinity. The Journal said the Pocatello crew finished that grill fire with a Class K. The placard is the order for your own people. Trip the system. Then the extinguisher.

Who walks in to look at the tag is not the same office in every city on this side of the valley. The Los Angeles County EMS Agency list, reference 404, revised January 1, 2026, names the incorporated cities where the county fire department provides fire and EMS. Duarte, Azusa, and Glendora are on it. Monrovia, Arcadia, Pasadena, and Sierra Madre are not. Rancho Cucamonga is not a Los Angeles County city, so it is not a question that list answers. The city pages are where I write down the department I use for each town. The code already lets that official approve a different inspection interval. The table is the start. The official’s interval, if you have one in writing, is the one you keep.

A current tag tells me someone was here. It does not tell me the nozzle will open.

The page that can argue with a fire claim

Damage from the fire, if fire is still covered when you reach the end of the form, is a property claim. The endorsement that can take that fire claim away is protective safeguards. The ISO wording I can point at in public is CP 04 11 09 17. A carrier can file its own form. The schedule on the policy that was issued is the one that counts.

On that ISO endorsement, the safeguards listed in the schedule are a condition of the insurance. You maintain the ones you control, in complete working order. You keep any automatic fire alarm, or any other automatic system listed in the schedule, actively engaged and in the on position. You notify the insurer if you know of a suspension or an impairment in a listed safeguard. The notice has a narrow pass. If part of an automatic sprinkler system, or part of an automatic commercial cooking exhaust and extinguishing system, is shut off because of breakage, leakage, freezing, or the opening of sprinkler heads, notice is not required if full protection is restored within 48 hours. A nozzle you have known was packed with grease since summer is not that sentence.

Then the exclusion. The form adds it to the causes-of-loss forms: the insurer will not pay for loss or damage caused by or resulting from fire if, prior to the fire, you failed to comply with any of those conditions. Read it as a fire exclusion with a maintenance condition in front of it. It is not a credit for owning a hood. It is a way for a fire to fall outside the policy.

The kitchen symbol on that form is P-5. ISO defines it as an automatic commercial cooking exhaust and extinguishing system installed on cooking appliances, with four components: a hood, a grease removal device, a duct system, and wet-chemical fire extinguishing equipment. The last noun matters. P-5, as this edition writes it, is a wet-chemical system. Carbon dioxide, or sprinklers over the line, may be on the schedule as P-9, which is whatever system the schedule describes in words, or they may not be on the schedule at all. I read the symbol and the description. I do not treat the brand on the service sticker as the symbol on the policy.

A court has read “complete working order” the hard way, and the limits of that case belong in the same conversation. FC&S, on January 11, 2024, walked through Scottsdale Insurance Co. v. Logansport Gaming, L.L.C., 556 Fed. Appx. 356, an unpublished Fifth Circuit opinion from 2014. The restaurant’s protective-safeguards endorsement required the extinguishers and the Ansul system to be kept in complete working order. The system was on a twice-a-year service. The last service before the fire was in August 2010. The kitchen fire was January 31, 2011. The system did not activate. The insured argued that reasonable care was enough. The court said the words did not say due diligence. It read “complete working order” to mean the system had to work on the day of the fire. The insured had already conceded that it did not. The court of appeals affirmed. FC&S notes that the opinion is unpublished and of limited precedential value, that the ambiguity argument was waived because it was not raised below, and that the result might have differed if the court had reached that argument. The piece was originally published March 10, 2014, and the page I read carries the January 11, 2024 update.

That case is not a California case, and it is not the 09 17 ISO form. I will not tell an Arcadia owner that a system which fails to trip is an automatic denial. I will not tell them the sentence is decorative either. Oliver’s is the other half of the same problem. The tags were current, and the system still did not deploy. A tag records a visit. Where this endorsement is attached, the form asks whether the listed system was maintained in complete working order, and whether you knew it was impaired and did not say so. Those are facts. The sticker is one of them.

The night you closed is a different line

The hood that took the fire is property. The seats you could not sell are business income, if that coverage is on the policy. FC&S walks through CP 00 30 10 12, the business income and extra expense form. The insurer pays the actual loss of business income from a necessary suspension of operations during the period of restoration. The suspension has to be caused by direct physical loss of or damage to property at the premises described in the declarations, from a covered cause of loss. For business income, that period begins 72 hours after the time of the direct physical loss. Extra expense begins immediately. FC&S calls the 72 hours a time deductible. On that form, the first three days of lost income are yours.

Set Oliver’s next to that sentence as a clock, not as a claim I handled. Closed Monday morning, open again Tuesday at 1. On the edition FC&S is describing, that lost Monday sits inside the 72 hours. If the fire is covered, the physical damage is the property claim. The empty room needs business income on the policy, and then it needs a waiting period shorter than the time you were actually closed, or the income line has nothing to pay. A later edition can move a word. A carrier’s form can type a different number of hours. The number on your schedule is the number. I walked this same form, for a dining room that went dark without a fire, in the note on Edison’s shutoffs.

A cook who is burned is not the property form. That injury is the workers’ comp note. A guest who is hurt on the way out is general liability, and it is a different conversation from the hood. I am not going to fold three policies into one story because they share a morning.

What I do before the parties are on the book

This is the order, while the line is still cold.

  1. Read both tags, not one. The suppression system is serviced at least every six months, and again after a discharge, with a certificate to the fire code official. The hood, the filters, the fan, and the duct are inspected on the row that matches the cooking: monthly for solid fuel, every three months for the high-volume examples, every six months for everything else, every twelve months for the low-volume examples, unless the fire official has approved another interval in writing. The tag shows the company, the address, the phone, and the date. If the date has passed, book the visit before the first buyout.
  2. Look where a tag cannot look for you. Filters in place, listed to UL 1046. Nozzle caps on, so grease is not sitting in the opening that is supposed to fire. The pull between 10 and 20 feet from the kitchen exhaust, 42 to 48 inches off the floor, unless the fire official accepted another spot, and nothing stored in front of it. Class K within 30 feet, and the placard that says the system comes before the extinguisher. If the oil changed, or a piece of equipment moved or was replaced under the hood, section 904.14.5.1 says the system has to comply again. Call the company that services it. Do not aim nozzles by eye.
  3. Tell the person who will be there early. A November hire is the one most likely to be alone with the fryer. The placard’s order is the whole lesson: activate the system, then use the Class K. Show them the pull on a quiet afternoon, not during the party.
  4. Then read the policy, before you need it. Is a protective-safeguards endorsement attached, ISO’s CP 04 11 or the carrier’s own version. Is the schedule marked P-5, or P-9 with words that actually describe the cooking system, and do those words match wet chemical, carbon dioxide, or sprinklers. If you learn the system is impaired, who gets the call the same day, and is the 48-hour pass only the one the form names: breakage, leakage, freezing, or opened sprinkler heads, with full protection back inside 48 hours. Is business income on the policy, and what waiting period is typed on it. If the declarations page is a mystery, text me the photo. That part is free. If the hood endorsements look fine, I will say so.
  5. If the system does trip, leave the fuel off. The reset is manual. The code wants service after activation, and a certificate to the fire official. Photograph the tags, the nozzles, and the damage before anyone starts wiping the line. Write down the time. Call me the same day. The hood and the lost seats are two files, and the second one is counted from the loss, not from the afternoon you remember to ask.

If you would rather do this over a plate of food than over a PDF, pick the restaurant. A photo of the hood tag and the suppression tag is enough to start. I’ll buy.
