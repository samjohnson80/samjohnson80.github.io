Note · November 17, 2026 · Monrovia

The sign on the door is not the guest’s claim.

A guest who says the meal made them sick, and a health department that tapes the door, are two files. General liability is the person. A food-contamination endorsement, if you bought one, is the cleanup, the food, the lost nights, and a small advertising number. A failed inspection is often neither.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. Written for owners, not for insurance people. I am not your health inspector, and I am not your lawyer.

It is the middle of November. Somebody calls in the morning and says they were up all night after dinner. Or the inspector is already in the kitchen, and the conversation has moved from a temperature to whether you are open tonight. Those feel like the same problem. They are not the same piece of paper. I would rather sort them while the dining room is still yours. Monrovia, Arcadia, Alhambra, Pasadena, the rest of the valley. The season does not care which city issued the permit.

What November actually is

The CDC’s norovirus facts page says norovirus is the leading cause of foodborne illness in the United States, and that it causes 58 percent of foodborne illnesses acquired in the United States. The same page says there are about 2,500 reported norovirus outbreaks in the United States each year, that those outbreaks occur throughout the year, and that they are most common from November to April. The chart on that page is older than this winter. It says reported norovirus outbreaks were highest between November and April during 2009 to 2017. I am not turning a chart of those years into a forecast for Huntington Drive. The sentence I am using is the one the page still prints: most common from November to April.

A different CDC page, Norovirus Outbreaks, dated April 22, 2026, uses the word differently, and I want both. It says norovirus is the leading cause of outbreaks from contaminated food in the United States, and that about 50 percent of all outbreaks of food-related illness are caused by norovirus. Most of those outbreaks occur in food service settings like restaurants. Infected food workers are frequently the source, often by touching ready-to-eat food, such as raw fruits and vegetables, with bare hands before serving it. Any food served raw or handled after it is cooked can get contaminated. The foods that page names are leafy greens, fresh fruits, and shellfish such as oysters. An outbreak, on that page, is two or more similar illnesses from a common exposure that is suspected or laboratory-confirmed to be norovirus. One angry phone call is not that definition.

The same page says the most commonly reported setting for norovirus outbreaks in the United States is healthcare, and that over half of all reported norovirus outbreaks occur in long-term care facilities. Cruise ships are the story people tell. The page says norovirus outbreaks on cruise ships account for about 1 percent of all reported norovirus outbreaks. I am not putting a cruise ship, or a hospital, on a café in Sierra Madre. I am keeping the food-service sentence, because that is the one with a dining room in it.

The CDC’s fact sheet for food workers says the same half, in its words: norovirus causes about half of all outbreaks of food-related illness, and it is the leading cause of foodborne illness outbreaks in the United States. Most reported norovirus outbreaks, that sheet says, are caused by people who touch or prepare food when they are sick, or sometimes by food that comes from contaminated water. It only takes a very small amount. People infected with norovirus can shed billions of particles. You are most contagious when you feel sick, and during the first few days after you feel better. The prevention page adds a longer tail. The virus can stay in your poop for two weeks or more after you feel better, and you can still spread it then. I am not going to turn “two weeks or more” into a house rule. The clearance rule, further down, is California’s, and it is not a number of days I get to pick.

One phone call is not the CDC’s outbreak. It can still be a claim. Those are different counts.

The guest is the liability file

If the guest is hurt, the form I can point at in public is ISO’s occurrence form, CG 00 01 04 13. On this edition, bodily injury means bodily injury, sickness, or disease sustained by a person, including death resulting from any of these at any time. Coverage A pays the sums the insured becomes legally obligated to pay as damages because of bodily injury or property damage to which the insurance applies. The company has the right and duty to defend a suit seeking those damages, and it has no duty to defend a suit the insurance does not apply to. An occurrence, on this edition, is an accident, including continuous or repeated exposure to substantially the same general harmful conditions. I am not deciding from Huntington Drive that a particular dinner was an accident. I am telling you which file the complaint lands in if the insurance applies.

Which aggregate the payment spends, what medical payments will and will not do, and why the plate you throw out is not the hospital bill, I already wrote for a guest who reacts to a food. That note is the peanut on the menu. An allergic reaction and a norovirus illness are different facts. The bodily-injury sentence is the same form. I am not teaching products-completed operations again, and I am not teaching the recall exclusion again. If you are about to dump a batch because you are afraid of it, read that note before you assume the general liability policy reimburses the food. The recall exclusion is the sentence. This note is the other half: the person, and then the sign on the door.

A cook who is sick is not the guest. On this edition, the workers’ compensation exclusion removes any obligation under a workers’ compensation, disability benefits, or unemployment compensation law, or any similar law. The employer’s liability exclusion removes bodily injury to an employee arising out of and in the course of employment, or of performing duties related to the business. The other policy is the one in the workers’ comp note. I am not moving a line cook onto the guest’s claim because they ate family meal.

The condition I want you to meet before you get generous is on the same form. No insured will, except at that insured’s own cost, voluntarily make a payment, assume any obligation, or incur any expense, other than for first aid, without the company’s consent. First aid is the exception I read. A promise to cover the urgent care is not a sentence I will call first aid. I am not your lawyer. Do not tell the guest the kitchen did it. Do not tell them the policy will pay. If you already said something, tell me the words. I would rather know them on Tuesday than read them in a demand on Friday.

The sign on the door

Los Angeles County Environmental Health is the inspector for a lot of this valley, and it is not the inspector for all of it. The county’s grading and posting page says the grade rules apply in unincorporated Los Angeles County and in cities that adopted County Ordinance No. 97-0071. Monrovia adopted it on June 1, 2004, effective July 1, 2004. Arcadia, Alhambra, Azusa, Duarte, Glendora, and San Gabriel are on that list too. Sierra Madre is marked No. Pasadena is not on the table. Pasadena’s own Environmental Health page says the city’s division is a separate agency from the county, and that a health permit issued by Los Angeles County Environmental Health is not valid in the City of Pasadena. If you are not sure which office walks your kitchen, the city pages name the one I use for each town.

Where the county grades you, the inspection starts at 100 points. Ninety to 100 is an A. Eighty to 89 is a B. Seventy to 79 is a C. A score under 70 is a score card, not a letter, and the card shows the number. The grade or score stays posted until the next routine inspection. A copy of the most recent report has to be available if the public asks. All food facilities that have a critical violation may be subject to closure regardless of the score. A facility that scores below 70 percent twice in twelve months is subject to closure and further legal action. When the inspector directs you closed, a notice of closure goes on the entrance door, states the reasons, and stays up until the inspector reopens you.

The county still posts closures. Its inspection-results page says you can view closures of active restaurants, food markets, and swimming pools from the past 100 days, and the closures page says it redirects to the Environmental Health online public portal. The same inspection-results page has a limit I do not want you to miss. Reports for restaurant and food-market inspections conducted on or after August 25, 2025, have to be requested through a California Public Records Act request to Environmental Health. A guest who cannot pull yesterday’s report off the website can still see the card in your window, and can still see a closure if you are on the 100-day list. The public line printed on the grading page, for someone reporting a restaurant without a permit or an unsanitary condition, is the Environmental Health hotline at (888) 700-9995.

Pasadena posts a different card. The city’s retail food field guide, the PDF linked from the environmental health page, describes a Pass at 85 to 100, a Conditional Pass at 75 to 84, and a hearing plus a follow-up if the score is below 75. A Closed placard is a permit suspension. The guide’s list of imminent health hazards includes vermin, a sewage overflow, no water or no hot water, a lack of power, no operable bathrooms, and a fire or other disaster. It also lists a suspension for non-compliance, and operating without a health permit. A Conditional Pass gets a follow-up, and the guide says a return to Pass takes a score of 95 or above with no major violations. The city’s environmental health office, on the page I linked, is at 1845 N. Fair Oaks Avenue.

A closure is a fact. It is not yet a coverage. The reason on the sign is the fact I need. Vermin, no hot water, and a night of sick guests are three different reasons. Only one of them is the shape of the endorsement in the next section.

Read the reason on the sign before you decide which policy you are holding.

The endorsement that pays the cleanup

The property form I can open does not want a virus. On BP 00 03 07 13, the businessowners coverage form, exclusion B.1.j. is Virus Or Bacteria. The insurer does not pay for loss or damage caused by or resulting from any virus, bacterium, or other microorganism that induces, or is capable of inducing, physical distress, illness, or disease. That exclusion supersedes any exclusion relating to pollutants, for a loss the virus exclusion reaches. A carrier can file its own form. A later edition can move a word. The forms list is the one that counts. On this edition, the building limit is not a norovirus limit.

The endorsement that turns that exclusion off, for its own coverage only, is BP 04 31 07 13, Food Contamination. I opened that edition. It modifies the businessowners coverage form. If your business at the described premises is ordered closed by the Board of Health or any other governmental authority as a result of the discovery or suspicion of food contamination, the form pays five things.

  1. Your expense to clean your equipment as required by the Board of Health or any other governmental authority.
  2. Your cost to replace the food which is, or is suspected to be, contaminated.
  3. Your expense for necessary medical tests or vaccinations for employees, including temporary or leased employees, who are potentially infected. It will not pay an expense that is otherwise covered under a workers’ compensation policy.
  4. The loss of business income you sustain because you had to suspend operations. Business income coverage begins 24 hours after you receive notice of closing.
  5. Additional advertising expenses you incur to restore your reputation.

Business income, for this endorsement, has the same meaning as in the business-income additional coverage of the coverage form. On the 07 13 edition I opened, that meaning is the net income, net profit or loss before income taxes, that would have been earned or incurred if no physical loss or damage had occurred, not including extra income from a boom caused by the loss itself, plus continuing normal operating expenses incurred, including payroll. I am borrowing the definition. I am not borrowing the rest of that additional coverage. The base business-income grant wants direct physical loss at the premises. This endorsement has its own trigger, the order to close, and its own wait, 24 hours. I will not paste a 72-hour wait, or an extended-period sentence, onto an endorsement that did not say them.

The limits are the part owners skip, because the words sound like a full closure policy. On this edition, the most the form pays for all loss under the first four items, including business income, is $10,000, unless a higher Food Contamination limit is indicated in the schedule. The most it pays for the advertising is $3,000, unless a higher Additional Advertising Expense limit is indicated in the schedule. Those are not two piles of $10,000. The cleanup, the food you replace, the tests, and the lost income share one number, unless the schedule types a higher one. The ad is a second, smaller number. A blank schedule is the $10,000 and the $3,000. I will not invent a higher number and call it yours.

The form will not pay any fines or penalties levied against you by the Board of Health or any other governmental authority as a result of the discovery or suspicion of food contamination. With respect to the coverage this endorsement provides, the virus-or-bacteria exclusion does not apply. That sentence is about this coverage. It is not a repeal of the exclusion for the rest of the property form.

Food contamination, in the definition this endorsement adds, means an outbreak of food poisoning or food-related illness of one or more persons arising out of tainted food you distributed or purchased, food improperly processed, stored, handled, or prepared in your operations, or food contaminated by virus or bacteria transmitted through one or more of your employees, including temporary or leased employees. Read “one or more” next to the CDC’s “two or more.” They are not the same count. A single sick guest can fit the endorsement’s definition and still not be a CDC outbreak. Neither count, by itself, writes a check. The payment sentence still wants an order to close, from the Board of Health or another governmental authority, as a result of the discovery or suspicion of that contamination.

Health and Safety Code section 113949.1 is one way that order can exist. When a local health officer is notified of an illness that can be transmitted by food in a food facility, or by an employee of a food facility, the officer informs the local enforcement agency. The officer or the agency, or both, notify the person in charge, investigate, and may, for reasonable cause, require the immediate restriction or exclusion of a food employee, the immediate closing of the food facility until the identified danger of disease outbreak has been addressed, and a medical evaluation of an employee. The illnesses named in that section include norovirus. An appeal of the closure is in writing within five days. I am not your lawyer on the appeal. I am telling you the closure, if it is about that danger, is the shape of the endorsement’s trigger. The notice on the door, with the reason, is the paper I want photographed the hour it goes up.

What usually stays with you

A guest who calls, and a door that stays open, is not this endorsement. There is no order to close. The complaint, if it becomes a claim, is the general liability file. Call me the same day anyway. Do not wait for a lawsuit to decide the call was real.

A closure for a reason that is not food contamination is not this endorsement either. The county says a critical violation can close you no matter the score. Pasadena’s guide lists vermin, sewage, no water, no hot water, no power, and no operable bathroom as imminent health hazards. Those can put a sign on the door. The endorsement I opened pays when the order is the result of the discovery or suspicion of food contamination, and it defines that as an outbreak of food poisoning or food-related illness. I will not stretch a cockroach, or a water heater, into that definition. Bring me the sign. If the reason is the water heater, we are in a different conversation, and it may be a short one.

The first 24 hours of business income, on this edition, are yours. The hours start when you receive the notice of closing, not when you decide the weekend is ruined.

The food you replace is your cost to replace it. I did not read a selling-price box on this endorsement. Menu price is a different form. The warm walk-in after Edison shuts the power off is a different form too. Spoilage, the power-outage box, and the hours you were dark are the note on the walk-in. A temperature change is not a sick guest, and a sick guest is not a public-safety shutoff. If both happen in the same week, they are still two files.

Fines and penalties are out, in the endorsement’s own words. A grade of B, or a score under 70, is not a fine and it is not a closure. It is a card. The card does not start BP 04 31. Lost regulars, a mean review, and the month after you reopen are not “additional advertising expenses” unless you actually incurred the advertising, and then only inside the advertising limit. I will not call hurt feelings a covered loss because the form did not.

If the endorsement is not on the declarations, the virus exclusion on the edition I opened is the property answer I can give you. I have not counted how many valley policies have BP 04 31, or a company form with a different number and a different limit. Some carriers file their own wording. The schedule that was issued is the schedule. If you are opening and you do not have a page yet, the liability line is one item on what a new restaurant actually needs. The food-contamination grant is a separate ask. I will not assume it came along because the quote said restaurant.

The line, before anyone is sick

The California rule and the CDC’s advice are not the same clock. I want you to know which sentence the inspector can mark, and which sentence I still want in a November kitchen.

Section 113733 defines an acute gastrointestinal illness as a short illness most often characterized by diarrhea, alone or with other gastrointestinal symptoms such as vomiting, fever, or abdominal cramps, or by vomiting together with either diarrhea or two other gastrointestinal symptoms such as fever or abdominal cramps. Article 3 of the Retail Food Code is the employee-health article. The owner with a food safety certificate, or the employee who has one, has to require food employees to report a diagnosis of norovirus, and of the other agents named in section 113949.2, to the person in charge. The employee has to make that report, and has to comply with the exclusion or the restriction.

Section 113950 is the split. The person in charge shall exclude a food employee from the food facility if the employee is diagnosed with an agent named in section 113949.1, and norovirus is one of those agents. The person in charge shall restrict a food employee who is suffering symptoms of an acute gastrointestinal illness from working with exposed food, clean equipment, utensils, and linens, and unwrapped single-service and single-use articles. Restrict is not the same verb as exclude. A symptomatic employee who has not been diagnosed is off the food and the clean dishes. A diagnosed employee is out of the facility, on the person-in-charge sentence I just read.

Section 113950.5 is who lets them back. The person in charge may remove a restriction when the employee reports that the symptoms are gone, if the employee states they no longer have any symptoms of an acute gastrointestinal illness. Only the local health officer or the local enforcement agency, or both, may remove an exclusion or a restriction related to a diagnosed illness on that list, and only after the local health officer provides a written clearance that the employee is no longer considered infectious. You do not write that clearance. Feeling better is not the clearance.

The CDC’s food-worker sheet says food workers should stay home when sick and for at least 48 hours after symptoms stop, and should tell a manager if they have symptoms or were recently sick. The prevention page says the same 48 hours, and it names restaurants. That is longer than the symptom-only California rule, which lets the person in charge lift a restriction when the employee says the symptoms have resolved. I would not use the shorter clock in November. For a diagnosis, California does not offer you a clock at all. The health officer writes it. Section 113949.5 is the call you do not skip. The person in charge notifies the local enforcement agency when notified that a food employee has been diagnosed with an agent on that list. The person in charge also notifies the agency when aware that two or more food employees are sick with acute gastrointestinal symptoms at the same time. A single guest on the phone is not that sentence. Two cooks down on the same Saturday is.

Hands. Section 113953.3 says employees wash with cleanser and warm water, vigorously rubbing the lathered hands and exposed arms for at least 10 to 15 seconds, then rinsing with clean running water and drying. Pay attention under the nails and between the fingers. The list includes immediately before food preparation, after the toilet room, after coughing or sneezing or eating, when switching from raw food to ready-to-eat food, and before initially putting on gloves for working with food. The CDC’s prevention page says soap and water for at least 20 seconds, and that hand sanitizer does not work well against norovirus. Sanitizer can be in addition to washing. It is not a substitute. Section 113953.4 says a hand antiseptic that meets the section goes only on hands that have already been cleaned the way 113953.3 describes. The inspector’s rub is 10 to 15 seconds. The CDC’s rub, for this virus, is 20. I want the longer one. A bottle at the host stand does not retire the sink.

If someone vomits or has diarrhea in the restaurant, the CDC prevention page says to wear rubber or disposable gloves, wipe the area with paper towels, and throw the towels in a plastic trash bag. Then disinfect. The food-worker sheet says a chlorine bleach solution of 1,000 to 5,000 ppm, which it writes as 5 to 25 tablespoons of household bleach at 5.25 percent per gallon of water, or another disinfectant registered with the EPA as effective against norovirus. The prevention page describes the same 1,000 to 5,000 ppm as 5 to 25 tablespoons of household bleach at 5 to 8 percent per gallon, says to leave the bleach on the area for at least 5 minutes, then clean the area again with soap and hot water, and wash your hands. I am not averaging 5.25 percent and 5 to 8 percent into a recipe of my own. Use the page you are following, and use a product whose label you can show the inspector. The food-worker sheet says food that might be contaminated should be thrown out. If the health department is the one telling you to throw it out, do what they say, and keep the invoices. If nobody has ordered the dump, leave the suspect food identifiable until you have talked to me. The property form wants damaged property set aside for examination when that is feasible.

Temperatures. Section 113996 says potentially hazardous food is held at or above 135°F, or at or below 41°F, except during preparation, cooking, cooling, a trip of less than 30 minutes, when time is the control, or as the rest of that section allows. The section has real exceptions. Roasts held at 130°F or above, after the cook the statute points at. A list of foods that may be held at or below 45°F, including raw shell eggs and unshucked live molluscan shellfish. A serving line or a salad bar that may sit above 41°F and not above 45°F for no more than 12 hours in 24, if the unused food is discarded by the end of that period. A display case is not a serving line. I am not going to pretend the only legal numbers in California are 41 and 135. Those are the numbers I want a line cook to be able to say, and the exceptions are on the page if you are using one.

The code requires the thermometer. It does not, in the section I read, require you to keep an hourly log of every pan. Section 114157 says each refrigeration unit has a thermometer, located to show the air temperature in the warmest part, and, except for vending machines, affixed so it is readily visible. Cold or hot holding equipment for potentially hazardous food is designed with at least one integral or permanently affixed temperature device that is easy to read, with exceptions the section lists, including a salad bar. The scale is in increments no greater than 2°F. Section 114159 says a food facility that holds potentially hazardous food keeps an accurate, easily readable metal probe thermometer on the premises, and a thin probe for thin food such as a patty or a fillet. A Fahrenheit-only device is accurate to plus or minus 2°F in the range you use. Calibrate it the way the manufacturer says, as necessary to keep it accurate. No glass stem, unless it is encased so it will not shatter.

If you are using time instead of temperature, the writing is required. Section 114000 says the food is marked with the time that is four hours after it left temperature control, and it is served or discarded within those four hours. Unmarked food, or food marked past four hours, is discarded. Written procedures stay in the facility and are available to the enforcement agency on request. A rice pan with no time on it is not a system. A log of the walk-in, with a time and a temperature, is what I want when someone later asks how long the food sat there. The statute did not make me say that. The claim will.

Shellfish, if you serve it. The CDC prevention page says to cook oysters and other shellfish thoroughly to an internal temperature of at least 145°F, and the same page says noroviruses can survive temperatures as high as 145°F and that quick steaming will not heat the food enough to kill them. I am not resolving those two sentences into a chef’s trick. I am telling you the page says a quick steam is not the kill step, and that food contaminated with norovirus may look, smell, and taste normal. Wash the produce. Keep raw shellfish away from ready-to-eat food. Throw out food that might contain the virus. That is their list, not a new one of mine.

When the phone rings

Write it down before you apologize your way into a fact. The dish. The time they ate. Who served them. What they say the symptoms are, and when the symptoms started. Their name and a phone number. Whether anyone else at the table, or anyone on your schedule, is sick. Do not diagnose them. Do not say the chicken did it. Do not offer to pay the doctor. First aid, if someone needs it in the moment, is the exception I read on the liability form. The form says you do not make a payment, or assume the bill, without the company’s consent, except at your own cost.

On CG 00 01 04 13, you must see to it that the company is notified as soon as practicable of an occurrence or an offense which may result in a claim. To the extent you can, the notice includes how, when, and where it took place, the names and addresses of injured people and witnesses, and the nature and location of the injury or damage. If a claim is made or a suit is brought, you immediately record the specifics and the date you received them, you notify the company as soon as practicable, and you see to it that the company gets written notice as soon as practicable. You send the papers. You cooperate. I am not waiting for a process server to decide the morning call “may result in a claim.” Call me the same day. I will get it to the company. If it turns out to be a bad oyster and a bad mood, I would rather have made the call.

If the sign goes up, the property form has its own clock. On BP 00 03 07 13, you give prompt notice of the loss or damage, including a description of the property. As soon as possible, you describe how, when, and where. You protect the property from further damage, and you keep a record of what that cost. If it is feasible, you set the damaged property aside so it can be examined. At their request, you inventory it. You send a signed, sworn proof of loss with the information they ask for, within 60 days after they request it. They supply the forms. Cooperate. Resume operations as quickly as possible. The food-contamination endorsement, if you have it, sits on top of that form. The 24 hours and the limits are still the endorsement’s. The notice is still prompt. A photo of the sign, a photo of what you threw out, and the invoices are the record. Your memory on Thursday is not.

Two employees sick at once, or a diagnosis of norovirus, is the call to the local enforcement agency under section 113949.5. Do that, and then call me. Follow the inspector if they tell you to close, to exclude someone, or to discard food. Keep the closure notice. The reason on it is the coverage question.

What I want on the table

  • The declarations page, and the forms list. I am looking for general liability, for whether products-completed operations is included or shut off, and for BP 04 31 or a company form that does the same job. The limit and the advertising limit are either in the schedule or they are the defaults I read. I will not guess from a certificate.
  • The grade card, or the Pasadena placard, and the last inspection if you have it. If the county inspected you on or after August 25, 2025, the report may be a records request. The card in the window is still the card.
