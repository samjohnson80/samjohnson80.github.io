The four thousand dollars is not the fall.

A letter about the restroom, the parking, or the website is asking for a different check than a guest who slipped. Here is what the two laws actually provide, what a general liability form is written to pay, and what a CASp inspection can change before the letter arrives.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. Written for owners, not for insurance people. I am not your lawyer, and I am not your accountant.

The letter usually comes out after we order, or it is already on the counter when I sit down. A van space that is a little short. A restroom door that does not give the chair room to turn. A step where the patio meets the sidewalk. Sometimes the person never came inside, and the letter is about the menu on the website. The owner wants to know which policy answers it. I would rather read the letter than guess.

I am not going to name a dining room from a complaint I have not read. The opinion I opened is a hotel, and I will tell you what it actually decided. The bills I checked from this legislative session did not become a new right to cure. The tools in the code are the ones you have.

Two laws, and two different remedies

Title III of the Americans with Disabilities Act applies to businesses that serve the public. Restaurants are on the Justice Department’s list. The duty ADA.gov states in plain words is an equal opportunity to get to the goods or services, including removing architectural barriers when that is readily achievable. Readily achievable means easy to accomplish without much difficulty or expense, and the department says that test looks at the size and resources of the business. A stair at the only door is the example the department uses for a barrier. There is no grandfather clause that lets an old room off. The Division of the State Architect says the ADA has contained no grandfathering, and that which edition of the standards applies depends on the age of the place and the date of the work.

A private lawsuit under that federal law is an order to change the place. It is not a damages check. The Ninth Circuit said so again on May 13, 2025, in In re Tsay JBR LLC. The plaintiff used a wheelchair. She alleged that barriers deterred her from entering a hotel in Burbank, the Ramada by Wyndham Burbank Airport. The district court found an ADA violation because the passenger loading zone had no access aisle, and it ordered the owner to paint one. The court of appeals quoted the statute: injunctive relief shall include an order to alter facilities so they are readily accessible. Private parties, the opinion says, can obtain only injunctive relief under the ADA. I am using a hotel, not a dining room. The shape of the federal remedy is the point. Paint the aisle. Rebuild the restroom. That order is not a guest’s medical bill.

California is why the letter also asks for money. Civil Code section 51 is the Unruh Civil Rights Act. People are entitled to full and equal accommodations in all business establishments of every kind, and disability is one of the characteristics the section names. Subdivision (f) says a violation of a right under the ADA is also a violation of that section. Section 52(a) is the money: for each and every offense, actual damages, and any amount a jury or a judge determines, up to three times the actual damage, but in no case less than four thousand dollars, plus attorney’s fees the court determines, in addition. The Disabled Persons Act is a smaller minimum. Section 54.3 says not less than one thousand dollars, and it says a person may not be held liable under both that section and section 52 for the same act. The letter pleaded under Unruh is reaching for the four thousand.

The Ninth Circuit opinion is also the recent word on what that four thousand is. The court called the statutory damages a penalty with punitive and deterrent purposes, and it held that in federal court the Seventh Amendment entitles the parties to a jury on a claim for those damages. It also said a construction-related violation does not automatically produce them. Section 55.56 allows statutory damages, under section 52 or section 54.3, in a construction-related claim only if a violation denied the plaintiff full and equal access on a particular occasion. That means the plaintiff personally encountered the violation, or was deterred. Difficulty, discomfort, or embarrassment because of the violation can be enough for an encounter. A person who never came, and who is claiming deterrence, has to have actually known of a violation that prevented or reasonably dissuaded them, and the violation would have denied access if they had come. The Burbank case left that factual question open. The opinion did not award the four thousand. It sent the question to a jury.

Count the visit, not every measurement on the same visit. Section 55.56(f) says statutory damages are assessed based on each particular occasion, not on the number of construction-related violations identified that day. If the place has distinct facilities that offer distinct services, damages may be assessed for each denial of access to a distinct facility. The State Architect’s page puts the same idea in shorter words: four thousand dollars may be assessed per occasion under section 55.56, not four thousand dollars per violation. A short van space and a restroom door, on one visit, are not automatically two minimums. A dining room and a separate bar can be argued as distinct facilities. I am not deciding that from a note. The attorney’s fees sit on top of whatever the damages are. The State Architect’s page says that if you are found liable, you pay the plaintiff’s attorney’s fees in addition to the statutory damages. Section 52(a) is where that “in addition” is written.

The federal claim, in the opinion I opened, was an order to paint an access aisle. The four thousand dollars was the state claim, and it was not decided yet.

The parking, the restroom, the patio, the website

The physical list is on ADA.gov’s page for businesses, under the 2010 Standards. Parking is sections 208 and 502. The route is section 206 and chapter 4. Dining surfaces are sections 226 and 902, and the department points restaurant and cafeteria routes to section 206.2.5. Sales and service counters are sections 227 and 904. Doors, ramps, and curb ramps have their own sections. I am not going to turn lunch into a code class, and I am not going to recite an inch I have not pulled off that page. The letter names the space, the path from that space to the door, the restroom, the counter where someone orders, and the table.

A patio table is still a dining surface if that is where you serve. If the tables sit in a parklet the city permitted, in the street, the permit is the other piece of paper, the way the lease is. I did not open a San Gabriel Valley parklet ordinance for this note, and I will not paste one from memory. Read the permit. It is the page that says who maintains what the city let you put in the curb lane.

Which standard applies is a date question, and the State Architect’s page is the one I am using. A place built or renovated before January 26, 1992, removes barriers if that is readily achievable, and a CASp can help make that determination. From that date through March 14, 2012, the page says the 1991 ADA Standards. On or after March 15, 2012, the 2010 Standards. A later change in the building code does not, by itself, make an old room newly illegal. The page says a CASp knows which version applies from the age of the building and the history of the improvements. Accessible features also have to be maintained. A grab bar that was right in 2014 and is loose on a Saturday is a different fact from a room that was never altered.

An old storefront on a Monrovia or Pasadena block is often the first category. The dining room can be from long before 1992, with a restroom redone in 2009 and a patio added later. Three eras, one address. That is why “we passed inspection when we opened” is not the whole file. The State Architect also says an architect or an engineer can evaluate the room, and that only a CASp can provide the services that offer qualified-defendant status. I will come back to what that status actually is.

The website is not that inspection. ADA.gov’s web guidance, dated March 18, 2022, still says the department has consistently taken the position that Title III applies to the goods, services, privileges, and activities a public accommodation offers, including those offered on the web. Food and drink establishments are on the list. The same page says the department does not, in that guidance, have a regulation setting out detailed standards, that businesses have flexibility in how they comply, and that they still have to make the goods and services they offer online accessible. A banner at the top says the page does not reflect requirements for state and local governments published in the Federal Register on April 24, 2024. A restaurant is not a city website. I am not going to hand you the government’s technical rule and call it yours.

What the guidance does list, as examples and not a complete code, is the stuff a guest actually hits. Contrast between the text and the background. A word, not color alone, when a field is required. Text alternatives on images. Captions on video. Labels, keyboard access, and error messages on a form. Headings that a screen reader can use. A way to move through the page without a mouse. It also says an automated checker or an overlay can help, and that a clean report does not necessarily mean the site is accessible. A widget on the menu page is not the end of the question. A scanned menu that a screen reader cannot read is the kind of barrier the guidance is describing, in the department’s examples of images without text alternatives and forms a person cannot complete.

The counting rules, the stay, and the reduced minimums below are written for construction-related accessibility standards. Section 55.52 defines those standards as the provisions for making new construction and existing facilities accessible, including the building code, the ADA, and the ADA accessibility guidelines. A website is not a parking stall. I will not tell you the ninety-day stay applies to a complaint that is only about the site. I also will not tell you a website claim stacks four thousand dollars per page, or that it is one check. The complaint is the document. Bring it.

What the general liability form is written to pay

Start with the form, not the certificate. On CG 00 01 04 13, the commercial general liability coverage form, Coverage A pays those sums the insured becomes legally obligated to pay as damages because of bodily injury or property damage to which the insurance applies. The company has the right and duty to defend a suit seeking those damages, and it has no duty to defend a suit seeking damages for bodily injury or property damage to which the insurance does not apply. The injury or damage has to be caused by an occurrence. An occurrence is an accident, including continuous or repeated exposure to substantially the same general harmful conditions. Bodily injury means bodily injury, sickness, or disease sustained by a person, including death resulting from any of these. Property damage means physical injury to tangible property, including loss of use of that property, or loss of use of tangible property that is not physically injured. Electronic data is not tangible property.

A guest who falls on the step is that sentence. Bodily injury, caused by an accident, on the premises. That is the claim this form is written for. Medical payments on the same form pay medical expenses for bodily injury caused by an accident on premises you own or rent, or on the ways next to them. A discrimination claim with no injury is not that payment.

The other half of the form is personal and advertising injury. The definition is a list: false arrest, detention, or imprisonment; malicious prosecution; wrongful eviction, wrongful entry, or invasion of the right of private occupancy; a publication that slanders, libels, or disparages; a publication that violates a right of privacy; the use of another’s advertising idea; or infringing a copyright, trade dress, or slogan in your advertisement. Disability discrimination is not on the list. The form’s definition of a suit is a civil proceeding in which damages because of bodily injury, property damage, or personal and advertising injury are alleged.

Put the letter next to those words. The four thousand dollars is statutory damages under section 52(a). The order to paint the aisle, or to rebuild the restroom, is the injunction the Ninth Circuit described. The restroom is not injured tangible property. The statutory check is not a sickness. IRMI’s glossary says a general liability policy does not cover a harassment or discrimination claim by a nonemployee, which is why third-party employment-practices coverage exists. Marsh, in an August 2017 briefing, said claims citing an inability to get into the store or the website, and the upgrades the law then requires, are often outside a general liability policy, and that a discrimination exclusion is common. I am citing that briefing for the description. I am not citing it as a count of the forms on Huntington Drive this fall. I have not made that count. If your policy has a discrimination exclusion, it is another sentence. The grant I just read already does not name this claim.

Supplementary payments are the expenses the company incurs defending a suit it defends. They are the company’s bills. They are not the plaintiff’s attorney’s fees, and they are not the contractor. The fees, if a court awards them, are the “in addition” in section 52(a). They ride with a claim. I will not tell you a policy pays them, or refuses them, until the claim itself is one the form covers.

If the complaint also says someone was hurt, that allegation is the one that can match Coverage A. A fall because of the step is a bodily-injury suit, and it can arrive in the same envelope as the access claims. Send me the complaint and the forms list. I will not decide the defense from the title of the letter. Coverage depends on the wording of the policy that is actually issued. Nothing in Marsh’s 2017 description is a promise about yours.

The form I can open pays damages because of a bodily injury or property damage. An order to alter the restroom is a different sentence.

Where a third-party endorsement might sit

The page people hope will catch a guest’s discrimination claim is not the slip-and-fall page. IRMI describes third-party employment-practices coverage as a claim by a nonemployee, typically a customer, a client, or a vendor, against your people. It says that coverage is most often its own insuring agreement, with a limit of its own, and that a substantial minority of insurers add it by endorsement, with the limit inside the limit for claims by employees. A dining room, a bar, a café counter: the exposure IRMI describes is contact with the public. The employment-practices note is where I wrote the rest of that form, including wage claims, the retention, and defense inside the limit. I am not teaching it again.

Marsh’s 2017 briefing calls third-party employment-practices coverage a more direct place to look for this kind of claim, and then it draws the line I do not want you to miss. The costs an owner incurs to bring a property or a website into compliance, and non-monetary or injunctive relief a court awards or a settlement includes, are not typically covered. Typically is Marsh’s word, in 2017. Your form is the word that counts. A wrongful-act definition written for how an employee treated a guest is not the same sentence as a restroom that was built with the wrong door. I will not tell you the endorsement rebuilds either one. I will tell you to look for the third-party agreement, or the endorsement, and to read whether injunctive relief and the cost of a modification are inside it or outside it. If the dec page has no employment-practices coverage at all, say so before you answer the letter.

The cure period you may have heard about did not pass

Owners have been told a bill was coming that would make a plaintiff send a letter and wait while the business fixes the barrier, and that statutory damages would go away if the fix was done in time. I opened the California Commission on Disability Access legislative report from the full commission meeting on October 14, 2026. The report says the 2025–26 session ended when the houses adjourned, that August 31 was the last day for each house to pass bills, and that September 30 was the last day for the Governor to sign or veto. Then it marks the access bills.

AB 649, the Small Business Right to Cure, is dead. It failed the fiscal-committee deadline. The report says it would have given small businesses a six-year protection period to fix most construction-related violations within 120 days, after a CASp inspection obtained after January 1, 2026. SB 84 is dead. It failed the policy-committee deadline. The report says it would have barred a statutory-damages claim against a small business until the business had a letter specifying each alleged violation and 120 days to correct it. AB 780, a similar notice-and-correct bill aimed at businesses with 50 or fewer employees, is dead. It failed the house-of-origin deadline. AB 2190, on website accessibility, is dead. The report says it would have created an affirmative defense for a business that published and updated a digital accessibility report, or showed good-faith efforts. AB 2607, expanding a state ADA small-business loan program, is also dead.

I am not telling you those were good bills or bad ones. I am telling you they are not law. Do not wait on a cure period this session did not pass. Do not assume a website report is now a defense because a bill described one. The procedures below are the ones already in the code.

What a CASp inspection can change

A Certified Access Specialist is a person certified by the state. The inspection is optional. Section 55.53(f) says nothing in that section or any other law requires an owner or a tenant to hire one, and that choosing not to hire one is not admissible to prove a lack of intent to comply. The State Architect’s page says the same thing, and it says that if someone solicits you by threatening a construction-related claim unless you hire them, you should complain to the division.

The legal status has a name. A qualified defendant, in section 55.52, is a defendant in a construction-related claim where the place had already been determined to meet applicable standards, or had been inspected by a CASp, before the defendant was served. You do not have to be the person who hired the specialist. A tenant can be a qualified defendant on a report the landlord obtained, if that status was in place before service, and if the claim against you is the construction-related one. The court does not have to find that the room in fact complies with every standard. It has to find the status.

There are two kinds of report, in section 55.53. If the specialist determines the inspected areas meet the standards, the report says so, and it has to say whether that opinion included readily achievable barrier removal. If corrections are needed, the report identifies them, says what correction is needed, and gives a schedule. The State Architect’s page says your qualified-defendant status does not carry over to public areas that were not inspected. Have the agreement define the public area, state that the report will be prepared under Civil Code sections 55.51 through 55.545, and state that a disability access inspection certificate will be provided. The specialist’s certification number and expiration date belong on that agreement. The division’s list is how you find one. A “Yes” in the column for doing inspections means that person is available to hire. The page also says a CASp at the city or county building department is a different use: information on the state standards, not the ADA, and usually the permit for new work, an addition, or an alteration. A plan-check stamp is not, by itself, the report on the dining room you already occupy. Section 55.53 does say that when a building inspector who is a certified specialist performs the inspection the section describes, that inspection is treated the same, legally, as a private one. Ask which one you are buying.

The stay is section 55.54. It is not automatic from a certificate in a drawer. A qualified defendant, and certain other defendants the section lists, may file an application for a stay and an early evaluation conference before or with the first response. If you file it before the response is due, the time to respond is tolled until the stay is lifted. The court shall then grant a 90-day stay of the construction-related claim, unless the plaintiff has temporary injunctive relief still in place, and it shall set a mandatory early evaluation conference no later than 70 days after the order and no earlier than 50 days after the request. The parties with authority to settle have to be there in person. Counsel walking in alone does not satisfy that, though the court can allow a person whose disability keeps them from attending to participate another way. You file the report with the court and serve it on the plaintiff at least 15 days before the conference. The report is confidential during the case, with a list of who may see it, including insurance representatives involved in evaluating and settling. If you ask, the order can direct a meeting at the premises no later than 30 days after the order, to look together at the conditions the complaint names. Take pictures and measurements before you correct anything. The notice in section 55.54 says to document the barrier before the work, in case a court needs to see what it was.

The stay pauses that claim. It does not erase it. You still have a lawsuit. Miss the application and you are on the ordinary clock, which the notice describes as 30 days after you receive the summons and complaint.

The reduced minimums are three different paths in section 55.56(g). They do not apply to an intentional violation. They do not limit actual damages, or treble actual damages. Section 55.56(h) says the section does not change the law of injunctions. A lower minimum is not a pass on fixing the barrier, and it is not a cap on fees.

The one-thousand-dollar minimum requires you to correct all of the construction-related violations that are the basis of the claim within 60 days of being served, and to fit one of the histories in the statute. Two of them are the CASp histories. The area was determined “CASp-inspected” or “meets applicable standards,” and to the best of your knowledge nothing was modified afterward that affected the claim. Or the report says “CASp determination pending” or “Inspected by a CASp,” and before the occasion the plaintiff describes, you had already taken reasonable steps to correct the violation, or you were in the process of correcting it within a reasonable time. A third history is new construction or an improvement that passed inspection by a local building official who is a certified specialist, with no relevant modification afterward. There is an older permit path, and it applies only to a claim filed before January 1, 2018. I am not going to spend the lunch on a claim that old. The State Architect’s shorter version, for a qualified defendant, is a minimum of one thousand dollars for each occasion if the violations that are the basis of the claim are corrected within 60 days of service. Read your facts against the subdivision, not against my summary. The words “within 60 days” do not help if the other half of the test is missing.

The two-thousand-dollar minimum does not require a CASp. You correct all of the construction-related violations that are the basis of the claim within 30 days of being served, and you are a small business that has employed 25 or fewer employees on average over the past three years, or over the life of the business if it is younger, and has average annual gross receipts of less than $3,500,000 over that same period. The statute says the employee count is shown by wage report forms and the receipts by federal or state income tax returns. It also says the Department of General Services shall adjust that dollar amount biannually for the California Consumer Price Index and post the adjusted amount. The number I can quote from the code is $3,500,000. If the department has posted a different figure, that posted figure is the one the sentence points to. I am not going to guess an inflation adjustment I did not find on the page.

The third path can wipe out the minimum statutory damages for violations a specialist already wrote down, and it runs from the inspection, not from the lawsuit. It is for a business that, as of the inspection, has employed 50 or fewer employees on average over the past three years, or over its life if shorter, shown by wage reports filed with the Employment Development Department. The report has to indicate “CASp determination pending” or “Inspected by a CASp.” The inspection has to predate the claim and any demand letter about that violation, and you were not on notice of the violation before the inspection. You correct, within 120 days of the inspection, the noted violations in the inspected area that are the basis of the claim. If a building permit is required and the work cannot reasonably be finished in 120 days, the statute gives you the protection only if you are in process, with at least an active permit for the repair the report noted and you had not finished, and the work is done within 180 days of the inspection. You get this protection once for each structure or area, unless you later alter it and obtain another inspection within 30 days of the building department’s final approval or the certificate of occupancy. For a business that qualifies, section 55.53 puts duties on the specialist: the report within 30 days, a notice to the State Architect within 10 days, and a notice posted within five feet of the public entrances on the day of the inspection, kept up until the corrections are done or 120 days have passed, whichever is earlier. The State Architect’s page says those steps are how the grace period is received. Put them in the agreement. Do not discover on day 40 that nobody filed the notice.

“Structure or area” means the interior, the exterior, or both. An interior-only report does not describe the lot. The patio guests use is a public area. The State Architect’s page says areas used only by employees do not have to be part of this inspection. The line, if the public never enters it, is a different question from the restroom door the guest uses.

You may post the disability access inspection certificate. Section 55.53 says you should not post it after the inspected site has been modified, or construction has started, in a way that may affect compliance. Keep the report. The notice the specialist gives you says to keep it, because the stay depends on having it to show the court.

If the plaintiff files these often

A high-frequency litigant, in Code of Civil Procedure section 425.55, includes a plaintiff who has filed 10 or more complaints alleging a construction-related accessibility violation in the 12 months immediately before the current one. The statute also reaches certain lawyers who have represented high-frequency-litigant plaintiffs, with a list of resolved cases that do not count, and it does not apply to a legal-aid lawyer in the circumstances the section describes. I am not using the Legislature’s 2015 findings about old filing counts as a current statistic. The number that operates today is the ten.

Section 425.50 says the complaint has to be verified, and it has to explain the barrier in plain language, how it denied access or deterred the person, and the date of each occasion. A high-frequency litigant’s complaint also has to say that it is filed by or for one, and, if the litigant is the plaintiff, the number of construction-related complaints that person filed in the prior 12 months, why the person was in the area, and the specific reason the person wanted access to the business. The caption has to say the action is subject to the supplemental fee in Government Code section 70616.5. That fee is one thousand dollars, paid to the clerk on behalf of the plaintiff, on top of the first-paper fee. It is not a payment to you. Failing to pay it has the same effect as failing to pay a filing fee.

The practical piece for the owner is the stay. Section 55.54(b) says the stay application is also available to a defendant who has been served with a construction-related complaint filed by a high-frequency litigant, even when the other qualifying histories are not yours. The notice served with the complaint tells you that you may request the meeting at the premises. Look at the caption. If the lines section 425.50 requires are missing, that is a question for the lawyer who answers the suit. It is not a defense I am declaring from a blog.

The advisory that has to come with the letter

Civil Code section 55.3 says an attorney shall provide a written advisory with each demand letter or complaint sent or served on a construction-related claim. The advisory is a set text. I am not going to reprint all of it. The parts I want an owner to see are these. The allegations do not mean you have to pay any money unless and until a court finds you liable. Receipt of the letter or the complaint and the advisory does not necessarily mean you will be found liable. You may contact your insurance provider. You may hire a lawyer, and you may also represent yourself. If you are a small business and you correct all of the construction-related violations that are the basis of the complaint within 30 days of being served, you may qualify for reduced damages. And if you are a commercial tenant, you may not be responsible for some or all of the premises, including common areas such as parking lots, because those areas may be the landlord’s. The advisory tells you to read the lease, talk to a lawyer, or contact the landlord.

The same advisory says that, except for limited circumstances, state law generally requires that a prelitigation demand letter from an attorney may not request or demand money or offer to accept money, and that the letter must include the lawyer’s State Bar number. Section 55.31 is that rule. A construction-related demand letter has to identify the barrier, how it interfered with access or deterred the person, and the date of each occasion. It may offer to talk about settlement. It shall not demand money. On the damages, it may only say that the owner or the tenant, or both, may be civilly liable for actual and statutory damages for a violation of a construction-related accessibility requirement. An attorney shall not issue a “demand for money” as the section defines it. A violation is cause for discipline. The lawyer can still discuss a number after you ask, or after there is an agreement to repair. The section does not apply the money prohibition to a claim involving physical injury and resulting special damages, and that demand still has to meet the identification rules. Section 55.3(c) says the advisory rules apply to a demand or a complaint made by an attorney, and that the section does not require anyone to send a demand letter before suing.

So a construction-related letter that arrives with a dollar demand, and without the advisory, is not the letter the statute describes. The advisory tells you how to send a copy to the State Bar. I am not your discipline counsel, and I am not telling you the suit goes away because the letter was sloppy. A complaint can still be filed without a prior demand. These rules are the construction-related ones. I have not read them onto a website letter. If the letter is about the building, keep the envelope, the advisory, and the date you were served. The 30 days and the 60 days run from service of the complaint, not from the day you first felt angry about it.

The lease divides the work. It does not erase the lawsuit.

The State Architect’s page answers the landlord question directly. Either the landlord or the tenant, or both, can be sued. The page says the ADA puts the obligation to remove barriers, provide auxiliary aids, and maintain accessible features on both, and that the lease may assign who handles which area, while both remain legally responsible. I am using the page’s sentence. I am not adding a rule about your particular lease.

Civil Code section 1938 is the California piece on the paper itself. On every commercial lease or rental agreement executed on or after January 1, 2017, the owner or lessor shall state whether the premises have been inspected by a CASp. If they have, and nothing relevant has been altered since, the owner provides the report before the lease is signed, and the tenant agrees to keep it confidential except as needed to do repairs the tenant has agreed to make. Repairs necessary to correct violations noted in a CASp report are presumed to be the owner’s or lessor’s responsibility, unless the two of you agree otherwise. The tenant gets a chance to review the report before signing. If it was not provided at least 48 hours before execution, the tenant may rescind, based on what is in the report, for 72 hours after execution. If there is no disability access inspection certificate, the lease has to include a statement the statute sets out: a CASp can inspect, state law does not require the inspection, the owner may not prohibit the tenant from getting one for the occupancy, and the parties shall agree on the time, the manner, the fee, and the cost of repairs to correct violations inside the premises.

Read that against the patio, the restroom, and the lot before you assume the letter is entirely yours, or entirely the landlord’s. A presumption in section 1938 is about violations noted in a CASp report, and it can be changed by the lease. The ADA point on the State Architect’s page is that the person who did not draw the short straw can still be in the caption. The advisory in section 55.3 tells a commercial tenant to look at this before paying anybody. The new-restaurant note is where I said to read the lease before you buy the policy. This is the page of the lease I would pull out at the table.

The credit, and the deduction beside it

Two federal tax rules can help pay for the work. They are not a refund I can promise, and they are not a reason to skip the accountant. The IRS page, reviewed as of June 30, 2026, describes both. ADA.gov describes them in the same amounts.

The disabled access credit is Internal Revenue Code section 44. For an eligible small business that elects it, the credit is 50 percent of the eligible access expenditures for the year that exceed $250 but do not exceed $10,250. Half of $10,000 is $5,000, which is the top ADA.gov states. Eligible access expenditures include removing architectural, communication, physical, or transportation barriers, and certain interpreters, readers, and equipment. They have to be reasonable, and they have to meet the standards the Secretary adopts. The IRS calls the credit nonrefundable. It reduces tax. It is not a check written because you spent the money. You can claim it each year you have eligible expenses. The IRS page points you to Form 8826.

An eligible small business, for the preceding year, had gross receipts that did not exceed $1,000,000, or, if the receipts were higher, employed not more than 30 full-time employees. Full-time, in the section, means at least 30 hours a week for 20 or more calendar weeks in the year. Members of the same controlled group are treated as one person. If you have a second company, ask whether the receipts and the employees are counted together before you assume the credit is open. Barrier-removal amounts in connection with a facility first placed in service after November 5, 1990, are not eligible access expenditures under section 44(c)(4). ADA.gov says the incentives are not for the cost of new construction. A ramp on a storefront that has been standing for years is the conversation. A building you are putting up from a hole in the ground is a different one.

The deduction is section 190. A taxpayer may elect to deduct qualified architectural and transportation barrier removal expenses, up to $15,000 in the year, instead of capitalizing them. The IRS says the deduction is available to businesses of any size, for items that normally must be capitalized, and that you claim it as a separate expense. The expenses are for making a facility or a public transportation vehicle that you own or lease, and use in the business, more accessible to people with disabilities and to elderly people, and the result has to meet the standards the Secretary adopts. ADA.gov says businesses of all sizes can use it for barriers in facilities or vehicles.

The two can be used in the same year if the spending qualifies under both. The IRS and ADA.gov say the deduction is then the difference between the total expenditures and the amount of the credit claimed. Section 44 also says you do not get a second deduction or credit for the amount of the credit itself, and you do not add that amount to basis. I am not going to do the return at the table. Bring the invoices to the person who signs it. A credit you cannot document to the standards is not a credit.

What I would do this week

  1. Read the lease and, if the tables are in the street, the permit. Who maintains the lot, the path, the restroom, and the patio. Whether a CASp report was ever attached, and whether section 1938’s statement is on the form. The presumption that the owner corrects what a CASp report noted can be changed by what you signed. Both of you can still be named.
  2. If you have no report, hire the inspection before a letter, not after you have invented a deadline. Use the State Architect’s list. The agreement should say the report is the one the construction-related statute describes, and that you will get the certificate, if you want the qualified-defendant status. Include the public areas, the patio included. Ask for the door notice, the filing with the division, and the report within 30 days if you want the 120-day protection and you fit the 50-employee test. Keep the report where you can find it on a Friday.
  3. If the complaint is already served, calendar it, and do not “just fix it” with no record. Look for the advisory. Look at the caption for the high-frequency fee. Photograph and measure before the work. The 30-day path and the 60-day path are available only if you also fit the rest of the test, and they do not apply to an intentional violation. The application for the stay has to be filed. A certificate in a folder does not file it. Call a lawyer who does this work. Then send me the complaint and the dec page.
  4. Treat a construction-related demand that asks for a number, before any suit, as the letter section 55.31 says an attorney shall not send. Keep it. The statute does not make the lawsuit impossible. It does tell you the letter is not supposed to arrive as a bill.
  5. Look at the website as its own list. The menu, the order form, the reservation widget, contrast, text on the pictures, a way through the page without a mouse. The 2022 guidance is the Justice Department’s position, and the department says a clean automated report is not proof. The CASp inspection does not answer this page. The website bill from this session did not become a defense.
  6. Then the policy, in this order. General liability, for a guest who was actually hurt. The third-party employment-practices agreement, if you have one, for a discrimination claim by a guest, and specifically whether an injunction or the cost of the modification is outside it. Neither page is a promise that the remodel is paid. The accountant, not me, runs section 44 and section 190 against the invoices.

If you would rather do this over a plate than over a statute, pick the restaurant. Bring the lease. Bring the dec page. If a letter is already on the counter, bring that too. I’ll buy.
