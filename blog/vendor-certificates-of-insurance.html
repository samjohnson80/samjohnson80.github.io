The certificate is not the coverage.

The hood cleaner, the crew opening a wall, the holiday-light installer, and the band all send a PDF before they work. An ACORD 25 confers no rights. Here is the endorsement, the dates, and which California contractors actually have to carry workers’ comp today.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. Written for owners, not for insurance people. I am not your lawyer.

The PDF usually arrives before the truck does. Certificate of liability insurance. Your restaurant’s name in a box at the bottom. A million dollars on a line. Somebody typed “certificate holder is additional insured” in the remarks. The Friday feels handled. The cleaner is on the roof Saturday. The band loads in at four. The contractor said the wall can come out in the slow week between the holidays.

I want the paper read before any of them is in the building. If the PDF is the one your landlord is waiting on, that reading is the lease note. This one runs the other direction. You are the one asking. The tag on the hood, and what the system has to do, is the hood note. This is the company that cleans it.

The words on the form

The copy I opened is an ACORD 25 (2025/12). The insured on that particular sheet is a title company, and the coverage on it is errors and omissions. I am using it for the sentences printed on the form. Those sentences do not change because the named insured is a hood company in El Monte.

Across the top: “THIS CERTIFICATE IS ISSUED AS A MATTER OF INFORMATION ONLY AND CONFERS NO RIGHTS UPON THE CERTIFICATE HOLDER. THIS CERTIFICATE DOES NOT AFFIRMATIVELY OR NEGATIVELY AMEND, EXTEND OR ALTER THE COVERAGE AFFORDED BY THE POLICIES BELOW. THIS CERTIFICATE OF INSURANCE DOES NOT CONSTITUTE A CONTRACT BETWEEN THE ISSUING INSURER(S), AUTHORIZED REPRESENTATIVE OR PRODUCER, AND THE CERTIFICATE HOLDER.”

Then the sentence I want on the table when someone says the box was checked. “IMPORTANT: If the certificate holder is an ADDITIONAL INSURED, the policy(ies) must have ADDITIONAL INSURED provisions or be endorsed. If SUBROGATION IS WAIVED, subject to the terms and conditions of the policy, certain policies may require an endorsement. A statement on this certificate does not confer rights to the certificate holder in lieu of such endorsement(s).”

Under that, the form certifies that the policies listed have been issued to the insured named above for the policy period indicated. Notwithstanding any requirement, term, or condition of any contract the certificate may pertain to, the insurance is subject to all the terms, exclusions, and conditions of those policies. Limits shown may have been reduced by paid claims. On the same sheet, a further line says limits shown are inclusive of amounts requested by the certificate holder and may not reflect policy limit amounts in excess of those requested, and it marks that line as not applicable in Wyoming. The cancellation line says that if a policy is cancelled before its expiration, notice will be delivered in accordance with the policy provisions. That is the policy’s notice rule. It is not a letter with your name on it.

ACORD’s own certificate FAQ says the same thing in prose. A certificate is not an insurance policy. It does not provide, endorse, amend, extend, or alter the terms of a policy. Only an endorsement, a rider, or an amendment can change coverage. A reference on the certificate to a contract between the client and a third party does not provide coverage. ACORD also says the forms, as required by regulation, are issued as a matter of information only, and that an outdated edition is no longer checked for whether it stays compliant. If the PDF looks like an old form, ask for a current one. The current one is still not the policy.

The certificate holder box is who the PDF was addressed to. It is not a status on the policy.

The endorsement is the page that adds you

Additional insured means the vendor’s liability policy, for the slice of it the endorsement describes, treats you as an insured. If a guest is hurt by their work, or they damage something the policy actually covers, you can ask their insurer to defend you and to pay. Whether that insurer says yes depends on the endorsement, the facts, and the contract. It does not depend on a sentence in the description box. The form I just quoted says a statement on the certificate does not confer rights in lieu of the endorsement.

Ask for the endorsement, and read your legal name on it. Some endorsements schedule one restaurant and one address. Some add anyone the named insured agreed in a written contract to add, if that contract was signed before the loss. A blank schedule, or the name of the landlord from their last job, is a page I want fixed before they climb. I am not going to assign one form number to every vendor in the valley. A carrier can file its own wording. The lessor endorsement I walked for a landlord, and the way a certificate can repeat a lease without performing it, is the lease note. I am not walking that page again.

Read how far the page goes in time. A band that is gone at midnight, and a wall that leaks in March, are different jobs. If the endorsement stops when the work is finished, the night of the party and the month after the remodel are not the same grant. I want the page that matches the work. I also want the waiver, if the agreement asks for one, on an endorsement. The certificate’s own sentence says a waiver of subrogation may require one, and that a statement on the certificate does not take the place of it. Primary and noncontributory is the same kind of ask. Both of those, on the landlord’s side, are already in the lease note.

Dates, the name, and the limit

The policy period is two dates on the row. The day they are in your building has to sit inside those dates. A certificate from the spring, for a policy that expired in October, describes a policy that is over. It does not describe the Saturday they are on the roof.

The named insured has to be the company on the invoice and on the side of the truck. A certificate for a different LLC, or for the owner’s other business, is a different insured. I match the name before I match the limit.

The limit is a number someone typed. The form says the limits shown may have been reduced by paid claims, and on the 2025/12 copy I opened they may be only the amount the certificate holder asked to see. A million in the box is not a promise that a million is left, and it is not a promise that the policy’s exclusions leave the claim inside. The contract you signed may ask for a number. The policy pays what the policy pays. I will not turn a typed limit into a price, and I will not tell you a number that “everyone” carries. Bring the page.

The person who gets hurt on your roof

General liability is the guest, and the damage to property that is not their own work. Workers’ compensation is the injury to an employee, and it is the employer’s duty. Labor Code section 3700 says every employer except the state shall secure the payment of compensation, by insurance from an insurer authorized to write it in this state, or by a certificate of consent to self-insure from the Director of Industrial Relations. The workers’ comp row on the ACORD 25 is the producer’s statement that a policy was issued to the named insured for that period. It does not add your restaurant to their workers’ comp policy. Their employees are their claim, when they are in fact their employees, and when the policy is in force.

The problem is the job that required a contractor’s license, done by someone who does not hold a valid one. Business and Professions Code section 7026 defines a contractor, for that chapter, as a person who undertakes, offers to undertake, bids, or does, by himself or herself or through others, construct, alter, repair, add to, subtract from, improve, move, wreck, or demolish any building or other structure, or any part of that work. “Contractor” includes a subcontractor and a specialty contractor. A remodel is that sentence. A band is not. A rental company dropping tables is not. Routine hood cleaning is a service I am not turning into a license from this desk. The narrow exemption in section 7048, as amended effective January 1, 2026, is a job under one thousand dollars for labor, materials, and all other items, of a casual, minor, or inconsequential nature, that does not require a building permit. It does not cover a larger job split into pieces under one thousand dollars to get around the chapter. It does not cover a person who advertises as a contractor, or who employs another person to do or help with the work.

Labor Code section 2750.5 puts a rebuttable presumption on a worker performing services for which that license is required, or performing those services for a person who is required to have the license. The presumption is that the worker is an employee rather than an independent contractor. The last condition is the one I care about on a remodel. In addition to the other factors in the section, any person performing any function or activity for which a license is required under that contractors’ chapter shall hold a valid contractor’s license as a condition of having independent contractor status. For workers’ compensation, the section says the presumption supplements the existing definitions and is not intended to lessen the coverage of employees.

The Court of Appeal stated the consequence in Cedillo v. Workers’ Compensation Appeals Board, a published opinion filed February 14, 2003. For workers’ compensation purposes, under section 2750.5, the hirer of a contractor for a job requiring a license is the statutory employer of the unlicensed contractor. The hirer is also the statutory employer of the workers that unlicensed contractor employs. The opinion says the presumption is conclusive once the license is missing. Read the holding before you borrow it for a house. The injured worker in that case had worked about fourteen hours on a homeowner’s roof. The court agreed he was not the homeowner’s employee. The exclusion it applied is the one now in Labor Code section 3351(d) and section 3352(a)(8): a person employed by the owner or occupant of a residential dwelling, whose duties are incidental to the dwelling, or personal and not in the course of the owner’s trade, business, profession, or occupation, and whose work for that employer in the 90 days before the injury was for less than 52 hours, or for wages of not more than one hundred dollars. A dining room is the trade. I am not moving a house exclusion onto Huntington Drive.

A license that has been pulled for lack of coverage is the fact I want caught before the crew starts, because unlicensed work is the 2750.5 question. Business and Professions Code section 7125.2 says the failure of a licensee to obtain or maintain workers’ compensation, if required under that chapter, results in the automatic suspension of the license by operation of law. The suspension is effective on the earlier of the date the coverage lapses, or the date the coverage is required to be obtained. The Contractors State License Board’s workers’ compensation page says any work performed while the license is suspended is considered unlicensed. The same page says that once the contractor employs anyone in a manner subject to California workers’ compensation law, an exemption on file is no longer valid, and proof of coverage has to reach the board’s headquarters within 90 days of the hire. I am not going to pick, from a blog, which of those clocks a judge would use if a helper is hurt on day ten. I am going to tell you to count the people who got out of the truck. An exemption is a statement that they had no employees when they signed it.

The board’s page on hiring a contractor tells the person doing the hiring that if a worker is injured on the property and the contractor does not have insurance, you could be financially liable for the injuries and the rehabilitation. That page then talks about a homeowner’s policy, which may or may not cover those costs. A restaurant does not buy a homeowner’s policy for the dining room. I am not pasting that sentence onto your businessowners form. The statute I am using for a commercial job that needed a license, done without a valid one, is 2750.5, and the case I opened is Cedillo, with the house exclusion left on the house.

A contractor who holds a valid license, and whose workers’ compensation is in force, is the employer of that contractor’s own crew. I am not folding your cook into their policy, and I am not folding their helper into yours, because they shared a morning. A band, a rental company, and a hood cleaner who are not doing the work in section 7026 are outside the license sentence. If they have employees, section 3700 is still their duty. If the person on the ladder is actually your employee, section 3700 is yours, and the payroll question is the audit note. I am not redoing the audit. The certificate does not decide which of those is true. The facts do.

What January 1, 2026 actually changed

You will hear, this month, that every licensed contractor in California has had to carry workers’ compensation since New Year’s Day. That is what Senate Bill 216 would have done. It was chaptered as Chapter 978 of the Statutes of 2022, approved September 30, 2022. Section 1 kept a version of Business and Professions Code section 7125 in effect only until January 1, 2026. Section 2 added the next version, and said that version would become operative on January 1, 2026. In that next version, the board would require a current Certificate of Workers’ Compensation Insurance, or a Certification of Self-Insurance, as a condition of issuing, reinstating, reactivating, renewing, or keeping a license. The only exception written into that version was an applicant or licensee organized as a joint venture under section 7029, with no employees, who files the board’s statement. There was no general “I have no employees” exemption in the version that was supposed to turn on this year.

Senate Bill 1455, chaptered September 22, 2024, as Chapter 485, moved both dates. The Legislative Counsel’s digest says the provisions that were to end on January 1, 2026 are extended until January 1, 2028, and that the all-classification requirement that was to become operative on January 1, 2026 is instead operative on January 1, 2028. Section 12 of the bill is the version in effect now. The board’s posting of section 7125 matches it, and the heading on that posting says the section is repealed effective January 1, 2028.

Here is the rule I can read today, November 27, 2026. Subdivision (a) still requires the certificate of insurance or the certification of self-insurance on file at all times, except as subdivision (b) provides. Subdivision (b) says the section does not apply to an applicant or licensee who meets both of these conditions. First, the person has no employees, and files the board’s statement, before issuance, reinstatement, reactivation, or continued maintenance of the license, certifying that the person does not employ anyone in any manner so as to become subject to California workers’ compensation law, or is not otherwise required to provide coverage. Second, the person does not hold a C-8 license, a C-20 license, a C-22 license, a C-39 license, or a D-49 license, which is a subcategory of C-61. The board’s workers’ compensation page names those classifications in words: C-8 concrete, C-20 warm-air heating, ventilating and air-conditioning, C-22 asbestos abatement, C-39 roofing, and C-61/D-49 tree service. Those five have to carry workers’ compensation, or have a certification of self-insurance on file, whether or not they have employees. A licensee who does not hold one of those five, who truly has no employees, and who filed the exemption, still does not, under the section now in effect. Subdivision (c) is a separate exception for a joint venture with no employees that files the statement. It does not repeat the five-classification bar. Subdivision (i) says this section remains in effect only until January 1, 2028, and as of that date is repealed, unless a later statute enacted before then deletes or extends the date.

Section 13 of SB 1455 is the version that becomes operative on January 1, 2028. The general requirement is the same. The exception in its subdivision (b) is only the joint venture with no employees that files the statement. A sole owner with an empty payroll does not have that exception in the 2028 text. An inactive license, during the inactive period, still does not have to file the certificate. Between now and that date, the Legislature can move it again. I am reading the bills that are chaptered. I am not predicting the next one.

So the timely sentence is the opposite of the rumor. January 1, 2026, did not put workers’ compensation on every CSLB license. It put the universal date on a later statute, and that statute says January 1, 2028. Today, the five classifications above carry it even with no employees. Anyone with employees carries it under section 7125(a) and under Labor Code section 3700. A filed exemption is not a policy, and it is not a headcount of the crew in your parking lot.

The hold-harmless paragraph, in plain words

Civil Code section 2772 defines indemnity. It is a contract by which one engages to save another from a legal consequence of the conduct of one of the parties, or of some other person. “Hold harmless” is that promise. If a claim comes out of their work, they agreed to stand in front of you for the consequence the paragraph describes. The promise lives in the agreement. ACORD’s FAQ says a reference to the contract on the certificate does not provide coverage. The endorsement is what you ask their insurer for. The paragraph is what you ask them for. They are not the same piece of paper, and either one can be narrower than the other.

On a construction contract, California limits how far that promise can go. Civil Code section 2782(a) says that, except for the sections it lists, a clause in, collateral to, or affecting any construction contract that purports to indemnify the promisee against liability for death, bodily injury, property injury, or any other loss arising from the sole negligence or willful misconduct of the promisee, or of the promisee’s agents, servants, or independent contractors who are directly responsible to the promisee, or for defects in design furnished by those persons, is against public policy, void, and unenforceable. The same subdivision says this does not affect the validity of any insurance contract, workers’ compensation, or agreement issued by an admitted insurer.

Section 2782(c)(1) is the owner’s sentence. Except as the subdivision and the other listed sections provide, for a construction contract entered into on or after January 1, 2013, with the owner of privately owned real property to be improved, where the owner is not acting as a contractor or as a supplier of materials or equipment to the work, a clause that purports to impose on any contractor, subcontractor, or supplier of goods or services, or to relieve the owner from, liability is unenforceable to the extent of the active negligence of the owner, including the owner’s employees. For a restaurant that owns or leases the room and is not itself the contractor, that is the paragraph I do not want you to pretend you can write around.

In the room, that means this. You can ask the remodel contractor to answer for a claim that comes from the contractor’s work. You cannot, in that construction contract, make them cover a loss that is only your negligence, or only your willful misconduct, or a design defect you furnished. And on a contract with you as that owner, entered on or after January 1, 2013, you cannot push your own active negligence, including your employees’ active negligence, off onto the contractor, a subcontractor, or a supplier. Active negligence reaches further than a loss that was yours alone. I will not grade the paragraph in your vendor agreement from a blog. Bring it.

A band agreement, a rental contract, and a cleaning agreement are not the construction section I just read. The promise can still be written, in words a person can understand: claims that arise out of their work, their equipment, and their people. Whether their insurance pays the promise is the policy that was issued. I have not opened your vendor’s general liability form, and I am not going to invent the contractual-liability sentence that may or may not be on it.

What I want before they come in

This is the order, whether the truck is the hood company, the lights, the band, the rental house, or the crew that is opening the wall.

  1. Name the work first. If they are going to construct, alter, repair, add to, or improve the building, section 7026 is the license question, and section 7048 is the only small-job exemption I could read: under one thousand dollars, no building permit, no employees, and they are not advertising as a contractor. A remodel is the license. A string of lights may or may not be. I have not read the city’s permit rule for temporary lights, and I will not call a rental tree in the dining room a CSLB job from Monrovia. A band and a stack of rented tables are not that statute. Hood cleaning stays on the hood note unless the visit is actually construction.
  2. Look up the license, if the work needs one. The board’s check-a-license page takes the license number, the business name, or the person’s name. The hiring page says the license number has to be on the advertisement, the bid, and the contract, and that seeing the number does not mean the license is valid. Read the status. Current and active is the phrase I want. Read the classification against the work. The same lookup shows the workers’ compensation information the board has on file: when a policy is there, the insurer, the policy number, and the effective and expiration dates. The board’s database, on the check page, is down Sundays at 8 p.m. through Monday at 6 a.m. Do it before Sunday night.
  3. Read the workers’ comp block against the crew. Employees mean a policy, under section 3700 and section 7125(a). No employees, and a filed exemption, is still allowed today unless the license is one of the five the current statute names: concrete, warm-air HVAC, asbestos abatement, roofing, or tree service. Those five carry it anyway. On January 1, 2028, unless the date moves again, the no-employee exemption in the operative text is the joint-venture exception, not a sole operator. An exemption on the screen is not a headcount. If the license is suspended, the board says the work is unlicensed, and unlicensed work is the 2750.5 question.
  4. Read the ACORD 25 as information. The disclaimer I quoted confers no rights, and it does not amend the policies. Match the named insured to the invoice. Match the policy dates to the day they will be in the building. Read each limit with the form’s own warning that the number may have been reduced by paid claims, and that it may be only what someone asked to print. The cancellation line follows the policy. It does not promise you a letter.
  5. Ask for the endorsement, not a sentence in the remarks. Your name, or blanket wording you can actually meet, for liability caused by their work. Read whether it stops when they leave. If the agreement asks for a waiver of subrogation, that is an endorsement too. The lease note is where I already walked the landlord’s versions. Commercial general liability is not a CSLB requirement. The hiring page says so, in those words. A current license can arrive with no liability policy at all. If they have none, ask how a damaged hood or an injured guest gets paid. “Your insurance will handle it” is not an answer until someone has read your policy.
  6. Read the hold-harmless paragraph once, in English. Claims from their work, their equipment, and their people. On a construction contract, section 2782(a) voids a promise that they cover your sole negligence, your willful misconduct, or a design defect you furnished, and section 2782(c) makes a promise unenforceable to the extent of your active negligence, including your employees, on an owner’s contract entered on or after January 1, 2013. The insurance contract is a different page. The statute says 2782(a) does not undo an insurance contract issued by an admitted insurer.
  7. Keep the PDF with the date they were there. The certificate, the endorsement, the license printout, and the agreement. A claim in February does not get easier because the email was deleted in December.

If the license is active, the dates cover the day, the endorsement actually adds you for the work they are doing, and the crew you counted matches the workers’ comp on file, I will say that. If the PDF is all you have, I will say that too. Coverage depends on the wording of the policy that is actually issued. I will not promise that asking for the endorsement leaves their price, or yours, where it is.

Lunch is the easier table. Bring the certificate, the endorsement, and the one-page agreement if they sent one. Tell me who is coming in, and whether they are opening a wall or plugging in a speaker. I’ll buy.
