Note · November 15, 2026 · Monrovia

The patio paper is not the house in Arcadia.

Assembly Bill 592 kept a COVID catering authorization alive until 2029. That paper is the lot or the sidewalk next to your own door. A holiday job at a client’s house or office is a different ABC form, and the liability and property policies do not pack themselves into the van.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. Written for owners, not for insurance people. I am not your lawyer.

The second week of November is when the book changes shape. A house in Arcadia wants the whole table the Saturday after Thanksgiving. An office in Pasadena wants a reception in a conference room, sterno and all. The trays fit in a van, or they fit in the back of the car you drove to work. Then someone says the patio is already on a catering authorization, so the party is covered. I would rather sort the papers while the pans are still on the shelf.

The paper with the new date is next to your own door

Assembly Bill 592, chapter 469 of the Statutes of 2025, took effect January 1, 2026. The Department of Alcoholic Beverage Control’s summary says it moved the expiration of COVID-19 Temporary Catering Authorizations from July 1, 2026, to January 1, 2029, and it bars the department from issuing any new one on or after January 1, 2027. Business and Professions Code section 25750.5 is the section. It stays in effect only until January 1, 2029, and then it repeals itself. If the department finds a licensee abusing the relief, or jeopardizing public health, safety, or welfare, it may summarily rescind the relief for that licensee.

Read what the authorization actually is. Section 25750.5 says it authorizes the on-sale consumption of the alcoholic beverages for which the licensee already has on-sale privileges, on property adjacent to the licensed premises and within the control of the licensee. ABC’s instructions for Form ABC-218 CV19, revised October 2021, say a Type 58 caterer’s permit is not required for it. The same page lists the kind of adjacent area it means. A room you can reach from inside that is not on the license yet. An outdoor area you can reach from the premises. A parking lot. A sidewalk or other public way that is closed to public access while you are serving. Another area close to the premises, immediately accessible to you, secured by you, and under your control, if the department allows it. A house in Arcadia is not on that list. An office tower in Pasadena is not on that list.

The instructions page is five years old, and parts of it still talk like 2021. It still says the authorization will be considered for counties that have loosened restrictions, and it still prints a $100 fee. I did not find that fee on the application fee schedule ABC marks effective January 1, 2026. I am not going to treat a 2021 instruction sheet as this year’s price, and I am not going to treat its reopening language as the test. The dates that govern are the ones in section 25750.5. If you already have the authorization, the section now carries it to January 1, 2029, subject to the cancellation terms the statute and the instructions both describe. If you do not have one, the department shall not issue a new one on or after January 1, 2027. Between now and that date, ask the district office. Do not assume a diagram you never filed is the same thing as an approval in the drawer.

Two more sentences from those instructions, because holiday parties spill sideways. If you share the expanded area with another licensee, the page says you are jointly responsible for violations in the shared area, and it points you at section 25607 before you operate a shared space. I have not taught that section here. And a public-premises license, the instructions use a Type 42 or a Type 48 as the example, cannot share an area with a license that allows minors. A bar and the café next door are not automatically one patio.

The paper with the new date is the lot next to your own door. The house in Arcadia is a different form.

The four-day permit is also next door

There is a second paper people mix up with a catering job, and it is also about the property touching your own. Section 23399(b) is the event permit. An on-sale general licensee, or an on-sale beer and wine licensee, may apply for it. It authorizes sales, no more than four days in any calendar year, on property adjacent to the licensed premises, owned or under the control of the licensee, secured and controlled by the licensee, and not visible to the general public. Beer, wine, and distilled spirits if the underlying license is on-sale general. Beer and wine if the underlying license is beer and wine. ABC’s license-type list calls this a Type 77. The fee for each day is the same schedule as a catering authorization, in section 23399(c), and any event authorization needs approval from the local law enforcement agency.

Four days, adjacent, not visible to the public. A closed lot behind the restaurant can be that conversation. A client’s conference room cannot. I am not going to spend the lunch turning a Type 77 into a holiday route.

The off-site party is a Type 58, and then a night

The permit that lets certain on-sale licensees sell alcohol somewhere else is a Type 58 caterer’s permit. ABC says it may be held with an on-sale beer and wine license, Type 41 or 42, an on-sale general license, Type 47, 48, or 57, and a list of club and special licenses. A seasonal license, an on-sale general for a boat, and an on-sale general for a vessel of more than 1,000 tons are not eligible. One caterer’s permit per licensee. You apply on Form ABC-239. For a Type 58 held with an on-sale general license or an on-sale beer and wine license, section 23320 sets the annual fee, on and after October 1, 2019, at $215. That is the line for a Type 41, a Type 47, or a Type 48. The same section prices a club’s Type 58 by the size of the city. ABC’s caterer’s page says the fee can vary by license type and by population, which is why I am quoting the eating-place and bar line and leaving the club schedule in the code. This is ABC’s fee. It is not a premium.

The permit is the right to ask. The night itself is a catering authorization, and ABC’s Type 58 page says the department has to authorize every catering event. Paying the fee does not approve it. If it is approved, you print the approval and keep it at the event, with any conditions you signed.

Section 23399(a) is the privilege. Under an on-sale general license, the caterer’s permit authorizes the sale of beer, wine, and distilled spirits for consumption at conventions, sporting events, trade exhibits, picnics, social gatherings, or similar events, at a place in the state the department approves. Under an on-sale beer and wine license, the same list, and the alcohol is beer and wine. A Type 41 does not become a Type 47 because the party is in a backyard. Section 23399(d) says that at every approved event you may exercise only the privileges your license already has, and you have to comply with the on-sale rules. A violation can be grounds to suspend or revoke the license or the permit, or both, as though it happened on your licensed premises.

The daily fee is in section 23399(c), and the fee schedule effective January 1, 2026 prints the same dollars. One hundred dollars when the anticipated attendance that day is under 1,000. Three hundred twenty-five dollars from 1,000 people up to, but not including, 5,000. One thousand dollars at 5,000 or more. The fee is for each day alcohol is sold. A house party and a normal office reception are the hundred-dollar day, if the head count you put on the form is honest. I am quoting ABC’s fee. I am not quoting a premium.

The clock is three different sentences, and a Friday booking can miss all of them.

Title 4, section 60.5, of the California Code of Regulations says a catering authorization shall be obtained at least three days before the event, unless the department waives that for good cause. The written application has to give the address and the common name of the place, how many times you have catered that place in the current calendar year, who is sponsoring it, the type of event, and the estimated attendance. The instructions for Form ABC-218, revised January 2024, say the paper form has to be in at least three days before the event and no more than 30 days before. Outside that window, it may not be processed. The online application is a different window: at least five days before the event, and not more than 90. If the event is inside five days, you cannot apply online. You use the paper form at the district office, and you still need the three days unless the department waives them. Consecutive days with the same hours and the same attendance can be one application, up to three days online. Different hours, a different head count, or a gap in the dates means a separate event.

The place has a cap. Section 23399(f) and rule 60.5 say a catering authorization shall not be issued for any one premises for more than 36 events in a calendar year, unless the department decides more are needed for substantial public demand. ABC’s caterer’s page says that 36 applies to all Type 58 permits, including clubs. A ballroom that already hosted everyone else’s December can be out of authorizations before you call. The form asks for your own count at that address. The cap is on the premises.

ABC may also want the property owner, the law enforcement agency, operating conditions, or a diagram on Form ABC-253. Rule 60.5 says an authorization shall not issue for a premises previously denied a license because of consideration points or a zoning conflict, unless the right person waives that in writing. Near a university, a state college, a veterans home, or another state or federal institution, the rule requires bona fide meals, and the event still has to be consistent with public welfare and morals. The Type 58 page says food service is not otherwise required at the catered event. I am quoting that sentence. I am not telling a Type 41 or a Type 47 that the eating-place rules on the license went away. Those rules are the note on the license itself.

A few operating lines I do not want discovered at the venue. With the department’s approval, the license types ABC lists, including 41, 47, and 48, may cater anywhere in the state, subject to the 36. If the site already has a license, that license has to be surrendered for the time of your event, on Form ABC-231, and the restrictions on it remain. ABC’s example is a Type 48 premises: minors still cannot attend. You may not store distilled spirits at the event site. They go back to your licensed premises after the event. People under 21 may be at the event unless that location normally prohibits them. They may not possess, purchase, or consume the alcohol. Wholesalers may deliver to the site.

The people pouring have to be certified. ABC’s license list says a Type 58 is subject to Responsible Beverage Service, and the servers and their managers have to be certified. The RBS FAQ says a catered event is an extension of the permanent on-sale licensee. If you hire extra help for the night, those people have to be certified before they serve. The license on your wall, and the lawsuit that can follow a bad sale, are the other note. I am not teaching them again.

The form goes to the district office for the event if you are not online. The office ABC labels Monrovia is not on Huntington Drive. The address on that page is 12750 Center Court Drive, Suite 720, Cerritos, and the email is monrovia@abc.ca.gov. I am not deciding from this note which valley cities sit in which district. Use the office that has the event.

A daily license belongs to someone else

Section 23399(c) says none of this limits the department’s power to issue special licenses under section 24045, or daily on-sale general licenses under section 24045.1. The daily-license page is who those are for. A general daily license, beer, wine, and distilled spirits, is for an organization formed for a charitable or civic purpose, a fraternal organization in existence more than five years, a religious organization, or a political organization. A beer and wine daily license is for an existing nonprofit, and the page’s list is charitable, civic, cultural, fraternal, patriotic, political, religious, social, or amateur sports. A first-time organization has to qualify, with IRS or Franchise Tax Board papers. The form is ABC-221, to the district office for the event location, at least 10 days before and not more than 30 days before. The property owner has to authorize it. On the fee schedule effective July 1, 2026, a daily beer and wine license is $50 a day and a daily general is $75.

A company in Pasadena throwing a party for its own staff is not that list. Your restaurant is not that list either, unless you are one of those organizations, which a normal dining room is not. If a church, a booster club, or a civic group does hold the daily license, they are the licensee for that day. Your Type 41 does not become their license because you plated the food. If the contract says you are selling the alcohol, you need your own authority. If their name is on the daily license, the approval should say so, and the liquor question below is whose policy answers a sale made on their license. I will not invent a legal way for an unlicensed host to pour.

The liability form follows the work, until an endorsement pins it to the dining room

Start with the form, not the address on the dec page. On CG 00 01 04 13, the commercial general liability coverage form, Coverage A pays what the insured becomes legally obligated to pay as damages because of bodily injury or property damage to which the insurance applies. The occurrence has to take place in the coverage territory. Coverage territory, on that form, includes the United States, its territories and possessions, Puerto Rico, and Canada. A house in Arcadia is inside that sentence. The form I can open does not, by itself, say the injury has to happen at the restaurant.

The endorsement that can say that is CG 21 44 04 17, Limitation of Coverage to Designated Premises, Project or Operation. Attached to CG 00 01, it replaces the Coverage A trigger. The bodily injury or property damage has to occur on the premises in the schedule, or on the grounds and structures appurtenant to them, or it has to arise out of the project or operation in the schedule. It does the same kind of cut to personal and advertising injury and to medical payments. If the schedule shows the dining room and the project line is blank, the house is not the scheduled premises. Read the schedule before you tell the venue the policy follows you. A carrier can use its own wording. The endorsement on your forms list is the one that counts.

Two different moments, if the form is not pinned to the address.

While you are still there, serving, the claim is your operations. A guest who slips on a spill you made is bodily injury caused by an occurrence, and the place is in the coverage territory. Medical payments on the same form are written for an accident on premises you own or rent, on the ways next to those premises, or because of your operations. “Because of your operations” is how a medical-payments sentence can reach a house you do not rent. CG 21 44 replaces that sentence if it is attached. Medical payments are not the lawsuit. They are the no-fault expenses the form describes, up to the limit on the declarations. I am not quoting a dollar the dec page has not shown me.

After the work is done, or the food has left your hands, look at the products-completed operations hazard. CG 00 01 defines it as bodily injury and property damage occurring away from premises you own or rent and arising out of your product or your work, with exceptions. Products still in your physical possession are one exception. Work that has not been completed or abandoned is the other. The form then says when work is deemed completed, including when that part of the work has been put to its intended use by someone other than a contractor on the same project. The definition also sets aside injury or damage arising out of the transportation of property, with a narrow loading exception. The drive is the note on the car. I am not re-teaching it in this paragraph.

Your product, on that form, includes goods you sell or handle, other than real property. The food is that kind of goods. A guest who is sick the next day, at home, is the shape of claim the products definition is written around: away from premises you own or rent, arising out of your product, and the product is no longer in your possession. There is a separate products-completed operations aggregate. The dec page shows it next to the general aggregate. I will not sort a particular tray into one box or the other from a blog. I will tell you that a designated-premises endorsement, if it requires the injury to occur on the scheduled premises, can close the off-site claim in both moments. The products hazard is defined as happening away from premises you own or rent. An endorsement that demands the injury happen on the dining-room premises is aimed at the same geography.

Damage to the house is property damage, and the form has cuts. The exclusion I can read includes personal property in the insured’s care, custody, or control. It includes that particular part of real property on which you, or a contractor working on your behalf, are performing operations, if the property damage arises out of those operations. It includes that particular part of any property that has to be restored because your work was incorrectly performed on it. A guest’s injury is bodily injury. A burned table, a ruined floor, a rug you had in your hands: those are property-damage sentences, and I will not decide them here.

Fire has its own line. Exclusions c through n do not apply to damage by fire to premises while rented to you or temporarily occupied by you with the owner’s permission. A separate limit applies. The damage-to-premises-rented-to-you limit is what the form points at for fire damage to premises you temporarily occupy with permission. The number is on the declarations. A sterno under a chafer is why I want that number before December, and why I will not pretend the occurrence limit is the number.

If you are in the business of manufacturing, distributing, selling, serving, or furnishing alcoholic beverages, the liquor exclusion on the same form applies. It takes out bodily injury or property damage for which an insured may be held liable by reason of causing or contributing to intoxication, furnishing alcohol to a person under the legal drinking age or under the influence, or a statute relating to the sale, gift, distribution, or use of alcoholic beverages. It applies even when the claim is framed as negligent supervision, hiring, or training, or as a failure to provide transportation, if the occurrence involved that alcohol. The sentence that says letting a person bring their own alcohol is not, by itself, being in that business is written for your premises. I am not stretching it to a client’s house. The lawsuit, the obvious-intoxication hole in the immunity, and the misdemeanor are the Type 41 and Type 47 note. The fight is the New Year’s Eve note. A fight in a conference room is still a fight.

A server who is burned at the house is an employee. The same form excludes bodily injury to an employee arising out of and in the course of employment, or out of duties related to the business, and it excludes an obligation under a workers’ compensation law. That file is the workers’ comp note.

The address on the dec page is not a wall. An endorsement can build one. Read the schedule.

The chafers are a property form, and the van is a hole in the extension

The property form I can open is CP 00 10 10 12, Building and Personal Property Coverage Form. It pays for direct physical loss of or damage to covered property at the premises described in the declarations, caused by a covered cause of loss. The dining room is that premises. The client’s house is not, until an extension says otherwise.

The extension is Property Off-Premises. On this edition you may extend the insurance to covered property while it is away from the described premises, if it is temporarily at a location you do not own, lease, or operate, or in storage at a location you lease under a lease executed after the policy term began, or at a fair, trade show, or exhibition. The extension does not apply to property in or on a vehicle. It does not apply to property in the care, custody, or control of your salespersons, unless they have it at a fair, trade show, or exhibition. The most the extension pays is $10,000.

Read that against a Saturday. The chafers, the sterno, the hotel pans, the platters, if they are covered property at all, can be temporarily at a house you do not own, lease, or operate. The cap on this edition is ten thousand dollars, and it is not a second limit stacked on top of a blank. Property that was never covered property does not become covered because it left the building. And the sentence stops when the pans are in or on a vehicle. The van, the owner’s car, the closer’s hatchback: that is the hole. A businessowners form can be written differently. I will not paste this $10,000 onto a form I have not read. Bring the property declarations.

The form people buy for property that moves is inland marine. IRMI’s glossary describes inland marine as property insurance for property in transit over land, and for certain movable property that may not stay in one place, among other categories. It says many of those forms cover without regard to where the property is, and that those are sometimes called floaters. It also says the forms are generally broader than property forms, on causes of loss and on location, and that they have exclusions of their own. Wear and tear, inherent vice, and employee dishonesty are the examples IRMI gives. I am not quoting a price, and I am not saying a floater is sitting on your package because you own chafers. Ask whether one is on the policy, what it schedules, and whether transit is inside it. A shrug is not a yes.

The drive is the other note

Who is driving, whose name is on the car, and what happens if that car hits someone are the Thanksgiving note. Hired and non-owned auto is that page. The endorsement I walked through there is written for the other person’s injuries. It is not written for the pans in the back. This note is the pans, the guest at the house, and the alcohol. If the only open question is the Civic, read that one and then come back with the list of what was in the car.

What the venue types into the contract

The email from the venue, or from the company’s risk office, usually asks for three things. Name them as an additional insured. Waive subrogation. Send a certificate. Sometimes the number on the certificate has to be higher than the number on your primary policy. Those are three different pieces of paper, and the certificate is the one that does not do the work.

Insurance Code section 384 says a certificate or verification, when it is used as evidence of insurance in place of the policy, shall contain this statement or words to the effect of it. The certificate is not an insurance policy and does not amend, extend, or alter the coverage afforded by the policies listed. Notwithstanding any requirement, term, or condition of any contract the certificate relates to, the insurance is subject to all the terms, exclusions, and conditions of the policies. A sentence in the venue’s contract does not rewrite your form. A sentence someone types in the remarks box does not either. The section does not apply to a surplus line broker certificate as section 48 defines it. For the admitted policy this note is about, the certificate is evidence. The forms are the coverage.

Additional insured, on the edition I can open, is CG 20 26 04 13, Additional Insured – Designated Person or Organization. It adds the person or organization in the schedule, and only for bodily injury, property damage, or personal and advertising injury caused, in whole or in part, by your acts or omissions, or the acts or omissions of those acting on your behalf. The acts have to be in the performance of your ongoing operations, or in connection with premises you own or rent. A catering job can be ongoing operations. The client’s house is not premises you own or rent, so do not lean on the second prong. The injury still has to be caused in whole or in part by your side. A cord the venue ran, with nothing of yours in the story, is a different sentence. If a contract requires the coverage, the insurance afforded will not be broader than the contract requires, and the most paid on behalf of that additional insured is the amount the contract requires or the limit on the declarations, whichever is less. The endorsement does not increase your limits. If the contract wants two million and the primary policy has one million, this page does not create the second million. That layer is the umbrella note. I am not teaching it again.

The name has to be in the schedule, or the form says the declarations will show it. “The venue, as their interests may appear,” with no name and no contract the form can read, is how these come back. A later edition, or a carrier’s own form, can move a word. Read the edition on your policy.

Waiver of subrogation, on the general liability side, is CG 24 04, Waiver of Transfer of Rights of Recovery Against Others to Us. FilingFocus, updated November 4, 2026, quotes the 12 19 edition. The insurer waives any right of recovery against the person or organization in the schedule because of payments it makes under the coverage part. The waiver applies only to the extent you waived your own right of recovery against that person before the loss. It applies only to the person in the schedule. FilingFocus shows companies still filing the 12 19 edition, including California filings dated November 4, 2026, and it also shows older editions and at least one group that files its own version. The contract has to waive, and it has to exist before the loss. A line added to a certificate the morning after is the wrong order. The endorsement on your forms list is the one I want to see, not the remark on the certificate.

Who is selling, and whose liquor form that is

If you are the one selling under the Type 58, you are the licensee, and section 23399(d) treats the event like your premises for a violation. The liquor form is the page the general liability exclusion pointed at.

FC&S, on the liquor liability policy, describes CG 00 33 04 13 as the occurrence form. It pays damages because of injury if liability is imposed by reason of selling, serving, or furnishing any alcoholic beverage, and the injury occurs during the policy period in the coverage territory. The coverage territory FC&S quotes is the same map as the general liability form: the United States, Puerto Rico, and Canada, with the travel and short-trip extensions. FC&S also says the premises you list are not, by themselves, a wall. The endorsement that builds the wall, in that article, is CG 28 06 01 96. Through it, the liquor coverage applies only to damages arising out of the insured premises, meaning premises shown on that endorsement or on the declarations, plus premises you acquire during the policy period if you notify the company within thirty days and no other valid insurance applies. A house you visit for one night is not a premises you acquired. If that endorsement is on the policy, I want to read it against the address before anyone pours. If it is not, I still want the forms list. FC&S is describing ISO wording from the editions in that article. Your carrier’s form can differ.

The civil rules, the misdemeanor, and why two liquor quotes with the same million can be different policies are the Type 41 and Type 47 note. If the night includes a fight, or an employee stepping into one, the exclusion and the smaller limit are the New Year’s Eve note. Both of those can show up in a ballroom. The certificate the venue is holding will not show the carve-out.

If the nonprofit holds the daily license, they are the seller on that license. Your liquor form is not their license. If the venue held a license and surrendered it so you could cater, ABC says the restrictions on that surrendered license remain, and your approval is the document in the room. Bring the approval. Bring the liquor declarations. I will read whose name is on the sale before I tell you whose form I think answers it.

What I want on the table before the trays leave

  1. Which ABC paper this job actually is. The COVID authorization, if you have one, for the lot or the sidewalk you control, with the new dates in section 25750.5.   2. Who is selling, and what they are allowed to sell. Beer and wine if the license is a Type 41. Spirits only if the license is the general one. No distilled spirits stored at the house.   6. The venue’s contract, and then the endorsements, and only then the certificate. The additional insured in the schedule, on a form that reaches ongoing operations.   7. Who is driving. That list is the Thanksgiving note. Add it. Do not make me reconstruct the car from a group text.
