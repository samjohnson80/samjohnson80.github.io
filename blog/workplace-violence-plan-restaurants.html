The café was not the ten-person exception.

A written workplace violence plan has been required since July 1, 2024. A dining room, a bar, or a café open to the sidewalk does not fit the small closed workplace the statute lets out. The permanent Cal/OSHA standard is still a draft.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. Written for owners, not for insurance people. I am not your lawyer.

The version I hear is a head count. Eight on a Tuesday in Monrovia. A Pasadena bar that is three people at five and a full floor at ten. Someone at the table says the law is for hospitals, or for a company with a human-resources office, or for a shop the public cannot walk into. A café with a door on the sidewalk is the public walking in.

Labor Code section 6401.9 is the workplace-violence section from Senate Bill 553. Cal/OSHA’s general-industry page says the governor signed that bill on September 30, 2023. The section was effective January 1, 2024, and subdivisions (b) through (g) have been operative since July 1, 2024. That is the written plan, the training, the violent incident log, the records, and a citation if they are missing. The permanent regulation is a separate piece of paper, and it is not finished. The plan did not get to wait for it.

The head count is the wrong test

The section applies to employers, employees, places of employment, and employer-provided housing, with a list of exceptions. The exception an owner hopes to be in is a place with less than 10 employees working there at any given time, that is not accessible to the public, and that complies with the injury and illness prevention program in Title 8, section 3203.

All three have to be true. Less than ten is who is working at one moment, not how many W-2s are in a drawer. Ten people on a Saturday means that moment is outside “less than 10,” even when Tuesday is quiet. Not accessible to the public means the public does not come in. A dining room, a bar, a patio, a coffee window on Huntington Drive: the public comes in. A closed prep kitchen with no guests, fewer than ten people in the room, and an injury program that actually complies with section 3203 is a different set of facts. If you cannot show all three, the section applies.

The other exceptions are health care already covered by Title 8, section 3342, certain corrections facilities and law-enforcement employers, and an employee teleworking from a location the employee chose that is not under the employer’s control. Books done at someone’s own kitchen table can be that last one. The closer on your line is at a place of employment you control.

Cal/OSHA’s FAQ says the employee-involvement rules apply at a non-union workplace. A representative is part of the statute where you have one. The plan is still yours to write with the people who work the shift.

Labor Code section 6401.7 says the written injury-prevention program includes a workplace violence prevention plan conforming to section 6401.9. Section 6401.9 says that written plan may be a stand-alone section of the injury and illness prevention program, or its own document. The FAQ says both: you may keep it separate, and you do not have to paste it into the injury program. The workers’ comp note is where I wrote about an injury and illness prevention program that sits in a binder and does not run. This plan can be a section of that program, or its own document. It still has to be the one the floor can get to.

The regulation is still a draft

Section 6401.9(h) sets two dates. The division shall propose standards no later than December 31, 2025. The standards board shall adopt them no later than December 31, 2026. Those standards have to include, at a minimum, what the section already requires, and any additional requirements the division decides employees need.

What is posted is not an adopted rule. Cal/OSHA’s advisory page is about a new Title 8 section 3343. The newest item there is a September 2026 discussion draft, for an in-person advisory meeting set for October 27, 2026, in Oakland. The same page posted discussion drafts on April 23, 2026, and July 22, 2026. Cal/OSHA’s guidance page still says the division is working on the general-industry standard, and that the board is required to adopt it no later than December 31, 2026.

The FAQ is dated August 6, 2024, and it tells you the proposed regulation will be posted on the Standards Board’s proposed-regulations page. That page is marked October 2026. It lists a crane-operator item for a hearing on November 19, 2026, and trichloroethylene and snow avalanche blasting for December 17, 2026. Section 3343 is not on the list. The statute’s date for the division to propose was the end of 2025. The text still up on the advisory page this fall is labeled a discussion draft. I am not going to call that a violation from a note. I am going to tell you the two dates in the statute, and that a discussion draft is not the rule for Saturday. Build the plan to section 6401.9. Leave the September draft in the “may change” pile.

The regulation can still move. The plan does not get to wait on it.

What the plan has to say about your room

The plan is in writing. It is available and easily accessible at all times to employees, to an authorized employee representative, and to Cal/OSHA. It is in effect at all times and in all work areas, and it is specific to the hazards and the corrections for each work area and operation. The FAQ’s examples of making it accessible are a common area, a binder, or the company website. The test is the statute’s. A file on a laptop the Friday manager cannot open is a hard way to meet “at all times.”

Cal/OSHA posted a model written plan for general industry, and a Spanish version. The FAQ says the model is a framework, that every employer has to tailor it to the work area, and that using it does not ensure compliance. You may write your own. The employer fact sheet is a summary of the bill. The publications list also offers that fact sheet in Spanish, Chinese (simplified and traditional), Korean, Punjabi, Tagalog, and Vietnamese. A fact sheet on the corkboard is not the training, and it is not the plan.

The plan names the people responsible, by name or by job title, and it describes the roles when more than one person has a piece of it. It says how employees, and a representative if you have one, take part in writing it, in finding and correcting hazards, in the training, and in reporting and investigating. It says how you coordinate with another employer when that applies, so those employees get the training in subdivision (e), and so an incident involving any employee is reported, investigated, and recorded. A guard who works for a company, a cleaning crew, a caterer in the kitchen: at a multiemployer worksite, the employer whose employee experienced the incident keeps the log and gives a copy to the controlling employer. The statute uses those words. I am not deciding, from this note, whether you are the controlling employer on a given night. If you are the house they would hand it to, ask for the copy before everyone goes home.

It says how you accept and respond to reports, and how you prohibit retaliation for a report. The FAQ says an anonymous system is not required. A way to report a violent incident, a threat, or another concern to you or to law enforcement, without fear of reprisal, is required. It says how supervisory and nonsupervisory employees comply. It says how concerns are investigated, and how employees are told the results and what you corrected.

For an emergency, it says how you alert employees to the presence, the location, and the nature of it. It has evacuation or sheltering plans that are appropriate and feasible for that worksite. It says how to get help from staff assigned to respond, if you have any, from security, if you have any, and from law enforcement. Calling law enforcement is one of those sentences. Telling the people on the line where it is, and what it is, is the other one.

Inspections: when the plan is first established, after each workplace violence incident, whenever you learn of a new or previously unrecognized hazard, and on a periodic schedule. Corrections in a timely manner, consistent with the injury-program rule the statute points to in section 3203. Procedures for the response after an incident, and for the investigation. A review at least annually, when a deficiency is observed or becomes apparent, and after an incident, with employees in that review.

Cal/OSHA’s general-industry page lists factors that can raise the risk. The ones that show up in a restaurant without a speech are exchange of money, working alone, working at night and in the early morning, cash and other valued items, and guarding that money or property. The same list includes customers known or suspected to have a history of violence, and employees with a history of assaults or who have been belligerent, intimidating, or threatening. A closer alone with the till is those factors in one shift: money, alone, and late. A guest you already know has been violent is the customer factor. The patio and the line are different work areas, and the statute wants the plan specific to each. The FAQ says the written plan has to be specific to the hazards and the corrections for each work area and operation. One paragraph labeled “all locations” does not do that if Saturday’s patio and the morning prep are not the same hazard.

The definitions are wider than a punch. Workplace violence means any act of violence or threat of violence that occurs in a place of employment. It includes the threat or use of physical force against an employee that results in, or has a high likelihood of resulting in, injury, psychological trauma, or stress, whether or not the employee sustains an injury. It includes an incident involving the threat or use of a firearm or other dangerous weapon, including a common object used as a weapon, whether or not anyone is hurt. A threat of violence includes a verbal or written statement, texts, electronic messages, social media, and other online posts, or conduct that conveys an intent to cause physical harm, or that is reasonably perceived to, and that serves no legitimate purpose. Lawful acts of self-defense or defense of others are not workplace violence. I am not your lawyer on a particular comment. What the general liability form does when an employee steps into a fight is the New Year’s Eve note. This one is the plan.

The section sorts the conduct into four types. Type 1 is committed by a person with no legitimate business at the worksite, including someone who enters with the intent to commit a crime. Cal/OSHA’s examples include retail robberies, and workplaces where employees have face-to-face contact and exchange money with the public. Type 2 is directed at employees by customers, clients, patients, students, inmates, or visitors. Type 3 is against an employee by a present or former employee, supervisor, or manager. Type 4 is committed in the workplace by a person who does not work there, but has or is known to have had a personal relationship with an employee. The same Cal/OSHA page says a workplace can be more than one type. Its example is a convenience store: a robbery, and a personal dispute. A restaurant that takes cash and stays open late can be more than one type. Write the types the room actually has. A plan that only imagines a stranger has skipped the guest, the former cook, and the argument that followed someone to the door.

The training is a person, once a year

You train when the plan is first established, and annually after that. You train again when a new or previously unrecognized workplace violence hazard has been identified, and when the plan changes. The additional training may be limited to that new hazard or that change.

The initial training, and the annual training, cover the plan, how to get a copy at no cost, and how to take part in developing and implementing it. They cover the definitions and requirements of the section. They cover how to report an incident or a concern to the employer or to law enforcement without fear of reprisal. They cover the hazards specific to the job, the corrective measures you have implemented, how to seek assistance, and strategies to avoid physical harm. They cover the violent incident log, and how to obtain the records in paragraphs (1) to (3) of the recordkeeping subdivision. They include an opportunity for interactive questions and answers with a person knowledgeable about your plan.

The material has to be appropriate in content and vocabulary to the educational level, literacy, and language of the employees. The FAQ says employers with employees working in California provide that training to those employees. A video in the office, with nobody there who knows the plan, is missing the person the statute names for the questions. If the language on the line is not the language of the plan, that sentence is the place to start.

The FAQ says an employer with questions can get free technical assistance from Cal/OSHA’s Consultation Services Branch. That is help. It is not a finding that the plan complies. The same FAQ says an employee may report to local law enforcement or to the local Cal/OSHA enforcement office, and that a complaint to the district office can be made as a confidential complaint. The plan’s reporting procedure has to include law enforcement, not only the owner.

The log is every incident

The employer records information in a violent incident log for every workplace violence incident. The FAQ says you may make your own log, as long as it contains what subdivision (d) requires. You do not have to use Cal/OSHA’s blank.

The information is based on what you solicit from the employees who experienced it, on witness statements, and on investigation findings. You omit personal identifying information sufficient to allow identification of any person involved, such as a name, address, email, telephone number, Social Security number, or other information that, alone or combined with publicly available information, reveals the person. The person completing the log is a separate line. The log includes that person’s name, job title, and the date completed.

The rest is specific. Date, time, and location. The workplace violence type or types. A detailed description. A classification of who committed it: a client or customer, family or friend of a client or customer, a stranger with criminal intent, a coworker, a supervisor or manager, a partner or spouse, a parent or relative, or other. A classification of the circumstances, including whether the employee was doing usual job duties, working in a poorly lit area, rushed, working during a low staffing level, isolated or alone, unable to get help, working in a community setting, or working in an unfamiliar or new location. Where it occurred, such as in the workplace, the parking lot or other area outside the workplace, or another area. The type of incident, including a physical attack without a weapon, an attack with a weapon or object, a threat of physical force or of the use of a weapon or other object, a sexual assault or threat, an animal attack, or other. Consequences, including whether security or law enforcement was contacted and their response, and the actions taken to protect employees from a continuing threat or from any other hazard identified because of the incident.

The FAQ answers the animal question, which matters if a dog comes onto the patio and someone reaches for this log. The section addresses person-to-person violence. An attack in which a person uses an animal as a weapon is workplace violence and goes on the violent incident log. An ordinary animal attack is recorded as an ordinary workplace injury. It goes on the Form 300 when it meets those rules. It is not, by itself, this log.

A threat that injures nobody still goes on the violent incident log. The FAQ says every workplace violence incident is documented there. Form 300 is the log of work-related injuries and illnesses. The same FAQ says you record a work-related death on it, and a work-related injury or illness that involves loss of consciousness, restricted work or job transfer, days away from work, or medical treatment beyond first aid, including when workplace violence caused it, plus the other cases in Title 8, sections 14300.8 through 14300.12. A threat that never becomes that kind of injury is the violent incident log. It is not automatically a 300 entry. The 300 log does not replace the violent incident log. The violent incident log is reviewed when you do the periodic review of the plan.

A threat that never becomes a claim still belongs in the log.

Five years, and fifteen days

Records of workplace violence hazard identification, evaluation, and correction are kept at least five years. Training records are kept at least one year, and they include the training dates, the contents or a summary, the names and qualifications of the people who conducted the training, and the names and job titles of everyone who attended. Violent incident logs are kept at least five years. Records of the incident investigations are kept at least five years. Those investigation records shall not contain medical information as Civil Code section 56.05(j) defines it. The cross-reference is in section 6401.9. The clinic chart stays out of the investigation file.

All of those records are made available to the division on request, for examination and copying. The hazard records, the training records, and the logs are made available to employees and their representatives, on request and without cost, for examination and copying within 15 calendar days. The investigation file is outside that employee-access sentence. Cal/OSHA’s general-industry page draws the same line, and it also says to keep the Form 300 for five years. That is a second clock, on a different form.

Three files. They are different policies.

When an employee is injured, Cal/OSHA’s page says to get the person medical care through the workers’ compensation insurer, to record the incident in the violent incident log, to investigate, and to review the plan and revise it if you need to. A serious injury or illness, or a death, is a separate report to the division. Title 8, section 342 says immediately, by telephone or through the online mechanism the division establishes. Immediately means as soon as practically possible, and not longer than 8 hours after the employer knows or, with diligent inquiry, would have known. If the employer can demonstrate exigent circumstances, the report may be made no longer than 24 hours after the incident.

Section 330(h) defines a serious injury or illness as one that requires inpatient hospitalization for a purpose other than medical observation or diagnostic testing, or in which an employee suffers an amputation, the loss of an eye, or any serious degree of permanent disfigurement. It excludes an injury, illness, or death caused by an accident on a public street or highway, unless the accident occurred in a construction zone. The eight hours run from knowledge, or from when diligent inquiry would have produced it. They do not start when the morning manager sits down. The report includes, if you have it, the time and date, the employer’s name, address, and telephone, the name and job title of the person reporting, the address of the site, a person to contact there, the injured employee’s name and address, the nature of the injury, where the person was taken, the other law enforcement agencies present, and whether the scene or the instrumentality has been altered.

The claim form is not the violent incident log, and the two pages that mention “one working day” are not the same width. Cal/OSHA’s workplace-violence page says to give employees notice of workers’ compensation eligibility within one working day of a workplace violence incident, and it points employers to the Division of Workers’ Compensation for the medical care. Labor Code section 5401 is the claim-form statute. Within one working day of notice or knowledge of an injury that results in lost time beyond the employee’s shift at the time of injury, or that results in medical treatment beyond first aid, the employer provides, personally or by first-class mail, a claim form and a notice of potential eligibility. First aid, in that section, means any one-time treatment, and any follow-up visit for observation of minor scratches, cuts, burns, splinters, or other minor industrial injury, which do not ordinarily require medical care. The notice has to be easily understandable and available in English and Spanish. Insofar as practicable, the notice and the claim form are a single document. A threat with no injury is the violent incident log. An injury past first aid, or time lost past the shift, is the claim form, and the log is still filled out.

The same Cal/OSHA page says to complete the Employer’s Report of Occupational Injury or Illness, Form 5020, for each injury or illness that results in lost time beyond the date of the incident or that requires medical treatment beyond first aid. “Beyond the date of the incident” is that page’s trigger for Form 5020. “Beyond the shift” is the trigger in section 5401. I am leaving them side by side. Use the words on the form you are filling out.

The premium, the restaurant class, and the audit are the workers’ comp note. The person on the clock is that file. The New Year’s Eve note already walks through the employer’s-liability exclusion on the general liability form. I am not teaching that page again.

A guest who is hurt, or two guests, is the general liability question, and often the liquor form with it. A fight can sit outside the million on the certificate, or inside a smaller number, depending on the endorsement. That is the other note. The workplace-violence plan is a Labor Code duty. It is not an insuring agreement. I have not read a form, in this note, that turns the binder into coverage. Bring the declarations and the forms list if you want that read. They are a different stack from the plan.

The plan has to prohibit retaliation against an employee who makes a report. If the letter is a firing, a schedule that got cut, harassment, or discrimination after someone spoke up, that question is the employment-practices note. I am not re-teaching the wage claim or the retention. A citation under section 6401.9(g) is another paper. The division enforces the section by a citation alleging a violation and a notice of civil penalty, consistent with Labor Code section 6317. The employer may appeal to the appeals board, consistent with section 6319. I will not tell you a policy pays that penalty. Nothing in this note is a form that says so.

What I want on the table

  1. The written plan. Its own document, or a real section of the injury program. Specific to the dining room, the bar, the patio, the lot, and the close. Who is responsible, by name or by title. How a person reports to you and to law enforcement. How you tell the room the presence, the location, and the nature. The model is a start, in English or in Spanish. A model you have not tailored to the room is what the FAQ says does not ensure compliance.
  2. The inspections and the review. One when the plan was established. One after any incident. One when a new hazard shows up. A periodic one with a date. The annual review, with employees in it. The review after the last incident, if there was one.
  3. The training record for the last year. The date, the contents or a summary, who taught it and that person’s qualifications, and the name and job title of everyone who attended. A person in the room who could answer a question about your plan. Material in the language the employees actually use.
  4. The violent incident log. Including the nights nobody went to a clinic. Five years of it. No names of the people involved. The name, job title, and date of the person who completed the entry. At a multiemployer worksite, the copy the statute says goes to the controlling employer.
  5. The injury papers, kept separate. The Form 300 when the case is recordable. The claim form when the injury is past first aid or past the shift. Form 5020 on the trigger Cal/OSHA’s page states. The report under section 342 when the injury is serious or someone has died. The workers’ comp policy is the other note.
  6. The two insurance pages, when you want them read. General liability and liquor, for a guest and for a fight. Employment practices, if the issue is retaliation or how someone was treated. I will read the forms. The certificate’s million is not the reading.

If you would rather do this over a plate than over a binder, pick the restaurant. Bring the plan if you have one. If what you have is a handbook that never mentions the log, bring that and say so. Tell me what the close looks like. Who is last, where the money sits, whether the patio stays open, who in the building is not on your payroll. I’ll buy.
