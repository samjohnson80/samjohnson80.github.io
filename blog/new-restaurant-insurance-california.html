What a new California restaurant actually needs

The week before you open, you are choosing glassware and arguing with the hood installer. That is a bad week to meet your insurance requirements.

By Sam Johnson. I work with people opening a place and people taking one over, from Monrovia out through the San Gabriel Valley.

Insurance shows up, in that last week, as a PDF the landlord wants by Friday. The list itself is not mysterious. The order is what gets missed. If you want the six-question version for your own concept, it is on the restaurant page. This is the longer version, the one I wish people read before they sign.

Start with the lease, not with a website

Before you buy a policy, read the insurance section of the lease. It usually names a general liability limit. It usually asks the landlord to be added as an additional insured. Sometimes it asks for a waiver of subrogation, or a set number of days’ notice before the policy can be cancelled. I have watched an owner buy something online that could not issue the endorsement the lease required, then pay a second time for a policy that could. The lease is the assignment. The policy is the homework.

If you are taking over a restaurant that is already open, the seller’s policy is in the seller’s name. It does not become yours because you have the keys, the recipes, or a week of their staff. Have yours start the day the ownership changes. The old declarations page is useful as a map. It is not your coverage.

The lease tells you the minimum the landlord wants to see. It does not tell you what would actually keep the place standing after a bad night.

The list I walk through at lunch

None of these is a universal legal requirement except where I say the statute says so. They are the pieces I go through with a new operator, in this order, because this is the order the gaps show up.

General liability. A guest slips. Someone says the food made them sick. This is the policy almost every landlord asks to see first, and it is the one a certificate is usually built from. Product claims of that “your kitchen did this” kind are typically part of general liability. Spoiled inventory in the walk-in is a different coverage. I keep that distinction on the scenario list.

Property. If you lease, this is your equipment, your stock, and the buildout you paid for and cannot legally take when you leave. If you own the building, it is the building too. A grease fire does not care which column you are in. The amount to insure is what it costs to replace the thing, not what you paid a used dealer on a good day, and not a number you picked because it made the premium look friendly.

Business income. Owners skip this because it is not on the landlord’s checklist. If a covered loss closes you for six weeks, the property coverage is there for the damaged things. Lost Friday nights are their own coverage, with their own limit and their own waiting period. Know both numbers before you need them. I cannot tell you a standard limit for a new café in Duarte. I can tell you to decide how many weeks of sales you could actually survive without.

Workers’ compensation, the day you have an employee. California Labor Code section 3700 requires every employer to secure the payment of compensation. The Division of Workers’ Compensation FAQ is the state’s plain-language companion to that rule. Part-time counts. A line cook who works two shifts a week counts. If it is truly just the owners and nobody else is paid, ask before you assume you are exempt. How the company is formed changes that answer, and I would rather look it up with you than have you guess from a group text. Going without coverage when the law required it can bring a stop order and penalties. I am not going to quote a penalty schedule from memory. The statute and the FAQ are linked so you can read them.

When a policy does get written, the class code should match the work. Full table service, counter service, and a bar that is not a restaurant are different WCIRB classifications, and as of September 1, 2026 the bureau’s advisory benchmark for those classes is not the same thing as your premium. That whole story, with the dollar benchmarks and why they are not a bill, is in the market note.

Liquor liability, if you will sell alcohol. Beer and wine count. A Type 41 and a Type 47 both belong in this sentence. General liability usually does not pick up alcohol claims, which is why the separate policy exists. The license, the obviously-intoxicated-minor rule, and the assault-and-battery exclusion that ISO filed for 2026 are written up in the liquor note. Do not let a general liability quote talk you out of reading the liquor exclusion.

A car, if a car is part of the work. A van the business owns is a commercial auto question. An employee using their own car for a catering drop or a supply run is a hired-and-non-owned question. The personal policy in their driveway is not a plan. If nothing leaves the building except the guests, say that. It takes a coverage off the list, which is a good day.

If the building itself is the hard part

Some new restaurants lease a space where the landlord insures the structure and you insure what is inside. Some of you are buying the building. And some of you are signing a lease in a neighborhood where the landlord has already been non-renewed and is hoping you will not notice.

If the only property insurance available on the structure is the California FAIR Plan, treat it as the property policy it is. The Department of Insurance approved commercial limits of $20 million per building and $100 million per location. The plan began offering the high-value version on July 26, 2025. The sample commercial form pays for direct physical loss to covered property. It is not your liability policy, and the plan does not sell you a restaurant package. Budget for general liability, liquor if you pour, and workers’ comp beside it.

The foothill cities I drive, Sierra Madre, parts of Pasadena, Azusa, Glendora, are a different property conversation from a storefront on Huntington Drive in Monrovia or a dining room in San Gabriel. I will not pretend every San Gabriel Valley address is a wildfire account. I will ask, because the carriers do. If you already have a non-renewal in your hand, the wildfire moratorium and what it does not cover are in the market note too.

What to have before we sit down

You will not have all of this. Bring what exists. A thin file is normal for an opening. A made-up precise file is worse than a labeled guess.

  • A sales guess for the first year, and how much of it you think is alcohol. Write “estimate” on it.
  • Payroll by job, even if the first payroll is three people. Kitchen, service, bar.
  • A list of equipment and what the buildout cost, aimed at replacement, not at the bargain you found.
  • The insurance section of the lease, the ABC application or license, and the date someone needs a certificate. Work backward from that date. An ordinary certificate is a few days. An endorsement nobody quoted is longer.
  • The hood-suppression inspection, or the date it is scheduled. Underwriters ask. The fire department will too, for its own reasons.
  • Anything that leaves the building: your van, staff cars, off-site catering, a stall at a weekend market.

I place programs built for a new restaurant, including an opening and a takeover. That is an invitation to bring a thin file. It is not a promise about price. The price is whatever a carrier offers after they see the operation, the building, and the losses. If I cannot place it, I will tell you that, and I will tell you why.

If you are already open and this list is making you nervous, you are not behind in a way that lunch cannot start to fix. Bring the policies you have. I’ll show you what is on them and what is not.
