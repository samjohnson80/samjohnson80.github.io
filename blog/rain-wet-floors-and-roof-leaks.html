A wet sidewalk is three different claims

Halloween weekend put rain on Alhambra. This week is the dry stretch. The guest at the door, the employee on the tile, and the water in the ceiling are three policies.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. Written for owners, not for insurance people.

The question shows up with the umbrella still in the coat check. Somebody tracked the rain in. The mat squished. A guest said the tile was slick, or a dishwasher did, or there is a new stain over the pass. Which page of the policy is supposed to care?

I want that answered while the sidewalk is dry. Monrovia, Arcadia, Pasadena, Sierra Madre, Duarte, Azusa, Glendora, Alhambra, the rest of the valley. The rain does not sort itself into a guest, an employee, and a roof. You do, the same day, or Thursday becomes a story.

What the weekend was, and what this week is

The Los Angeles Times, on the morning of November 2, described a dark Halloween night and showers that lasted into Sunday. Flood advisories went up Saturday night for Ventura, Orange, and San Diego counties. The paper, citing the National Weather Service, said the region came through without a major flash flood or mudslide. Most of the Los Angeles coast and metro area averaged a quarter of an inch to an inch. Alhambra, in this valley, was among the higher totals in the L.A. Basin, about an inch and a half. Downtown Los Angeles was 0.7 of an inch. San Diego, Orange, Riverside, and San Bernardino counties saw a quarter of an inch to three-quarters of an inch over most coastal and inland areas, with more in the mountains.

Then the dry stretch. Devin Black, a meteorologist with the weather service in Oxnard, told the Times to expect generally dry conditions and seasonal temperatures through Thursday. After that, weak to moderate Santa Ana winds could bring a low to moderate heat risk into the weekend, more likely near the coast. Paul Steward, in the San Diego office, said temperatures could reach the mid-80s by the end of the week, and that some wet weather might return the following week. Black called a return toward the middle of November uncertain, and said some models suggest it.

The office’s own discussion, written at 2:48 a.m. Tuesday, November 3, is the tighter version. The National Weather Service in Los Angeles called for dry weather, a warming trend, and offshore winds through the week, warmest and windiest Friday through Sunday, with highs in the lower to mid-80s across almost all of the coasts and valleys by Friday and Saturday. The line it used for the rain after that was plain: the next chance is Wednesday or Thursday, the 11th or the 12th. I am not turning “chance” into a date on the books. If the offshore wind becomes a power question, that is the walk-in note. This one is the water.

The season around that chance is already open, on the county’s calendar. Los Angeles County Public Works says storm season officially begins in mid-October and typically runs through early spring. The same sheet describes El Niño as a greater chance of heavier rain and back-to-back storms in Southern California, and it says a wet winter is not guaranteed. The odds are what they call elevated.

The climate office is more specific, and it is not a promise of a wet dining room. The Climate Prediction Center’s discussion of October 8, 2026, has an El Niño Advisory up. The synopsis: El Niño continues to strengthen, and a strong to very strong El Niño is likely through January–March 2027, remaining greater than an 83 percent chance. The chance of a historic event, one that would exceed previous El Niños back to 1950 on their measure, a three-month relative oceanic Niño index of +2.5°C or more, is 54 percent for September–November, 83 percent for October–December, and 70 percent for November–January. Then the sentence I do not want smoothed over: with an event of this size, the chances of impacts consistent with El Niño are larger, though not guaranteed. The next discussion is scheduled for November 12. The mats do not wait for it.

A caution sign that never comes in is wallpaper. The log is the line that still has a time on it.

The guest at the door

A guest who goes down on the tile is the general liability policy, if that policy is the one on the declarations page. The ISO form I can point at in public is CG 00 01 12 07. A carrier can file its own. A later edition can move a word. The form that was issued is the one that counts.

Coverage A pays the sums you become legally obligated to pay as damages because of bodily injury or property damage the insurance applies to, and it defends a suit seeking those damages. On that form the injury has to be caused by an occurrence, in the coverage territory, during the policy period. A wet floor is not automatically a check. It is a claim that you were responsible for the fall. Who owns the sidewalk, and what the lease says about the entrance, is a fact I want before anyone calls the tile “the restaurant’s.” The lease is the page I already asked you to read in the note on a new place.

Medical payments, Coverage C on the same form, is the smaller door. It pays medical expenses for bodily injury caused by an accident on premises you own or rent, on ways next to premises you own or rent, or because of your operations. The expenses have to be incurred and reported within one year of the accident. The form says these payments are made regardless of fault, and they do not exceed the medical expense limit on the policy. It also says it will not pay when benefits for the injury are payable, or must be provided, under a workers’ compensation law. A guest can fit. A person on the payroll does not, once comp applies. The limit is whatever is typed on your dec page. I will not guess it.

The notice condition is the part that cares about your notes. You see to it that the insurer is notified as soon as practicable of an occurrence that may result in a claim. To the extent you can, the notice includes how, when, and where it happened, the names and addresses of injured people and witnesses, and the nature and location of the injury. If a claim or a suit arrives, you record the specifics and the date you received them, immediately, and you notify the insurer as soon as practicable, in writing. Legal papers go to them. That is the form. A photo of the tile, the mat, and the sign, taken while the floor is still wet, is how those blanks get filled in.

The employee on the same tile

The person on the clock is a different file, even when the water is the same water. Labor Code section 3602 says that where the conditions in section 3600 are met, compensation is the sole and exclusive remedy of the employee against the employer, except where that section, section 3706, or section 4558 says otherwise. The exceptions the statute writes down include a willful physical assault by the employer, fraudulent concealment of the injury, and a defective product the employer made and sold. An ordinary slip on the shift is the compensation claim. I am not your lawyer, and I am not reading an exception into a wet mat.

The liability form points the same direction. CG 00 01 12 07 excludes any obligation under a workers’ compensation law. It also excludes bodily injury to an employee of the insured arising out of and in the course of employment, or while performing duties related to the business, and it applies whether you are liable as an employer or in any other capacity. There is a narrow pass for liability you assumed under an insured contract. Read it if a lease is trying to hand you someone else’s employee. For your own dishwasher, the exclusion is the sentence. The policy that pays the injury is the one in the workers’ comp note. A firing, a harassment complaint, or a wage letter is the other employee claim. A fall is not that form.

The clock for the paperwork is short. Labor Code section 5401 says that within one working day of notice or knowledge of an injury that results in lost time beyond the shift, or in medical treatment beyond first aid, you provide a claim form and a notice of potential eligibility, in person or by first-class mail. The statute’s first aid is one-time treatment, and a follow-up to observe a minor scratch, cut, burn, splinter, or similar injury that does not ordinarily need medical care. The state’s form is the DWC 1. The Division of Workers’ Compensation tells the employee to complete the employee section and give it back. You complete the employer section and send the form to the insurance company. That page, updated June 2025, says the company generally has 14 days to mail a letter on the status of the claim, and that while the claim is being decided the employee may receive up to $10,000 in medical treatment. If you do not deny a filed claim within 90 days, the page says the injury is presumed covered. If the slip needs more than first aid, or the person loses time past that shift, the form goes out within one working day. Write down the time and the floor either way, including a sore ankle that finishes the shift, so Thursday is not a reconstruction.

The floor rules I can cite are written for that crew, not as a guest’s lawsuit. California Title 8, section 3273, says permanent floors and platforms are kept free of dangerous projections or obstructions, in good repair, and reasonably free of oil, grease, or water. Where the work has to happen on a slippery floor, you protect against slipping with mats, grates, cleats, or something equivalent. Where wet processes are used, you keep drainage, and you provide false floors, platforms, mats, or other dry standing places. Yards, walkways, and storage areas stay free of dangerous depressions, obstructions, and debris. Federal OSHA 1910.22 is the same shape: the floor of each workroom clean and, to the extent feasible, dry; mats or other dry standing places where the process is wet; walking surfaces free of leaks, spills, snow, and ice. You inspect them regularly. A hazard is corrected before an employee uses the surface again, or it is guarded until the repair is done.

OSHA’s restaurant eTool adds the sign, pointing at 1910.145(c)(2). A caution sign is for a potential hazard or an unsafe practice, and employees are to be told that it means a possible hazard, so take the precaution. The same eTool’s practical list is the one I want in a pre-shift: non-slip mats or a grit surface, spills cleaned up, uneven floors and bunched mats repaired, aisles clear, and a shoe policy so the people on the wet side of the dish pit are not in slick soles. A mat that has curled up is its own trip. A mat that is soaked through is not a dry standing place.

The ceiling, the drain, and the street

Water in the building is the property policy, and the ISO causes-of-loss form I can point at is CP 10 30 10 12. Special means direct physical loss unless the form excludes it or limits it. A carrier’s form can read differently. I am walking this one because it is public.

The water exclusion is broad, and it applies even when something else contributes. Flood, surface water, waves, tides, overflow of a body of water, spray from any of those, whether or not the wind drives them, including storm surge. Mudslide or mudflow. Water that backs up or overflows or is otherwise discharged from a sewer, drain, sump, sump pump, or related equipment. Water under the ground pressing on, or flowing or seeping through, foundations, walls, floors, paved surfaces, basements, doors, windows, or other openings. Material moved by that water, or by mudslide or mudflow. The form says this is true whether an act of nature caused it or something else did, and it gives a dam or a levee that fails as the example. If any of that results in fire, explosion, or sprinkler leakage, the form pays for the damage that fire, explosion, or sprinkler leakage causes. The water itself stays out.

Three pictures, so the words have a kitchen.

Rain through a roof nothing opened
The interior, and the property inside, are limited when the cause is rain, snow, sleet, or ice, driven by wind or not. The form pays for that interior damage only if the building first sustains damage by a covered cause of loss to the roof or walls the rain comes through, or if the damage is from thawing snow, sleet, or ice on the building. A ceiling stain from a roof that has been tired for a year is this limitation. Wind that actually opens the roof, and then the rain, is a different file, and only if that opening is a covered cause on the form you bought.
A pipe that breaks, or a drip that has a calendar
Wear and tear, rust, decay, deterioration, and hidden defect are excluded. If one of those produces a specified cause of loss, the form can pay for what that specified cause does. Water damage, as this edition defines it, is accidental discharge from the breaking apart or cracking of a plumbing, heating, air conditioning, or other system or appliance on the premises that holds water or steam. A sump system, including its parts, is carved out of that sentence. A supply line that cracks under the sink and dumps water once can be that definition. The form will not pay to repair the defective part the water escaped from. If the water damage is covered, it will pay to tear out and replace building material to get to the damaged part of the system. Then the 14-day sentence: continuous or repeated seepage or leakage of water, or humidity, moisture, or vapor, over a period of 14 days or more, is excluded. A seep that has already lasted 14 days is that exclusion. Finding the stain on a Saturday does not start the count over.
The street, or a drain that comes back up
Surface water and flood sit in the exclusion above, and so does water that backs up or overflows from a sewer, drain, or sump. A floor drain that reverses during a hard rain is the backup sentence, unless an endorsement on your forms list gives it back, with whatever limit that endorsement types. The water-damage definition also says it does not include loss the water exclusion already removes. The form’s own examples: no coverage when a pipe breaks because of weather-driven flooding, even if wear and tear helped, and no coverage for weather-driven flooding that follows a pipe break. Read the endorsement schedule before you decide the drain is “plumbing.”

Once the water is in, the same form excludes neglect: failing to use all reasonable means to save and preserve the property at and after the loss. Move what you can. Photograph the ceiling, the line, and the floor before the fans rearrange the story. Call me the same day. If you have to close, the seats you do not sell are the business-income conversation, and the waiting period on that form is the one I walked in the shutoff note. It is not automatic because the dining room is damp.

Flood is a separate policy. The Insurance Information Institute says damage from flooding, including storm surge, is typically not covered on a standard commercial policy, a package or a businessowners policy included, and that flood insurance is available from the National Flood Insurance Program. FloodSmart, the NFIP’s own page, says the same thing for a business: most business insurance does not cover flood. Commercial building coverage and contents coverage each go up to $500,000. Business interruption is on the list of what flood insurance does not pay. The county’s storm sheet says the homeowner version of this warning, and it says the separate policy can take at least 30 days to go into effect. FloodSmart is the commercial clock I will use: coverage goes into effect 30 days after the purchase date, with four exceptions written on that page. No wait if you buy while making, increasing, extending, or renewing a mortgage. No wait if you change the coverage at renewal. A one-day wait if the property was newly mapped into a high-risk zone and you buy within 12 months of the map change. A one-day wait if a flood is caused or worsened by a wildfire on federal land and you buy within 60 days of containment. Buying it the morning the alley fills is the 30-day sentence, unless one of those exceptions is actually yours.

The two forms do not define the water the same way, and I do not want them mashed together. On the October 2015 NFIP General Property Form, flood means a general and temporary condition of partial or complete inundation of two or more acres of normally dry land, or of two or more properties, one of them yours, from overflow of inland or tidal waters, from unusual and rapid accumulation or runoff of surface waters, or from mudflow. The property form I quoted excludes surface water without asking how many buildings got wet. A sheet of water that comes only through your door can be surface water on CP 10 30, and it can still miss the NFIP definition if it never inundates two acres or a second property. That gap is why I want both pages, not a slogan that “water is covered” or “water is flood.” If a lender in a high-risk zone has already required the flood policy, the Institute’s article is describing that requirement: a commercial building in a high-risk flood area, with a mortgage from a federally regulated or insured lender. If nobody has asked, the map is the way to look. The county sheet tells anyone near a hillside, a canyon, or a recent burn area to check flood and mudflow risk, and to find their flood zone. For mud and debris, Public Works’ number on that sheet is (800) 933-0930.

The Institute also draws a plain line I will not stretch: water from above, their example is rain or melting snow overflowing gutters onto inventory, is generally the commercial property policy, and a flood is not. “Generally” is their word. The interior-rain limitation and the 14-day seepage exclusion are the finer cut, and they live on the property form, not on that sentence. A gutter that overflows into a dining room because the roof opening was already a covered cause is one claim. A gutter that has already been dripping for 14 days or more is another. The street at the threshold is the third.

What I do while the sidewalk is dry

The discussion gives this week, and then a chance of rain on the 11th or the 12th. This is the order, before that chance.

  1. Put a mat where the rain actually enters, and look at it twice a shift. Inside the door, and outside if that is your entrance to maintain. Flat, not curled, not shoved aside for a delivery. When it is soaked, swap it or wring the water out of the path. Title 8 wants the floor reasonably free of water, and a dry standing place where the work is wet. A mat that has become a puddle is not that place. The dish pit and the mop sink count, not only the dining room.
  2. Use a caution sign for the wet floor, and take it in when the floor is dry. OSHA’s caution sign is for a potential hazard. The wording is supposed to be easy to read and accurate. A sign that lives in the corner from November to April teaches the room to walk past it. Pair it with a log: when the floor got wet, who mopped, when the sign went out, when the floor was dry and the sign came in. The standard wants the inspection and the correction. The log is how you still know, on Thursday, what Saturday looked like.
  3. Tell the new hire which shoes belong on the wet side. The eTool’s recommendation is a shoe policy for slip-resistant soles, and a pre-shift that includes wiping a spill when it happens, not at the next lull. Grease and rainwater on the same tile is the slippery floor section 3273 already describes. Show the person the log, not only the sign.
  4. Walk the roof, the gutters, the downspouts, and the drains you can reach. The county sheet’s line is to clean gutters, downspouts, and storm drains, and to repair roof leaks, before the storm. That includes the drain in the alley and the one at the curb if it is yours to clear, and a call to Public Works if the street drain is buried and it is the county’s. Photograph the roof, the scuppers, and the ceiling stains this week, with the date in the photo. If you rent, write down what the lease says about the roof and the sidewalk before you assume either one is yours to fix. A stain you date now is the difference, later, between a new opening and a leak that has already passed 14 days.
  5. If someone goes down, write it before the floor dries. For a guest: the time, the exact spot, what the tile and the mat and the sign looked like, photos, names and phone numbers, what you saw, and notice to the liability insurer as soon as practicable, in the shape the form asks for. For an employee past first aid, or one who loses time beyond the shift: the DWC 1 within one working day, then the carrier. Call me the same day either way. The guest and the employee can fall an hour apart and still be two files.
  6. Then read the policy, before you need it. On the property form, is the water exclusion the one I walked, and is there a sewer or drain backup endorsement with a limit you can find. Is flood a separate policy, and does the 30 days still sit in front of it. On the liability dec page, what is the medical payments limit, and does any endorsement narrow the premises. Is business income on the policy if a leak closes you. If the declarations page is a mystery, text me the photo. That part is free. If the rain pages look fine, I will say so.

If you would rather do this over a plate of food than over a PDF, pick the restaurant. A photo of the entrance and of the ceiling is enough to start. I’ll buy.
