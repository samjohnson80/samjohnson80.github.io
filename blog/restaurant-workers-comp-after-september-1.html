The workers’ comp benchmark moved on September 1

The statewide average is $1.65 per $100 of payroll. The classes on a restaurant policy sit at $2.784. Here is what those numbers are, and what you can still change before the audit.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. Written for owners, not for insurance people.

The question usually shows up before the food does. “Did workers’ comp just go up six and a half percent?” Sometimes the owner is holding a renewal. Sometimes it is a July headline that finally caught a September policy. I would rather walk the documents than hand you a mood.

This is the version for a restaurant, bar, or café in the San Gabriel Valley. Monrovia, Pasadena, Arcadia, Duarte, Azusa, Glendora, Sierra Madre, San Gabriel. Property, the FAIR Plan, and liquor are the other note. This one is the payroll policy.

What the commissioner approved on July 10

On July 10, 2026, Insurance Commissioner Ricardo Lara adopted an average advisory pure premium rate of $1.65 per $100 of payroll. The Department of Insurance said that is a 6.6 percent increase from the 2025 approved rate, in line with the department’s own actuaries and below the 10.4 percent the Workers’ Compensation Insurance Rating Bureau had requested. The new rate is effective September 1, 2026. The WCIRB’s write-up of the same decision says the approved rates apply to policies incepting on or after that date, and that the gap between 10.4 and 6.6 is different assumptions about loss development and trend.

In the filing the bureau sent on April 30, the proposed average was $1.71 per $100 of payroll. The hearing officer’s proposed decision, which the commissioner adopted the same day, is the document that lands on $1.65. The order makes those advisory rates effective September 1, 2026 for new and renewal policies. A policy that already started before that date keeps the rates it started with until it renews.

The filing also puts this year in a longer line. From 2015 to 2024 the approved advisory pure premium rate level fell by more than half. The commissioner’s decision on the September 1, 2025 filing was an average increase of 8.7 percent, and the bureau describes that as the first approved increase in more than a decade. The pressures the bureau named then, higher loss development, rising medical costs, more cumulative trauma claims, and growth in allocated loss-adjustment expense, are the ones it says kept going. Cumulative trauma frequency and average allocated loss-adjustment expense, in the bureau’s words, have reached all-time highs.

I will tell you what the benchmark did. The invoice is a separate piece of paper.

Advisory means the carrier still files its own rate

The department’s press release says the adopted rate is advisory. Insurance companies are not bound by it. They set their own rates. The proposed decision says the same thing with the legal edges on it: an insurer may determine the rates it will use, so long as those rates do not threaten its solvency, are not unfairly discriminatory, and do not tend to create a monopoly. The bureau’s filing letter says an insurer is not required to use either the proposed or the approved pure premium rates when it sets what it charges, and a footnote in that filing says insurers may, and often do, file and use rates other than the ones the commissioner approved.

A pure premium rate is a benchmark for benefit costs and the cost of adjusting claims. The decision says it leaves out underwriting expenses, profit, and a return on the insurer’s investments, and that pure premium rates alone do not set an employer’s premium. Classification, the mix of employees and operations, and the employer’s own claims experience sit on top. The Division of Workers’ Compensation tells owners the same thing in plainer words. Premium rates are not regulated by the state. The bureau recommends rates, carriers file their own with the department, and what you pay turns on your classification, your experience modification, and your payroll.

The department’s own guide, revised June 23, 2025, walks the arithmetic. Estimated payroll in a classification, per $100, times the rate the carrier filed for that class, is the base premium. Experience modification, and usually a schedule or judgment factor, moves that base up or down. That product is an estimate. The final premium waits for the audit. I am going to tell you the statewide benchmark moved 6.6 percent. Your dining room is a different page.

The restaurant classes sit higher

Kitchens cut, burn, and slip, so the classes I see on restaurant and bar policies sit above that $1.65 average. On the bureau’s classification pages for full service, fast food or fast casual, and bars or taverns, the approved advisory pure premium rate effective September 1, 2026 is $2.784 per $100 of payroll. Those same pages show $2.530 for September 1, 2025, and $2.410 for September 1, 2024. From $2.530 to $2.784 is a step of about 10 percent. That is a bigger step than the statewide 6.6 percent. It is still a loss benchmark per $100 of payroll.

When the bureau retired the old restaurant class, it said the new classifications would share one advisory pure premium rate until it had enough payroll and loss data to justify a difference. The pages matching is the plan. I am not going to pretend a counter in Alhambra and a white-tablecloth room in Pasadena produce the same claims. I am going to tell you the benchmark has not split them yet.

9079 went away in September 2024

Until September 1, 2024, almost every dining room in California sat in one class, 9079(1), Restaurants or Taverns. The bureau eliminated it for policies incepting on or after that date and sent the work to six classifications: 9080, full service; 9081(1), restaurants not otherwise classified; 9082, caterers that are not restaurants; 9083, fast food or fast casual; 9084, bars or taverns that are not restaurants; and 9058, the food and beverage employees inside a hotel, motel, or short-term residential housing. A policy that started before September 1, 2024 stayed on 9079 until it was replaced by a policy that started on or after that date. If a dec page still says 9079 in the fall of 2026, that is a question for the renewal, not a rounding error.

The bureau’s stated goal was a closer look at payroll and claims in each kind of operation, so the cost could eventually line up with the exposure, and so loss prevention could aim at the injuries that segment actually has. While the classes stay on one pure premium rate, the same page says reassignment among them has no immediate effect on experience rating, because they share an expected loss rate. The premium still moves with payroll, with claims, and with the rate your carrier filed.

The class is a description of the work. On 9080, full table service includes greeting and reservations, seating, taking the order at the table, serving, clearing, and collecting payment at the end. Musicians and entertainers you employ are in it. You may pour. On 9083, the guest orders at a counter, a drive-through, a kiosk, or an app, pays at the time of the order, and seats themselves if they stay. Bringing the paid order over, refilling a glass, and clearing the table are still 9083. The bureau says a restaurant with full table service goes to 9080.

Two edges show up on real policies in this valley. If you brew on site and 50 percent or more of that beer is sold for off-site consumption, the brewing is classification 2121, Breweries. If more than half the receipts from baked goods go to wholesale customers, the baking is 2004, Baked Goods Mfg. — wholesale. And the restaurant classes apply to each separate and distinct restaurant. A sit-down room and a counter concept under the same roof can be two codes. The payroll has to be split to match the work.

What the department said is pushing the cost

The commissioner’s press release names the drivers in one sentence. Higher medical treatment and medical-legal costs, a greater number of projected cumulative trauma claims, and escalating costs of adjusting claims have produced deteriorating accident-year combined ratios. At the same time, the rates insurers have been charging stayed low. Wage increases from a growing economy offset some of the higher cost.

The decision he adopted goes further on cumulative trauma. It describes a 2026 WCIRB study: those claims are increasing and becoming more expensive, particularly the ones filed after someone is let go, which are highly likely to be litigated. They generate higher medical-legal and interpreter costs early, and a growing share show no medical payments at 18 months. Total pure premium costs for cumulative trauma claims, as the department wrote it, now account for about one-quarter of all pure premium costs.

On medical-legal fees, the same decision says the bureau’s review of the April 1, 2021 medical-legal fee schedule found those costs up 50 percent under the new schedule, higher than earlier estimates, mostly from record-review charges and from more medical-legal services per claim. The department writes that this works out to about a 3.2 percent increase in total medical costs. That is a fee-schedule finding inside a benchmark study. It is a line in a hearing record, and it belongs in the conversation about why the benchmark moved. It is not a line on your invoice.

What you can still do at renewal

The medical-legal fee schedule is not in the building. A few things are.

  1. Read the class code. Ask which code is on the policy, and whether the payroll is split the way the floor actually runs. Full service, a counter, a bar that is not a restaurant, a truck that cooks, catering that is its own operation: those are different descriptions. A wrong bucket is worth ten minutes before you argue about the rate.
  2. Make the payroll estimate honest, and sit for the audit. The department’s guide says the final premium cannot be calculated until the term is over and the payroll records have been audited. If payroll ran above the estimate, you owe the difference. If it ran below, the company owes you a return premium. Restaurants swing with the season and with a second concept. Some carriers will take monthly payroll. If yours will not, tell the underwriter when the number moves during the year, so October is not a surprise. Insurers generally may audit during the term and for three years after it ends. Skipping the audit can lead to cancellation or nonrenewal. The guide also says an employer who will not allow the audit may be liable for a premium equal to three times the estimated premium, and that the bureau can build an experience modification with the claims included and the unaudited payroll left out, which typically raises the modification from prior years. Deliberate under-reporting is insurance fraud. I want the right payroll in the right class. I do not want a clever one.
  3. Know whether you even have an experience modification. The bureau’s plain explanation is that a modification compares actual losses to expected losses. Actual losses are the medical and indemnity costs the insurer has paid or expects to pay. Expected losses are what a business of your size, in your classifications, is statistically expected to cost. The simple ratio is actual divided by expected. In practice the formula has extra pieces so one bad year does not swing a small employer off the chart. The department’s guide says a modification under 100 percent is better than average for that size and industry, and one over 100 percent is worse, and that it is applied to the base premium. For modifications on the plan effective September 1, 2026, the Experience Rating Plan sets the eligibility threshold at $11,700 of expected losses, up from $10,800. The experience period is three years of completed policies. It starts four years and nine months before the modification date and stops one year and nine months before it. The claim from last Saturday is not in the factor on the renewal you are holding. It will be in a later one. A smaller payroll may not be eligible at all. If you have a mod, ask for the worksheet.
  4. The injuries the kitchen actually produces. Cal/OSHA’s restaurant guide, August 2024, lists the common injury types: sprains and strains from slips, trips, falls, and from lifting, bending, and twisting; cuts, lacerations, and punctures from knives and other cutting tools; burns and scalds from hot objects, open flame, oil, and water. The practical list in that guide is ordinary, which is why it works. Clean a spill when it happens, and put a sign down while the floor is wet. Closed-toe shoes with slip-resistant soles and low heels. Do not leave oil unattended, do not carry it while it is hot, let it cool before you drain or strain it, and keep water and ice out of it. Keep knives sharp, use a cutting board, stay in control of the blade, and where it is feasible use a cut-resistant glove. Do not stand on a chair, a cart, or a bucket. The violation Cal/OSHA names first is an Injury and Illness Prevention Program that is not actually running. Title 8, section 3203 wants hazards identified, corrected, and trained. A binder nobody has opened is not the program.
  5. Bring people back when the doctor says they can work. The Division of Workers’ Compensation says getting employees back to work is one of the most important things you can do for their health and for the business. Workers who return as soon as medically possible recover faster and lose less in wages. The employer FAQ says to provide transitional work, light duty, whenever it is appropriate. If the doctor says the person cannot work, they cannot be required to come back. If the doctor writes restrictions and you can change the tasks to match, say so in writing and keep the note. For injuries on or after January 1, 2013, the department’s guide says an employee with permanent disability who is not timely offered regular, modified, or alternative work is owed a supplemental job displacement voucher, redeemable for up to $6,000. That is a benefit rule. It is not a discount I can promise on the premium. What I can say is that the modification is built from what claims cost, and time away from work is part of the indemnity in that cost.
  6. Hand over the claim form the day you know. The employer FAQ says you give the workers’ compensation claim form within one working day after the injury or illness is reported. When it comes back, you return a copy to the employee within one working day, and you send the form and your report of the injury to the claims administrator within one working day of receiving it. Within one working day of receiving the claim, you authorize up to $10,000 in appropriate medical treatment. The injured worker’s FAQ says that if you do not learn about the injury within 30 days, and that delay keeps you from investigating how it happened, the worker can lose the right to benefits. Tell the crew that on the first shift. A late story is a worse file, for them and for you.

If you are opening rather than renewing, workers’ comp starts the day you have an employee. That list is the other note. The department’s guide cites Labor Code section 3700: if the business employs one or more people, it has to secure the benefits. Part-time counts.

If you would rather do this over a plate of food than over a PDF, pick the restaurant. Bring the dec page, or a photo of the class code and the modification. I’ll buy.
