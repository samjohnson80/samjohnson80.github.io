The million on the certificate does not cover the fight.

A bar, or a dining room that turns into one after ten, can show a million dollars on the certificate and still be holding the lawsuit if two guests fight, or if someone on the clock steps in. Read the endorsement before the holiday parties.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. Written for owners, not for insurance people. I am not your lawyer.

The book for New Year’s Eve is already on a clipboard somewhere. A Pasadena dining room that keeps the bar open after the kitchen closes. A place in Alhambra with a patio that outlasts the tables. A Monrovia or Arcadia spot that puts one person at the door on December 31 because last year the sidewalk got loud. The claim people describe to me in November is not the wet mat. It is two guests, and then the employee who tried to end it.

I am not going to tell you how many policies exclude that night. I do not have a count I would sign. I can show you the two shapes the wording takes, and the pages to pull before the first party. One shape takes the fight off the policy. The other leaves it on, at a number smaller than the million, and sometimes lets the lawyers’ bills eat that number. Your policy is one of those, or it is neither. The certificate will not say which.

The dining-room form already sent the alcohol away

Start with the form under the certificate, not the certificate. On CG 00 01 04 13, the commercial general liability coverage form, there is no coverage for bodily injury or property damage for which any insured may be held liable by reason of causing or contributing to someone’s intoxication, furnishing alcohol to a person under the legal drinking age or under the influence of alcohol, or a statute, ordinance, or regulation on the sale, gift, distribution, or use of alcoholic beverages. That exclusion still applies if the lawsuit is dressed up as negligent supervision, hiring, employment, training, or monitoring, when the occurrence involved one of those three. It applies if you are in the business of manufacturing, distributing, selling, serving, or furnishing alcoholic beverages. A bar is that business. A restaurant that pours is that business. Letting someone bring their own bottle, by itself, is not.

So a fight that the complaint ties to the pouring is often sent off the general liability form and onto the liquor form, if you bought one. FC&S, writing about liquor liability form CG 00 33 04 13, says that form pays injury imposed because of selling, serving, or furnishing any alcoholic beverage, and that it picks up the liquor exposure the general liability form takes out. A fight the complaint ties to the door, the lighting, or the lack of anyone watching the sidewalk can stay on the general liability form, because that theory is not the pour. One night, two stories, two policies. They can answer differently. The license, the obviously-intoxicated-minor rule, and the short version of the exclusion are in the Type 41 and Type 47 note. This note is the fight.

The endorsement that takes the fight off both

ISO filed assault-or-battery exclusions with a January 1, 2026 edition date. FC&S, in PropertyCasualty360 on October 6, 2025, walked through the wording. CG 40 51 01 26 attaches to the commercial general liability coverage part. CG 35 09 01 26 attaches to the liquor liability coverage part. I wrote the market version of this in the fall note. Here is the part that matters on a Saturday in December.

Where CG 40 51 is attached, the insurance does not apply to bodily injury or property damage arising out of an actual, alleged, or threatened assault or battery committed by anyone. It also does not apply to any act or omission by any insured in connection with preventing or suppressing the assault or battery. And it does not apply to negligence or other wrongdoing in hiring, training, employment, investigation, supervision, monitoring, reporting to the proper authorities or failing to report, or retention, of a person the insured is or ever was legally responsible for, whose conduct would be excluded by the first two pieces. The same three pieces are added to personal and advertising injury. CG 35 09 puts the same three pieces on the liquor form, and it excludes “injury,” which is the liquor form’s word, not “property damage.”

Read “anyone.” A guest on a guest. Your closer. The person at the door. Someone you never hired. Read the second piece before you tell the staff to step in. Trying to stop the fight is inside the exclusion, not a way around it. Read the third piece before you tell yourself the lawsuit will be about training, not about the punch. The training claim is named.

The forms do not define assault or battery. FC&S says the words take their ordinary meaning, and it quotes a dictionary. Assault, in the legal sense they quote, is a threat or attempt to inflict offensive physical contact that puts the person in immediate apprehension of it. Battery is an offensive touching or use of force without consent. I am not handing you a California jury instruction. I am telling you the form did not give you a narrower definition to hide in.

FC&S’s point, which matches the filing, is that these are underwriting tools. Not every carrier uses the ISO form. Some policies already had their own exclusion. Adding either endorsement to a policy that did not have it makes the coverage smaller. If the endorsement is on the policy, the base form’s kinder sentence does not save you. On CG 00 01, and on the liquor form as FC&S quotes it, injury expected or intended from the standpoint of the insured is excluded, except bodily injury resulting from reasonable force to protect persons or property. An endorsement that excludes the act of preventing or suppressing the fight is written over that exception. Reasonable force is not a magic word once the endorsement is attached.

The certificate is the number for the slip. The fight is the page attached after it.

The smaller number, and who pays the lawyers

The other shape is a limit that sits under the million. I will not invent a typical cap, and I will not call a dollar figure common. I have read one limited endorsement because a court reprinted it. It is not a California case, it is not your policy, and it is not a price.

The West Virginia Supreme Court of Appeals, in a 2014 opinion on a bar’s policy, printed a “Limited Assault or Battery Coverage” endorsement. The general liability limit in that opinion is $1 million per occurrence. The endorsement scheduled $25,000 per event and $25,000 aggregate, for the general liability, professional liability, and liquor liability coverage parts together, and it said those limits are not in addition to the other limits on the policy. It agreed to pay bodily injury arising out of an assault, a battery, or a physical altercation, whether or not an employee, a patron, or someone else was involved, whether or not it came from failing to supervise or keep the premises safe, and whether or not it came from trying to prevent or suppress it. Then it said the most it would pay for damages and for supplementary payments is that per-event number, and that any supplementary payments reduce the limits shown.

The court left the $25,000 cap in place, including where the claim was framed as negligence and a failure to keep the premises safe. The bar had not appealed the finding that lawyers’ fees and costs reduce what is left of the $25,000. It appealed the next step: the lower court’s ruling that the insurer could stop defending once those fees used the $25,000 up. The Supreme Court reversed that part. The endorsement never clearly said the duty to defend ends when the bills eat the limit. The general liability form said the duty to defend ends when the limit has been used up paying judgments or settlements, and it said supplementary payments will not reduce the limits. The court called the pages contradictory and read them for the bar.

That is the lesson I want, and it is smaller than the story. On the ISO general liability form I can open, CG 00 01 04 13, the company pays the expenses it incurs defending a suit it defends, and those supplementary payments will not reduce the limits of insurance. The duty to defend ends when the company has used up the applicable limit in the payment of judgments or settlements, not when the invoices arrive. FC&S quotes the same two ideas on liquor form CG 00 33 04 13: supplementary payments will not reduce the limits, and the duty to defend ends when the limit is used up paying judgments or settlements. An endorsement can rewrite both. If yours says defense costs, claims expense, or supplementary payments reduce the assault limit, the money left for the injured person is smaller than the number in the schedule. If it also says the defense stops when that number is gone, ask someone to show you the sentence. A muddy page lost that argument in another state. I will not tell you a California court will read yours the same way. I will tell you to have both sentences in front of you before December 31.

Two more lines on the same ISO form, because owners hope a small page will still pay the ambulance. Medical payments on CG 00 01 do not pay an injury that is excluded under Coverage A. An assault exclusion added to Coverage A is worth reading against that sentence. And the employer’s liability exclusion takes bodily injury to an employee arising out of employment, or out of duties related to the business, off this form, whether you are liable as an employer or in any other capacity. The person on your clock who gets hurt is a different file. That one is the workers’ comp note.

The person at the door

On CG 00 01, your employees are insureds, other than executive officers or managers in the cases the form spells out, but only for acts within the scope of their employment or while performing duties related to the business. They are not insureds for bodily injury to a co-employee in the course of the job. A closer who steps between two guests may be an insured for that act, on the base form, until an assault endorsement says the insurance does not apply to an assault committed by anyone, or to the act of trying to stop one. A guard who works for a security company is that company’s employee, not yours. Whether your policy covers you for what they do, and whether their policy covers the night, are two questions. Bring the contract and both forms. I will not guess which one answers.

The license is a separate question from the coverage, and it is a California question. Business and Professions Code section 7574.01, amended effective January 1, 2025, defines a proprietary private security officer as an unarmed person employed exclusively by one employer, whose primary duty is security for that employer, whose services are not contracted out, who is not exempt under section 7582.2, and who meets both of these: a distinctive uniform that clearly identifies them as a security officer, and work that is likely to include interacting with the public. Section 7582.2(a), also amended effective January 1, 2025, says the private-security-services chapter does not apply to a person who does not meet that definition, is employed exclusively and regularly by an employer that does not provide contract security to others, works only in that employer’s affairs, is an employee, and at no time carries or uses a deadly weapon. The statute’s list of deadly weapons includes a firearm, a knife with a blade longer than five inches, and a metal pipe or bar used or intended as a club.

The Bureau of Security and Investigative Services FAQ, revised January 2025, puts the registration point in plain words. With some exceptions, registration is required when the person works for one employer, the primary duty is security, the services are not contracted out, and both of these are true: a distinctive uniform that clearly identifies them as a security officer, and they are likely to interact with the public. The same FAQ says a proprietary private security officer cannot carry a firearm, a baton, or pepper spray. Before registration, the training it lists is three hours of power to arrest and a minimum of five hours on the appropriate use of force. It also says the person cannot work on a temporary registration. A valid registration has to be in hand before the shift. The bureau’s December 1, 2016 chart used an unarmed bouncer who is not in a distinctive security uniform as an example of someone outside the license. I am citing it as the bureau’s example, not as a newer statute. The uniform test I am using is the one in the current code and the 2025 FAQ.

A black shirt and a calm voice is one fact. A shirt that says security, on a person whose job is the door, is the other fact. Tell the carrier which one you have on December 31, and whether that person is on your payroll or on a company’s. A registration does not create coverage. A missing registration does not get cured by a policy. I am not claiming a discount for getting the registration right. I am claiming the application will ask, and the exclusion may not care what the shirt says.

What to have in place before the parties

None of this buys the endorsement back. It is the part you can do in the building, and it is the part the state already wrote down for people who pour.

Business and Professions Code section 25680 is Assembly Bill 1221’s definition. An alcohol server is a person who serves alcoholic beverages for consumption on licensed premises, or a person who manages or supervises that person. The training the section describes includes intervention, and management policies, aimed at not serving minors and not serving intoxicated patrons. Section 25682 says that beginning July 1, 2022, a licensee subject to the article shall not employ or keep employing an alcohol server who does not have a valid certification. Someone hired on or after that date has 60 calendar days from employment. The licensee has to keep the certification records for the department to inspect. The ABC’s RBS page is the current how-to. Register in the portal, take training from an approved provider, and pass ABC’s exam within 30 days of the confirmed training. The certification is good for three years, and it has to be renewed before it expires. A provider’s certificate, by itself, is not the ABC certification. In the approved RBS regulations, section 170, the server has to answer at least 70 percent of the exam correctly. Look at the roster before you write the holiday schedule. A certification that expires on December 20 is not a certification on December 31.

The cut-off is a misdemeanor if you miss it, and a script if you make it. Section 25602(a) makes it a misdemeanor to sell or give alcohol to a habitual drunkard or to an obviously intoxicated person. The ABC’s intoxication page says a person is obviously intoxicated when the average person can plainly see it. That includes the regular who “always acts that way.” It does not matter whether the person is driving. The prosecutor has to prove the seller saw the signs, or had the chance to see them, before the service. The RBS curriculum, section 165, says to refuse service when the ID is invalid, when the patron becomes belligerent, when the patron is obviously intoxicated by alcohol, medication, or recreational or illegal drugs, when they want service outside operating hours, or when the server has any doubt. The technique in that section is spoken. Be courteous and firm. Be quiet, and not a scene. Be assertive. Talk about the server’s duty not to serve. Do not bargain. Tell the manager and the coworkers who the person is and why service stopped. Tell the rest of the group. Offer water, a non-alcoholic drink, or food.

That is the cut-off I want written down and taught. It is not a hand on a shoulder, walking someone to the curb because the door was short a person. I am not your lawyer, and I am not saying a person who is being hurt has to wait on a speech. I am saying the curriculum’s refusal is words, and the ISO endorsement, if it is on your policy, reaches “any act or omission by any insured in connection with the prevention or suppression” of an assault or battery. The employee who grabs a guest to end a fight is inside that sentence. ABC’s community-relations page says to cooperate and to call law enforcement when you have a serious problem. The same page says too many calls for service are grounds for suspension or revocation, and that a licensee who is always calling needs to change the operation. The changes it lists are more employees, closing sooner, hiring security guards, changing the music, or increasing the lighting. Dragging a guest out is not on that list. Section 166 of the RBS regulations says the same thing about frequent calls, and it adds that written policies should be reviewed when violence keeps requiring a peace officer.

The ID check is the same curriculum, and it is a statute. Section 165 says twenty-one is the age, and it says a diligent inspection that reasonably concludes the ID is legally acceptable can be a defense to a sale-to-a-minor charge when the person could have been twenty-one or older. A legally acceptable ID, in that section, is issued by a government agency and has the name, date of birth, photograph, and physical description, and a valid expiration date. Military IDs and passports are listed even without the physical description. Expired IDs, school or work IDs, an obviously altered ID, an ID that belongs to someone else, and an interim or temporary driver’s license are on the unacceptable list. The technique is to hold the ID, look at the front and the back, compare the photograph and the description to the person, and ask a question the card can answer. Section 25659 says a licensee, or an agent or employee, may refuse to sell or serve if the person cannot produce adequate written evidence of being over twenty-one. The same section allows seizure of an ID that shows the person is under twenty-one or that is false, if a receipt is given and the ID is turned over within 24 hours to the local law enforcement agency with jurisdiction. Choosing not to seize does not, on that section, create civil or criminal liability.

Write the night down before the shift ends. The ABC intoxication page says an incident log may be the greatest asset if a lawsuit is filed, and it says to fill it out immediately, not at the end of the shift and not the next day. It wants a report when you refuse a sale to an intoxicated customer, when you arrange a ride, when a minor presents a false ID, when ABC or the police visit, and whenever you call the police. The department’s blank is form ABC-607. Section 166 of the RBS regulations adds the list the training is supposed to cover: service stopped, a ride arranged, an ID seized, a patron ill from alcohol, any injury or medical treatment or hospitalization, any illegal or violent incident, and any call to law enforcement. One log, that night, with names if you have them. A story told on Monday is a worse exhibit.

I did not find a statewide ABC rule that a San Gabriel Valley bar has to hang cameras, and I will not borrow another city’s permit condition and paste it onto Monrovia. What I want, as a practical matter, is a camera that sees the door and the sidewalk, that is actually recording on December 31, and a number you know: how many days the system keeps the night. The log says what happened. The picture is how you show it. If the recorder overwrites in two days, the week between Christmas and New Year’s will eat the clip before anyone asks. Lighting is the item ABC already put on the list. A dark doorway is a worse conversation with a carrier and with a lawyer. I am not claiming a camera prevents the fight.

What I want on the table

This is the order, while the parties are still a reservation list.

  1. The declarations, and the list of forms. Search for assault, battery, CG 40 51, CG 35 09, or whatever number your carrier used. Do it on the general liability and on the liquor form. The certificate is not the search.
  2. Three answers, in writing. Is the fight excluded, capped, or at the same limit as everything else. If it is capped, what is the per-event number, what is the aggregate, and is that aggregate shared by every location and by both coverage parts. The $25,000 in the opinion above is one policy’s schedule. It is not a guess at yours.
  3. The defense sentence. Do defense costs, claims expense, or supplementary payments reduce the assault limit. When does the duty to defend end: on judgments or settlements, or when the bills use the number up. On CG 00 01 and on CG 00 33, as quoted above, the payments do not reduce the limits, and the duty ends on judgments or settlements. Your endorsement may say otherwise. Ask whether anything above the policy follows the exclusion or follows the smaller limit. I will not pretend an umbrella does that, or does not, until I read it.
  4. Who the wording applies to. Employees. A contracted guard. A guest on a guest. The sidewalk and the lot, or only the room. Preventing the fight, or only throwing the punch. Negligent hiring and supervision, or only the intentional act.
  5. The roster and the door. Who is RBS certified, and whose card expires before New Year’s Eve. Who is at the door, on your payroll or on a company’s, in a security uniform or not, armed with anything or not. The registration, if section 7574.01 describes the job.
  6. The script and the log. The cut-off in the words section 165 already uses. A stack of ABC-607, or a log with the same blanks. The camera’s retention, written on the same page as the manager’s name. Last year’s log if you have it, including the nights you walked someone out and did not call.

If the forms say the fight is excluded, that is the answer, and the work in the building is still worth doing for the license and for the people in the room. If the forms say the fight is covered at the full limit, I will say so. I will not promise that asking the question leaves the premium where it is. Coverage depends on the wording of the policy that is actually issued.

Lunch is the easier way to do this. Bring the dec page and the forms list. Tell me what December 31 actually looks like: last call, the door, the patio, who is working. I’ll buy.
