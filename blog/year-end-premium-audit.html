The estimate is not the audit.

Workers’ comp is priced on payroll. General liability may be priced on sales. The deposit was an estimate of one of those numbers. Here is what a year-end audit is allowed to count, and what to pull before the letter comes.

By Sam Johnson, account executive at Shaw, Moses, Mendenhall & Associates. Written for owners, not for insurance people.

The call usually comes after the holidays, when the dining room is quiet and somebody has finally opened the mail. The letter is not a renewal. It is the audit of a policy that already ended, or of one that is about to. The deposit cleared months ago. The deposit was the estimate we put on the policy when it started. The audit is the payroll, or the sales, the place actually ran between those two dates. If the year ran ahead of the estimate, there is an additional premium. If it ran behind, there can be money coming back. A policy that expires in March gets the same letter in the spring. The records are the policy year, whichever month it starts.

This is the version for a restaurant, bar, or café in the San Gabriel Valley. The class codes, the September 1 benchmark, and what happened to classification 9079 are the workers’ comp note. The January wage, and the longer version of the payroll estimate, are the other note. This one is the audit.

The deposit was the estimate. The audit is the year you ran.

Two policies, two bases

Workers’ comp and general liability do not audit the same column.

On workers’ comp, the Department of Insurance guide, revised June 23, 2025, walks the arithmetic in order. Estimated payroll in a classification, per $100, times the rate the carrier filed for that class, is the base premium. Experience modification, and usually a schedule or judgment factor, moves that base. The product is an estimate. The final premium waits until the term is over and the payroll records have been audited. Payroll above the estimate, you owe the difference. Payroll below the estimate, the company owes a return premium. Some carriers will take payroll every month. If yours will not, the guide says to work with the broker or the underwriter when the number moves during the year. A corrected estimate during the term, in the department’s words, can help keep a large audit bill, or a large return, from hitting cash flow all at once.

On general liability, the department’s commercial guide, Form 700, revised June 14, 2024, says the rating formula can be square footage, payroll, or gross sales, depending on the classification codes. It calls those the rating exposures, and it gives the arithmetic as rate times exposure. Read the declarations. The column that names the basis is the column the auditor is going to ask for. I will not tell you every dining room in Monrovia is rated on sales. I will tell you to read that column before you pull a report.

A general liability form in wide use treats the first premium as a deposit. Sonoma County has posted a sample of Insurance Services Office form CG 00 01 04 13. The premium-audit condition says the company computes premium under its own rules and rates, the advance premium is a deposit only, and at the close of each audit period it computes the earned premium and sends notice. If the advance premium and any audit premium already paid add up to more than the earned premium, the company returns the excess. The first named insured has to keep the records the computation needs, and send copies when asked. Your policy may be that edition, a later one, or a businessowners form with its own condition. The sentence that governs you is the one in your form. The audit prices the year that already happened. Whether a guest’s claim is covered is a different question, and it is the wording of the policy.

What the bureau counts as payroll

The payroll rules are in the California Workers’ Compensation Uniform Statistical Reporting Plan—1995. That plan is Title 10 of the California Code of Regulations, section 2318.6, and the edition I am using is effective September 1, 2026. The plan says an amendment applies to a policy with an effective date on or after the amendment, unless the plan provides otherwise. Payroll and remuneration mean the same thing: the money, and the substitutes for money, earned during the policy period by covered employees and officers, and by anyone else the policy covers voluntarily. The opening of the rule names gross wages, salaries, commissions, bonuses, overtime, the market value of gifts, and vacation, holiday, and sick pay. The bureau’s plain page is the short version of the same list. A restaurant audit usually turns on a handful of those lines, so I am staying with those.

Tips, voluntary or prepaid, received from someone other than the employer are not payroll. A service charge the house sets, collects, and then pays through payroll was not received from someone other than the employer. Keep the guest’s tip and the house charge in different columns. The auditor should be able to see which dollars came from which place. The January note already walks tips next to the wage change. The point here is the column.

The straight-time part of overtime stays in payroll. The premium portion, meaning the amount above the regular rate, comes out only when the records show it separately, by employee, and in summary by the kind of work. The plan allows two layouts. One is pay at the regular rate for all hours worked, plus the premium portion on its own. The other is pay at the regular rate for the non-overtime hours, plus the total paid for the overtime hours. Extra pay for a swing shift or a graveyard shift is not overtime under that rule, so it stays in. If the records do not isolate the premium portion, the exclusion is not available and the overtime payment stays in the payroll. That is the whole trick, and it is a records trick. The January note is where I tied the same rule to the wage that moves on New Year’s Day.

The value of a meal stays out of payroll unless the classification says to include it, the paycheck was reduced by the value of the meal, or the meal was provided in place of wages. A family meal after service is usually none of those three, so it stays out. A meal you deduct from the check comes back in, because the wages were reduced to pay for it.

Money an employee defers into a qualified retirement plan is still payroll, because it was earned and then set aside. The employer’s own contribution to that plan is not payroll. Subtracting the 401(k) line from gross wages, and handing the auditor the remainder, takes out dollars the plan still counts.

A payment the person receives only because the job ended is generally outside payroll. Accrued vacation, sick pay, commissions, and bonuses paid on the way out are inside it, when those sums would have been paid later if the person had stayed. December is when both kinds of money land on one check. Split the check in the register before the auditor has to.

The owner is a floor and a ceiling

If you are covered, the plan does not stop at the draw you took. It starts with your remuneration, and then it applies a minimum and a maximum per person. For executive officers, partners, individual employers, and members of a limited liability company, the September 1, 2026 plan sets the minimum at $66,300 a year and the maximum at $171,600 a year. The bureau’s table shows the prior step, effective September 1, 2025, at $63,700 and $165,100. The limitation follows the effective date of the policy. A policy that started before September 1, 2026 keeps the limitation it started with until it renews. The audit does not reach back and reprice the owner at the newer floor.

The figures below are annual, per covered person. On a phone, swipe for the amounts. These are payroll caps for the audit, not the price of the policy.

The plan prorates the floor and the ceiling when the person held that role for only part of the term. Its own example is a partner who joins at week 27 of a 52-week policy, draws $400 a week, and takes nothing else. That is $10,400. The prorated minimum is $66,300 divided by 52, times 26 weeks, which the plan states as $33,150. Reportable payroll is raised by $22,750, from the draw up to $33,150. A small draw is still the minimum if the person is covered. Pay above the maximum is cut back to the maximum, and that cap prorates the same way.

Whether you are on the policy at all is Labor Code section 3352. An officer or director who owns at least 10 percent of the stock can be excluded, with a written waiver the carrier accepts. An officer who owns at least 1 percent can be excluded when a parent, grandparent, sibling, spouse, or child owns at least 10 percent, the officer has a health plan, and the carrier accepts the waiver. The waiver takes effect when the carrier receives and accepts it. The carrier may backdate that acceptance up to 15 days. It stays in effect until the person withdraws it in writing. A sole shareholder who is an officer or director of a private corporation is already outside the definition of employee, unless that person or the corporation has elected to be covered under section 4151(a). The bureau’s page dates that sole-shareholder treatment to July 1, 2018. A general partner, or a managing member of a limited liability company, needs the waiver.

If the policy specifically excludes the person, the plan leaves that person’s payroll out of remuneration. The minimum is for someone the policy covers. The page to find before the auditor does is the waiver, or the election, with the date the carrier accepted it. A belief that you are off the policy, with no accepted waiver in the file, is how an owner who took no paycheck still picks up the floor.

An individual employer who is covered is assigned without division. The plan puts that person’s entire remuneration on the highest rated classification that describes any duty they did for any part of the time, or on the governing classification if that one is the higher of the two. A sole proprietor who is on the policy and also works a station does not get to park the owner payroll in a clerical class because the books live in the office. Which restaurant description fits the work, including the retirement of 9079, is the September note. I am not reprinting the class table here.

A 1099 does not decide it

Labor Code section 3357 says a person rendering service for another, other than as an independent contractor, or unless the code expressly excludes them, is presumed to be an employee. Section 2775 applies to the Labor Code. A person providing labor or services for pay is an employee unless the hiring entity shows all three conditions: the person is free from your control, in the contract and in fact; the work is outside the usual course of your business; and the person customarily does that work in an independent business of the same kind. A cook, a server, or a dishwasher is the usual course of a restaurant. The second condition is the one that fails. A form that says 1099 does not move the pay off the audit. The pay is payroll.

A licensed trade is the other statute. Section 2750.5 covers work that requires a contractor’s license. There is a presumption that the worker is an employee, and holding a valid license is a condition of independent-contractor status for that work. The section says this presumption supplements the workers’ compensation definitions and is not meant to shrink coverage. An electrician, a plumber, or the contractor on a buildout is that kind of work. I will not guess from the sidewalk whether the person who services the hood needs a license. If the work requires one and they do not hold it, the statute does not treat them as an independent contractor.

Insurance Code section 11760.1 describes the audit as the way the insurer determines remuneration earned by your employees and by any uninsured subcontractors and the employees of those subcontractors, during the policy period. State Compensation Insurance Fund’s construction audit guide, form 6903, revised May 2026, tells its construction policyholders to get a workers’ compensation certificate from subcontractors who have employees of their own. If the certificate is missing and there is no valid license, the guide says the subcontractor may be treated as an employee, and premium charged on the trade and on the amounts paid. A café in Arcadia is not the audience on that guide. The Insurance Code sentence is not limited to job sites. The folder I want, before the person starts, is a certificate that covers the dates they were in the building, and the license when the work needs one. A 1099 for a Friday night on the line is a different folder. It is the payroll folder.

When the time cards do not split the work

The plan will divide one employee’s pay between two classifications only when you kept complete and accurate records, supported by original time cards or a time book, showing the pay separately by employee and in summary by the operation. Percentages, averages, and estimates are not a division. If those records are missing, the employee’s entire remuneration goes to the highest rated classification that applies to any part of the work they did. If the books are not detailed enough to classify the payroll at all, the plan assigns the total remuneration to the highest rated classification that describes any part of the work.

The restaurant classes are still sharing one advisory pure premium rate. That comparison, and the reason a counter and a dining room can still be two descriptions, is the September note. Sharing a benchmark is why moving a server from one restaurant description to another may not change the pure premium rate. It can still matter when part of the work is not a restaurant class, when the carrier’s filed rates are not the same, or when the description on the policy is the wrong one. The audit is where the time cards either support the split or they do not. A policy page that still says 9079 is a renewal question, and that question is already written up in the other note.

How the extra bill happens

Most of the additional premium is the exposure you already had, at the rate you already had, minus the deposit you already paid. A December that ran past the estimate, a second concept, a wage that moved inside the term, an owner who was covered and drew less than the floor, overtime that was never split out, a 1099 who was on the line, a tradesperson with no certificate: each of those changes the exposure. The rate and the experience modification then do what the policy already said they would do. Ask for the audit worksheet if the bill is hard to read. The worksheet is the map from your records to the number.

A return can stop at the company’s minimum premium. The department’s guide describes a minimum premium as the smallest premium the company will charge to take the risk, filed with the department as part of its rating plan. If the audited premium falls under that floor, the difference does not all come back in cash. I will not guess whether your policy is on a minimum. The declarations, or the audit, will say. Nothing here is a promise of a refund or of a price.

Refusing the audit is the part with a multiplier. The department’s guide says insurers generally may audit during the policy period and within three years after it ends, that missing the audit can lead to cancellation or nonrenewal, and that an employer who will not allow the audit may be liable for a total premium equal to three times the estimated premium. Section 11760.1 is tighter than that summary, and the steps are the part worth knowing. Access means regular business hours during the policy and within three years after the policy period ends, or another time you and the insurer agree on. The statute’s figure is three times the insurer’s then-current estimate of the annual premium on the expiration date, plus the insurer’s costs of trying to get the audit done. It applies after a third request spread over at least 90 days, when you have given no compelling business reason, and only if the insurer then mails a certified notice and 30 days pass. Premium you already paid comes off that amount. If you open the records after the notice and the insurer finishes the audit, the statute says the premium and the costs are revised to the results of the audit. The multiplier is for a closed door. A busy December is the ordinary additional premium in the paragraphs above.

The same guide says the bureau can promulgate an experience modification using the reported losses and leaving the unaudited payroll out, which typically raises the modification from prior years, and that deliberate under-reporting of payroll is insurance fraud. A low estimate you correct in the summer is the conversation the guide invites. A low estimate you try to hold with a missing drawer is the one the fraud sentence is about. I want the payroll you ran.

How deep the audit goes depends on the final premium. On a policy whose final premium is $10,500 or more, the plan requires a physical audit at least once a year. If the carrier audits monthly, quarterly, or twice a year along the way, a signed payroll statement can stand in for those interim audits. The last audit of that policy still has to be a physical audit of the whole term. Under $10,500, the plan wants a physical audit often enough to get the payroll right, and a signed statement in any year without one. Physical does not mean a person has to sit at a table in the office. The plan defines a physical audit as an examination of your books of account and original payroll records, on paper or electronic, at your place or from a remote site, enough to verify the exposure by classification. A voluntary audit is the signed statement alone, without that examination. The plan also lets the bureau test the insurer’s audit against your records. The department’s guide says the same thing more briefly: the bureau may audit you to check the carrier’s audit.

If the liability page says sales

When the liability declarations use gross sales as the exposure, the report I want is sales for the policy dates. The calendar year is the wrong window if the policy does not start on January 1. If the page splits food and liquor, a second address, or catering, bring the sales split the same way. If the page does not split them, keep the split in your own file anyway. Adding a column later is easier than rebuilding one from memory.

The point-of-sale report is the sales document. A bank deposit has already been touched by card fees, a delivery app, sales tax, or a tip. I will not tell you which of those your carrier’s rating rule removes. The general liability condition I cited says the company uses its own rules and rates, so the definition of sales is that rule, on that policy. Ask which adjustments are allowed, and show them from the sales report, before you subtract anything yourself. An adjustment you cannot tie to the rule is the one that comes back onto the audit.

The mid-year habit is the same one the workers’ comp guide describes for payroll. If sales are running ahead of the estimate on the liability page, say so while the policy is open. A corrected estimate is a deposit you chose. The audit bill is the deposit the year produces after the fact.

What I want on the counter

This does not require a new payroll company. It requires the policy dates, and the records the plan already names: the books, and the original payroll records.

  1. Both declarations pages. Workers’ comp and liability, so the estimate and the rating basis sit in the same stack as the records. A photo is enough to start.
  2. Payroll for the policy period. Gross wages, with the overtime premium in its own column if you want it excluded, and tips in their own column. The calendar-year report is the wrong stack unless the policy ran the calendar year.
  3. Time records, if anyone worked two operations. Hours by person and by job. A percentage you liked in March will not divide the payroll.
  4. The owner’s wages or draw, and the waiver or the election. I need the date the carrier accepted it. If there is no accepted waiver, say that. For a person the policy covers, the floor in the table above is the number, prorated if they were not in the role all year.
  5. Every 1099, and what the person actually did. A cook, a server, or a dishwasher goes into payroll. A separate business doing licensed work stays out only with a certificate that covers the days they worked, and a license when the work needs one. Collect those before the invoice, not after the auditor asks.
  6. Sales for the same dates, if liability is rated on sales. From the point of sale, split the way the declarations are split. The bank statement can reconcile the report. It is not the basis.
  7. A note if the year changed shape. A second concept, a closure, a patio that became the dining room, a wage you lifted because the floor moved. That last item is the January note. Send the revised payroll estimate when you know it. Send a revised sales estimate in the same week if the liability page is on sales.

If the carrier offers monthly payroll reporting, use it. That is a check-in they already priced into the policy. If they do not, I want one around the middle of the term, and another if the holidays are not going to look like June. The department’s guide is the reason: a corrected estimate during the term is how a large audit bill, or a large return, stays smaller.

If the finished audit includes a kind of pay the plan excludes, ask for the worksheet and the rule before you pay the difference. The bureau’s page on what counts as payroll says the policyholder ombudsman can help when you and the company disagree about a type of compensation. The page has the contact. I am leaving it there.

If you would rather do this over a plate than over a payroll register, pick the restaurant. Bring the workers’ comp page and the liability page, or a photo of the two estimates. I’ll buy.
