The Derby, Arcadia: the steakhouse Seabiscuit’s jockey co-owned, still setting dessert on fire

In 1938 the jockey George “The Iceman” Woolf and Bill Petersen reopened a restaurant a short gallop from Santa Anita and called it The Derby. Here’s what to order, when to go, and why nobody agrees on when it started.

George Woolf won virtually every important stakes race in America. He rode Seabiscuit, Whirlaway, and Kayak II. The ironic exception, as Arcadia’s historical society puts it, was the Kentucky Derby. So in December 1938 he and Bill Petersen threw a grand reopening for a restaurant on Huntington Drive, “about seven furlongs” from Santa Anita, and named it The Derby. The line Arcadia’s historical society credits to him: “Maybe I’ll have to settle for a Derby in Arcadia instead of one in Louisville.”1

The Derby is still there and still a steakhouse. The racing never left: the historical society says the Sturniolo family, owners for nearly six decades, kept “the dynamic connection to the Sport of Kings alive,” and the house old fashioned is named for Seabiscuit.14

Why you should go

Order the steak the menu calls “The steak that made The Derby famous!” It’s the bacon-wrapped filet mignon, an 8-ounce center cut, at $57.4 On an August 2026 episode of the local podcast Tasting Arcadia, director of operations Tyler Zikuski called it the one dish that has stayed on the menu since the restaurant opened.5 If you want something bigger, the 16-ounce prime rib is $78 with au jus and creamed horseradish.4

Then order something on fire. Zikuski said the 24-ounce Chateaubriand for two is flambéed tableside, and so is the Baked Alaska.5 The historical society says Baked Alaska joined the menu in the 1950s, around the time The Derby started calling itself the “Home of the Cappuccino and Irish Coffee.”1 Today it’s $20.4 For a quieter finish, the bread pudding is $13. Zikuski said it’s a Derby recipe more than 50 years old, vanilla with a Jack Daniel’s whiskey cream sauce.5 At the bar, he said the old fashioned is the top seller. The house version is called the Seabiscuit: Buffalo Trace, bitters, sugar, and a Luxardo cherry, for $16.45

It isn’t only for history buffs. The Derby’s Yelp page shows about 2,800 reviews averaging 4.4 stars.7

Time it right, too. Zikuski described happy hour in the bar and lounge on Tuesday and Wednesday from 4 to 6, Thursday from 3 to 6, and Sunday from 4 to 6, with a third off appetizers. Saturday and Sunday start with brunch at 10:30, and reservations go through OpenTable on the restaurant’s website.5

Seven furlongs from the track

The building is older than the Woolf era, though not everyone agrees how old. Arcadia’s historical society marker, dedicated in 2011, says Hudson M. Proctor moved his popular Foothill Boulevard restaurant to the corner of Huntington and Third in October 1931. It had two dining rooms, each with a fireplace, and an apartment upstairs. The place changed hands and names through the mid-1930s before Woolf and Petersen reopened it in 1938.1 A city historic resources report lists Woolf as owner from 1939 to 1946.2

The restaurant tells it differently. Its website says it has served Arcadia since 1922, “104 Years of Tradition.”3 On the podcast, Zikuski said George Woolf started it in 1922 and that it moved to the current spot in 1938.5 The marker and the city report don’t say that. We couldn’t settle it, so here are both versions.

Woolf’s run ended on January 3, 1946, when he was thrown from his horse in an ordinary fourth race at Santa Anita and fatally injured. His widow, Genevieve, ran The Derby for five more years and kept his racing collection together.1

Two families, then a busboy

In 1951 Genevieve Woolf sold The Derby to Dominic and Lorene Sturniolo, whom the marker calls Murph and Slugger. They and later their son, Chip, owned it for nearly six decades.1 The Sturniolos expanded it.2 The marker says the dining room’s chandelier came from the estate of Lucky Baldwin’s daughter Clara. Baldwin was Arcadia’s founder and its first mayor. The city report says Dominic Sturniolo bought a chandelier in 1964 that had belonged to Baldwin himself.12 The same report says pianist Tony Antista played there in the 1940s and 1950s and that the patio was added in 1999.2

The marker says Dustin Nicolarsen and Michael Thomas took over in 2007.1 In 2024, Nicolarsen told What Now Los Angeles how he got there:

“I originally started working at The Derby as a busboy and worked my way up to general manager. When the previous owners gave me the opportunity to purchase it in 2007, I jumped on it.”

He also explained the cramped galley kitchen, with “only enough room for one person to comfortably walk down.” When it opened in 1922, he said, “there used to be a chicken coop in the back and we were only open four hours a day.” He said the restaurant displays only about a third of its memorabilia collection.6 That story described plans to tear down and rebuild the restaurant as part of a mixed-use project, with The Derby coming back as a tenant.6 The restaurant’s “Our Future” page still describes the project.8 On the August 2026 podcast, though, Zikuski said the plan had changed and described a remodel to expand the kitchen and dining room instead.5

If you go

The Derby is at 233 E. Huntington Dr., Arcadia, CA 91006 (map). It’s closed Mondays. Dinner starts at 4:00 p.m. Tuesday and Wednesday, lunch starts at 11:30 a.m. Thursday and Friday, and weekends begin with brunch at 10:30 a.m.7 For a cheaper first visit, sit in the lounge during happy hour.5 For the full show, book a table, order the bacon-wrapped filet, and save room for something flambéed. Before you leave, look up at the chandelier and the racing collection on the walls. Prices here were read on November 3, 2026, and can change.
