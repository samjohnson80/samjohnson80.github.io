Magic Lamp Inn, Rancho Cucamonga: Friday prime rib under a Route 66 lamp that burns real fire

A steakhouse at 8189 Foothill Blvd. since 1955, open again since October 2024. What to order, what to wear, which nights to book, and who kept the flame going for seventy years.

Drive Foothill Boulevard through Rancho Cucamonga after dark and look for the lamp. It is shaped like Aladdin’s, outlined in neon, and where a genie would rise from the spout, a natural gas flame burns.67 In 2012 the Inland Valley Daily Bulletin described the building under it as “vaguely Bavarian yet has an Arabian Nights name and a sign shaped like Aladdin’s lamp.”5 The Magic Lamp Inn has been on this stretch of old Route 66 since 1955.7

Why you should go

Because it is a real night out, with rules. The restaurant’s site takes reservations Wednesday through Sunday, 5:00 to 8:30 p.m., and says plainly: “Dress Code Enforced.”1 The posted policy rules out shorts, hoodies, graphic tees, gym or athletic wear, baseball hats and beanies, torn clothing, and scrubs. It asks you to silence your phone and step outside to take a call.3 OpenTable lists it as fine dining, business casual, $31 to $50, with happy hour from 5:00 to 7:00 p.m.4

Pick your night by the prime rib. On the June 2026 menu posted on the site, it is $68 with au jus, sautéed spinach, mashed potato, and creamed corn, and it is available Friday and Saturday only.2 The rest is the classic steakhouse card: bone marrow with bacon jam and crostini, $28; an 8-ounce center-cut prime filet mignon with garlic mashed potatoes and demi-glace, $64; a 22-ounce prime bone-in ribeye, $86; Chilean sea bass with an orange maple glaze, $58; and lobster mac and cheese, $28. The menu says items and prices may change, adds a 20% service charge for parties of six or more, and prints this in capitals: “THE CHEF PREFERS THAT HIS DISHES ARE SERVED AS ORIGINALLY PREPARED, WITHOUT ANY MODIFICATIONS.”2

Diners on OpenTable give it 4.7 overall from recent reviews, out of 695 written over time.4 A diner post shown on the listing, signed #wanderfuleatz, ran down a full order: bone marrow, the bone-in ribeye, lobster mac, the Chilean sea bass (“a must!”), and the magic chocolate cake (“another must!”). The same post describes weekend entertainment, “a singer by the fireplace and magician that visits your table.” A recent verified review says the same thing in fewer words: “Had Elvis impersonators singing live music and had a magician stop by the table.” Another reads, “It’s not just an incredible dinner at a truly lovely venue. It’s an experience.”4

Not every write-up is a rave. Foothill & Vine describes “red booths, a working fireplace, a bar built for Manhattans,” and then says “the kitchen’s best argument is consistency rather than surprise.” Its advice: come at dusk, photograph the sign with the flame lit, and go in for a drink at minimum.11

Lucy and John’s, then the lamp

The lot had a restaurant before it had a lamp. From 1941 to 1955 it was Lucy and John’s, an Italian place on Route 66.7 Daily Bulletin columnist David Allen later published one of its old menus, with radishes and celery for 25 cents and a 35-cent order of ravioli, and was told the couple’s last name was Di Censo Nosenzo.9 The accounts differ on how it ended. A Route 66 history site says Lucy and John’s burned down, and that Frank and Edith Penn and the Clearman family bought the property, rebuilt it, and renamed it.7 Anthony Vernola, the longtime owner, told Allen a quieter version: “When John passed away, Lucy ran it and sold it to Mr. Clearman and Mr. Penn.”9

The Daily Bulletin credited the founding to John Clearman and said he modeled it on a Steer ’N Stein he owned in Pico Rivera.5 Clearman was the restaurateur behind the North Woods Inn chain.13 It was built in Old World style: “stained glass, heavy wooden doors, red brick and Spanish tile.” Inside were a specially made lamp-patterned carpet and a fire pit that becomes a fountain in warmer months.5 An undated postcard from the restaurant’s early years sold the same trick: “Cocktails are served in our cozy waiting room featuring a circle fireplace which becomes a fountain during the summer months.”12 Lucy and John’s never fully left. Allen reported its flat roof still sits under the peaked tile one, and that the bar is original. “It’s a rooftop on top of a rooftop,” Vernola told him.9

Seven decades, three chapters

Vernola came from a Mira Loma farming family and bought the Magic Lamp in 1975, the Press-Enterprise reported; Allen wrote that the family had it “since the early 1970s.”69 “I thought (the restaurant) would be a cleaner business,” Vernola said. Then the hours caught up with him: “If I wanted to work seven days a week, I should have stayed on the ranch.”6

In 2012 he retired and leased it, with an option to buy, to Sartaj Singh, who owned several restaurants in the region. He had long been hesitant to hand over the keys. “I’ve seen restaurants go into ruins. I’ve invested too much into this place to have that happen,” Vernola said. Singh pledged to leave the Magic Lamp “as it is.”5 The restaurant closed in early 2023. The Press-Enterprise reported it was fully renovated over the next 18 months, and it held an invitation-only event and a soft opening in October 2024, by reservation through OpenTable.8 The sources found for this page do not name the current owners.

When Allen came in for lunch in 2011, he and his friends relaxed afterward in leather chairs, and one said what the place is still selling.10

“They don’t make restaurants like this anymore.”

If you go

8189 Foothill Blvd., Rancho Cucamonga, on old Route 66. Book on OpenTable for Wednesday through Sunday, 5:00 to 8:30 p.m., and make it a Friday or Saturday if you want the prime rib. Dress for the code and silence your phone. Stay until it is dark, when the neon and the flame are lit, then take the photo.12313 The map is here.
