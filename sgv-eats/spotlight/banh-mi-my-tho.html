Banh Mi My Tho

LA Weekly once named this Valley Boulevard counter the Best Sandwich Shop That Looks Like a Gas Station. More than 30 years in, it still sells lottery tickets a few feet from the No. 2 and the No. 8. What to order, when to go, and the story.

Walk into Banh Mi My Tho, LA Weekly’s Tien Nguyen wrote in 2011, “and you’ll think you’ve been transported not to Vietnam, but maybe the best gas station convenience store, ever.” Boxed green tea peeked out of cardboard on the right. Lotto tickets waited on the left. The hot case, the kind that holds corn dogs at a Shell station, was “stuffed with egg rolls, meat pies, and nem nuong (pork) on a stick.” The paper’s Best of L.A. issue gave the strip-mall shop a title to match: Best Sandwich Shop That Looks Like a Gas Station.1

The joke lasted because the sandwiches back it up. Time Out put them “at the top of the San Gabriel Valley line-up.”3 LA Weekly’s 2014 guide to Vietnamese street food in the valley called it “probably” the area’s bánh mì king.6 In 2020, Los Angeles Times critic Bill Addison put it on his list of eight favorite banh mi shops in L.A. and Orange County.2

Why you should go

Pick a number. The sandwiches go by number, and Addison’s advice was to “follow the wisdom of the crowd: Most everyone orders either the No. 8, stuffed with grilled pork deeply imbued with lemongrass and garlic, or the No. 2, a combination of grilled pork with cold cuts and pâté.”2 On the menu, the No. 8’s pork is garnished with green onion and crushed peanut.13 The Infatuation’s Garrett Snyder orders the No. 2 “with an added fried egg.”4 Food GPS founder Joshua Lurie’s pick was the steamed meatball sandwich: crumbled pork meatballs in tomato sauce with house mayo, pickled carrot and daikon, and thin-sliced jalapeño, “seeds, veins and all.”5

Look for the yellow sauce. Every sandwich gets pickled daikon and carrot, cucumber, cilantro, a couple of slices of jalapeño and a spread of house-made egg-yolk mayonnaise.13 Time Out singled out the spread: “See that magic yellow sauce in your sandwich? It’s a house-made egg yolk mayonnaise, a truly addictive agent.”3

About the bread, critics disagree. Addison wrote that “on contact the bronzed crust fractures like cracked earth.”2 Time Out found the baguette “a tad on the softer side of French.”3 Lurie called it “almost always too crusty” and still “light years ahead of local chains.”5 Snyder says it ranges from “a little tough to downright mouth-scraping,” then adds: “On a good day it’s still the best banh mi you’ll find north of Orange County.”4

What it costs. Lurie paid $2.25 for that meatball sandwich in 2010.5 In 2011, LA Weekly figured a charbroiled pork banh mi and a Vietnamese coffee cost “roughly the same amount as a gallon of gas.”1 Prices have climbed. A Google review reposted in April 2026 put most sandwiches at $7, with $3 for extra meat and $8.25 for grilled beef,11 and a September 2026 review on Restaurantji reported $7.50 for grilled pork.10 Go by the board at the counter.

When to go. Tuesday through Sunday, 7 a.m. to 4 p.m. Closed Monday.910 Snyder’s advice is to hit the original Alhambra shop earlier in the day.4 Lunch gets busy. Addison wrote that the phone “never stops ringing.”2 Reviewers suggest calling your order in. You still wait in the same line, but your sandwich is already made.11 None of the listings below shows Thanksgiving hours, so check the shop’s Google listing or Instagram before Thursday.

What to expect. A small counter, not a dining room. Time Out noted there is “no place to sit besides your car—and that, people do.”3 One reviewer counted a tiny outside table for two.11 Bring cash. Reviewers and listings describe the shop as cash only, though Restaurantji’s summary says cards are now accepted.1011 Add an iced coffee. Time Out called it “some of the best and strongest in the city.”3 The ratings hold up: 4.4 stars from about 2,400 Yelp reviews12 and 4.5 from 278 ratings on Restaurantji.10

A Mekong Delta name on Valley Boulevard

The shop has not told its founding story on any page we could find, and the dates differ by a year. In January 2010, Lurie called it “a nearly 17-year-old Alhambra sandwich shop,” which points to 1993.5 In 2025 the shop shared a throwback video labeled February 12, 1994, at 304 W. Valley Blvd.8 The name comes from Mỹ Tho, which Lurie described as “a town one hour from Ho Chi Minh City.”5

Snyder sums up what came next. It “might have started out as a low-key strip mall deli slash convenience store,” but it grew into “the most popular banh mi mini-chain in LA County,” with three shops across the San Gabriel Valley.4 Its Instagram bio still reads “Family Owned and Operated” and lists the Alhambra shop first, followed by two on Garvey Avenue in Rosemead.7 Snyder thinks the growth has cost some consistency, and he points people to the original.4

The convenience-store part never left. In 2010 Lurie found rice plates, cookies, “radioactive-hued beverages and pork floss by the pound,” and an incubator by the register holding egg rolls, pâté chaud and “glistening skewers of golf ball-sized pork meatballs.”5 A Google review reposted this year listed pâté, egg yolk and lottery tickets for sale.11 Lurie’s verdict from 2010 still sums it up:

“For now, Banh Mi My Tho rules the Valley Boulevard banh mi vendors.”5

If you go

Banh Mi My Tho is at 304 W. Valley Blvd. in Alhambra.4 Get there before the lunch rush with cash in your pocket. Order the No. 2 with a fried egg or the No. 8, add an iced coffee and a few egg rolls from the hot case, and, like plenty of customers, eat it in the car. Get directions on Google Maps.
