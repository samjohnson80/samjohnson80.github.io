La Paloma

A family-run Mexican restaurant on Route 66 since 1966, in a building that once sat in two cities. That confusion helped it get a liquor license. What to order, when to go, and the story.

La Paloma used to sit in two cities at once. One part of the restaurant on Foothill Boulevard was in La Verne and the other part was in Pomona, and that fuzzy border came in handy when the place wanted to sell margaritas.3 The Inland Valley Daily Bulletin told the story for the restaurant’s 50th anniversary in 2016, as general manager Steve Love told it: La Verne “wasn’t keen on alcohol,” but the head of the Chamber of Commerce, “who liked a good martini,” encouraged founder Joe Parker to apply for a license anyway, on the theory that no one would realize the restaurant wasn’t in Pomona “until it was too late — which turned out to be the case.”3

The restaurant has since settled entirely in La Verne.3 This year it turned 60, and it is still run by the founders’ family.49

Why you should go

You want the old-school, sit-down kind of Mexican dinner. Manager Brian Zajicek, the founders’ grandson, describes “a more authentic Sonoran Mexican menu,” and his mother, owner Cindy Zajicek, says regulars “don’t want the Southwest Tex-Mex.”4

Order a combination plate. Pick two items for $19.50 or three for $21.50 from a taco, a chile relleno (a green chile stuffed with Monterey Jack and fried in egg batter), an enchilada, or two taquitos.2 The chile verde and chile colorado plates, diced beef and pork in green or red chile sauce, are $19.75 with beans, rice and tortillas.2

Or go big. The Kitchen Sink Burrito, $25.50, is described on the menu as “a massive burrito” filled with chile colorado, chile verde, refried beans, rice and ground beef, then topped with sour cream sauce, guacamole, avocado and cheese.2 It is on Restaurantji’s list of customer favorites, next to the chicken enchilada suprema, the Enchiladas New Mexico and the chile verde plate.8 Two later additions have become favorites too, La Verne Magazine reported: the pollo diablo in chipotle cream ($23.50) and the spinach mushroom enchiladas ($19.75).25

Reviewers mostly agree, with one caveat. La Paloma holds 4.5 stars across 342 ratings on Restaurantji. One reviewer there calls it “pretty standard fare as far as Mexican restaurants go,” then adds that it’s not a chain, “so there are some unique items—the spinach and mushroom enchiladas come to mind,” along with “pitchers of margaritas, if that’s your thing.”8

When to go. Posted hours are 11 a.m. to 8:30 p.m. Tuesday through Thursday and Sunday, and 11 a.m. to 9 p.m. Friday and Saturday. It is closed Monday.1 Lunch specials are $13.50 with beans and rice, 11 a.m. to 4 p.m. on weekdays (the menu says Monday to Friday, but Monday is closed).12 Albondigas soup is served all week and fideo on Tuesdays.2 No Thanksgiving-weekend hours are posted on the restaurant’s site, so check its site or Google listing before you head over today.1

What to expect. LA Beat’s Nikki Kreuzer found wood-beamed ceilings and wrought iron left from the old steakhouse, red leather booths, stained glass taken from a demolished church, and an original 1960s neon sign out front.7 Wilson’s old dining room is now La Paloma’s east dining room.5

An orange grove, a steakhouse, then the dove

The building is older than the restaurant. In the late 1920s Harry and Cora Wilson bought part of an orange grove along Route 66. Cora began cooking breakfast for farmworkers, and in 1930 the couple opened Wilson’s Sandwiches, which grew into Wilson’s Dinner House.5 A fruit stand next door sold orange juice to motorists, all you could drink for $1, by Love’s account. In the mid-1950s the steakhouse pushed out toward the street and added the stained-glass windows from a church that are still there.3 The dates don’t line up perfectly. A 2009 La Verne Magazine story says the building went up in 1928 and was Wilson’s Steakhouse until 1962.6 The National Trust puts the closing in 1959, the year Cora died.45 Either way, it then sat empty for years.

The name came from Arizona. Hortensia Parker’s mother started the original La Paloma, Spanish for “the dove,” in Flagstaff in the 1940s. Hortensia moved to San Bernardino and opened a tortilla shop, and her husband Joe left construction to work in the business. They built up several San Bernardino restaurants before an acquaintance pointed them to the empty La Verne property.56 Their daughter Cindy described what came next: “my dad refurbished everything, borrowed money from whoever he could to get the business started, and just used family recipes.”4 They opened in July 1966.34

Accounts of the early competition differ too. In 2009 Joe William Parker Jr. said his parents opened the only Mexican restaurant in the area.6 In 2024 Cindy recalled “about three,” adding, “we were one of the first to serve alcohol in La Verne.”4

Staff tended to stay. Server Marsha Felix started at 16 and had put in 47 years when La Verne Magazine profiled her in 2018.5 She gave the magazine the best line in the place’s history:

“Customers will ask me, ‘What’s the name of your perfume,’ and I’ll say, ‘By the end of the day, it’s taco.’”5

Hortensia died in 2002. Her son Joe William Jr. oversaw the restaurant until Cindy took full ownership in 2013.5 The restaurant’s site says the founders’ six children and many grandchildren have worked there over the years.1 A 2024 Route 66 Legacy Business grant paid to repair a roof damaged by heavy rains.4 In July, city, county and state officials joined the family for a 60th-anniversary ribbon cutting.9

If you go

La Paloma is at 2975 Foothill Blvd. in La Verne, on the old Route 66, which turned 100 this year.14 Take whoever first brought you to a place like this, slide into a red booth, and order a combo plate. Here is the map. Sixty years in, Cindy says, regulars still tell her their parents brought them here as kids.4
