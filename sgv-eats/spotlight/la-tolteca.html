La Tolteca, Azusa: a 1948 tortilla factory that still sells its salsa by the gallon

The Arrietta family started out selling corn tortillas to Azusa. Three generations later they run a big, booth-lined restaurant on Azusa Avenue. What to order, when to go, and how a tortilla factory grew a dining room.

Most restaurants sell you a basket of chips. La Tolteca will sell you twelve pounds of them. On its online menu the chips come by the half pound for $2.50, the pound for $4.50, five pounds for $22.50, or a 12-pound box for $54, and the salsa comes by the pint, the quart, the half gallon, or the gallon.3 That is not a gimmick. It is what is left of the business the Arrietta family started in Azusa in 1948: a tortilla factory.1

Why you should go

Go for the burrito the menu itself shows off. The Tolteca Meat Burrito, $13.25, is carne asada or carnitas with beans, rice, cheese, pico, and what the menu calls home-style guac. The menu photo shows it wet, half red sauce and half green, and that half-and-half sauce with cheese is a $2 add-on.3 Order it the way they picture it.

Then look at the plates. A taquito plate is $14, a flautas plate $16, and a carne asada or carnitas plate $19.75. The combination plate, $23, is the whole tour: a taco, a taquito with guac, an enchilada, chile verde, beans, rice, tortillas, chips, and salsa. For something lighter there is a ceviche tostada at $9.50 and a potato taco at $3.25.3 Reviews posted on the restaurant’s own site point the same way. One, signed Jonathan A., opens, “You must get taquitos and chips and salsa!” Another, from Becky S., says, “I highly recommend the flautas.”2

Go hungry, and go with people. The restaurant says its booths are big and its portions are bigger, and it runs a full bar with margaritas.2 It opens at 8:00 a.m. for breakfast, which a 2014 Neon Tommy profile called the thing that “separates La Tolteca from most Mexican restaurants in L.A.,” quoting a Yelp reviewer: “Huevos rancheros have never been this good besides my momma’s.”4 And on the way out, stop at the to-go counter for a bag of chips and a quart of salsa. Corn tortillas, which the menu says are made from scratch in-house, are four for $1.05.3

A tortilla factory first

The restaurant’s history begins with Ben Arrietta Sr., who started La Tolteca in 1948 as a tortilla factory supplying fresh corn tortillas to local stores, schools, and neighbors, made with corn, water, and lime.1 Neon Tommy’s account adds restaurants to that customer list and says the business grew into a Mexican deli built on family recipes.4

In 1967, La Tolteca opened at 321 N. Azusa Ave. as a tortilla factory with just a few small tables. “By 1981, the community had spoken,” the family writes, and they built a proper dining room. In 2008 they moved to 429 N. Azusa Ave., where the restaurant is today.1 One wrinkle: the restaurant’s home page says, “We built our home at 429 N. Azusa Ave. in 1948.”2 Its own story page gives the 1967 and 2008 addresses and dates above, so 1948 is best read as the year the business began, not the year it reached this building.1 Neon Tommy reported that even after the dining space was enlarged, “people still had to line up outside to eat.”4

Three generations of Arriettas

Tim and Ben Jr. run La Tolteca today, with their sons, according to the restaurant.1 FOX 11 identified Tim Arrietta as one of the owners in 2021.6 Azusa Pacific University’s guide to eating near campus calls it “a staple in the Azusa community.”5

In 2014, a member of the Arrietta family explained the formula to Neon Tommy: “We keep our prices low to keep it a family-friendly place.” The staff then was local high school and college students plus family.4 The same story collected memories from an Azusa Facebook group. One man recalled his father sending him across the street with change for chicharrones. A woman remembered the chips:4

“We used to go get their chips before school and sneak them into class. Everyone knew when you had them because you could smell their yummyness.”

The restaurant says it still has regulars who have been coming since the 1960s.1
