Bulgarini, Altadena: the gelateria that survived the Eaton fire, lost 2,300 pounds of gelato, and came back for its neighbors

A Roman-born gelato maker apprenticed with an 82-year-old master in Sicily, opened behind an Altadena strip-mall courtyard, and won over Jonathan Gold. Then the fire took his home and spared his shop. Go for the Bronte pistachio gelato and the wild boar pappardelle, and book ahead.

Three days after the Eaton fire burned his house, Leo Bulgarini drove through his leveled neighborhood to see what was left of his restaurant. The Bunny Museum and the Open Road Bicycle Shop, less than a football field away, were charred rubble. He expected the same. Instead, tucked in a shopping-center courtyard, Bulgarini was still standing. 1

Inside, it smelled of smoke. Water from the roof had pooled on the kitchen floor and spilled onto the gelato machines. He spent three days cleaning and threw out spoiled meat, fish, pasta and 2,300 pounds of handmade gelato, about $100,000 worth of food by his count. One night after the fire, he put pencil to paper and wrote: “I’m still alive.” 1

This is the gelato that Jonathan Gold, the Pulitzer Prize-winning critic, called “a singular, perfect blossom in a world of international sweets.” 23 And it’s back.

Why you should go

Start with the pistachio. The nuts come from Bronte, the Sicilian town near Mount Etna. 4 Bulgarini buys them directly from the growers, roasts them and extracts the oils himself. 1 LA Weekly wrote in 2011 that the pistachio gelato “tastes somehow like an entire grove of pistachio trees has been crushed and condensed into a single, aromatic scoop.” 4 The shop’s gelato list marks Sicilian pistachio as “check for availability,” along with Sicilian almond, hazelnut and chocolate orange, so ask what’s in the case. 5

Then stay for dinner. The gelateria grew into a restaurant, and the pasta is made from scratch. 6 On the posted menu, the pappardelle al cinghiale (wide egg noodles with wild boar) is $41; the SGV Tribune reported that the boar marinates in wine and juniper berry for three days. The lobster pasta is $48, and its sauce takes three days to reduce. 73 A longtime regular told the Tribune his favorites are the spaghettini with duck ragù ($43) and the spaghetti carbonara with pork cheeks and egg ($41). 37 The menu marks several dishes as specials, so ask what’s on. If you can’t choose, the sampling menu is three courses and gelato for $95 a person. 7

Book first. Dine-in is by reservation only, and the restaurant takes them by phone. 8 The contact page lists Thursday to Sunday, 4 to 10 p.m. 9 Summer posts said Friday to Sunday, so confirm when you call.

Don’t rush. “His shop wasn’t about flipping tables,” the Tribune wrote. Guests were allowed to linger over dinner, and some learned to play scopa, the Italian card game. 3

Know what you’re walking into. It isn’t cheap. LA Weekly said so back in 2011: “Nothing is cheap at Bulgarini.” 4 It is also easy to miss. Gold called it “the most improbable ice cream store in California, almost a gelato speakeasy,” where the flavors were labeled only in Italian. 2

The backstory: a challenge, a cart and an 82-year-old teacher

Leo Bulgarini was born in Rome, raised there and in Siena, and came to the U.S. at 20 already knowing how to make gelato from his family. 3 He met his wife, Elizabeth, a Pasadena native, while working at Trattoria Tre Venezie on Green Street in Pasadena. “She told me she was leaving L.A. because there are no real, good men in Los Angeles,” he told the Tribune. He took it as a challenge. 36

The couple spent two years searching villages across Italy for artisans who still made gelato the old way. In Catania they found an 82-year-old, third-generation gelato maker in retirement and studied with him for two months, and he sent them to another octogenarian in Milan to finish. 6 Gold first tasted their gelato at the Pacific Asia Museum in Pasadena and later became a friend. 3 Bulgarini opened the Altadena gelateria in 2006, according to the L.A. Times and the Tribune. 13 Sources disagree on the year: LA Weekly put the opening in 2007, 4 and the restaurant’s own site dates the business to 2010. 6

The fussiness is the point. A friend, Mel Lynch, described how the peach gelato gets made: Bulgarini lines up organic peaches on tables outside the shop “with a sign that says, ‘Not for sale,’” brings them in at night and puts them back in the sun until they’re perfectly ripe. Then he and his crew spend “two days and two nights” pitting them and making gelato. “Everything is about ingredients with Leo,” Lynch said. 3

The food came from the neighborhood. Bulgarini projected Italian movies on the back wall of the Bunny Museum for locals and served a little pasta, and “that’s what started the food,” Lynch said. 3 After the fire, the family lost their 1923 Spanish bungalow and moved eight times in two months. 13 In early 2025 Bulgarini said he would likely move the restaurant to Eagle Rock or Montrose. 1 He stayed. The Tribune reported a soft opening in Altadena in February 2026, and he said Gold “would be the first to say, ‘Go ahead, open up, do this again.’” 3 “It’s all about the community and we need to get this community back,” he said. 3

Can’t get a table? Howie’s Market in San Gabriel sells his gelato, according to a July 2026 post from the store. 10

If you go

Bulgarini is at 749 E. Altadena Dr. in Altadena, in the courtyard of the shopping center at Lake Avenue. Call ahead for a table, order the pappardelle al cinghiale, and finish with the Sicilian pistachio if it’s in the case. Much of the neighborhood is still rebuilding. Dinner here is one way to help it come back.
