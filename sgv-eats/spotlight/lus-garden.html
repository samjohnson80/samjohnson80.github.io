Lu’s Garden

A cook from Taipei opened this porridge house on Valley Boulevard in 1989. Thirty-seven years later it still sets a red pot of sweet-potato porridge on the table, with about 50 Taiwanese dishes to pick from at the counter. What to order, when to go, and the story.

At lunchtime a line forms in front of Lu’s Garden. Just inside the door is a long counter of buffet trays: braised pork, lap cheong, sautéed string beans, bok choy. The kitchen refills them every five to ten minutes. On most tables in the dining room sits a red pot of rice porridge with bright orange sweet potato floating in it, Culinary Backstreets reported in 2025.1

One house rule to know before you go: Lu’s doesn’t allow guests to photograph the food. Culinary Backstreets needed special permission for its pictures.1 That is why the pictures that go with this story are drawings.

Why you should go

The porridge. Lu’s has always been known for it.1 It is Taiwanese style, studded with chunks of sweet potato and, in LAist’s words, “extremely mellow.” LAist also noted there is no caddy of soy sauce or chili oil to doctor it: “The porridge here is unadorned, just as grandma might have insisted.”2 Food GPS put the rice-or-porridge base at $1.25 a person in 2010.3 The online takeout menu now lists porridge at $3.00.4

The counter. The porridge is the base. The point is what you put next to it. Lu’s generally keeps about fifty dishes going at a time, cooked in small batches. Each dish may be cooked ten times a day, the popular ones more.1 Jon Yao, the chef behind Kato, which earned a Michelin star in 2019, eats here often.10 “Lu’s garden reminds me of my mom’s cooking and it’s super convenient so I always have it,” he told the Los Angeles Times in 2022.5 His order, as he gave it to Food & Wine, is sautéed dry bean curd with chives, bitter melon with salted egg, and “Spicy w/ Spicy.”6 On the online menu those run $11.35, $13.75 and $13.75.4

The dish called Flies’ Head. In Mandarin it is cāng yíng tóu. The “fly heads” are fermented black beans scattered through the dish. Descriptions vary. Culinary Backstreets lists minced pork, chives, bird’s eye chili and black beans.1 Food & Wine’s write-up of Yao’s order lists string beans, black soybeans, peppers and dried radish.6 Ask at the counter which tray it is.

What else regulars get. Culinary Backstreets names pan-fried whole squid, braised bamboo shoots, braised pork belly and sautéed bean curd as the most popular.1 The Times photographed simmered roast pork, eggplant with chef sauce, Taiwanese sausage, braised bamboo shoot and fried belt fish around one pot.5 Online, pan-fried squid and simmered roast pork are $16.15 each and Taiwan sausage is $11.35.4 Prices in the dining room may differ.

When to go. Lu’s is open every day from 11:30 a.m. to 11 p.m.78 That makes it a late-night spot. Yao told Food & Wine that his parents love eating late at night, xiāo yè in Mandarin, and that Lu’s is his late-night go-to when he can’t make it to their house.6 Google’s popular-times chart shows the crowds peaking Friday from about 7 to 9 p.m., Saturday early afternoon and Sunday around 4 to 6 p.m. Monday through Wednesday afternoons look calmer.8

What to expect. A cafeteria setup. Order first, then sit.9 At lunch the counter is a three-item combo. At dinner the portions are entrée-sized.6 It moves quickly. Food & Wine recalls that the late critic Jonathan Gold called Lu’s “the fastest restaurant in Los Angeles County.”6 PBS SoCal described it as cash only in 2017.9 The restaurant now takes orders online, but bring cash in case.4 Leave the camera in your pocket.

From Taipei to Valley Boulevard

Lu Shi Sam opened the first Lu’s Garden in Taipei in 1974. In 1989 he immigrated to San Gabriel and opened this one, and ran it for many years.1 When Food GPS visited in 2010, he was 83 and retired. Proprietor Sophia Fan carried on, and the recipes, Food GPS wrote, “remain constant.”3 Culinary Backstreets dates the sale to about twenty years before its 2025 story.1

Lu Shi Sam died in 2019. His daughter, Debbie Lu, still works at the restaurant under the new ownership.1 She told Culinary Backstreets how they keep it the same:

“Most of the menu is always available; we only change the seasonal vegetables. We make sure that the taste remains the same, that’s how we have so many returning customers.”

The sweet potato has a history of its own. During the World War II era and the rice shortage that followed in Taiwan, sweet potato was easier to grow on hillsides. Cooks added it to porridge to stretch what little rice there was.1 Food & Wine calls it a once-wartime ration that now “holds a special place in Taiwanese diners’ hearts.”6

The customers have changed with the generations. “I see that a lot of customers are second generation,” Debbie Lu told Culinary Backstreets. “When they eat here it’s like a childhood memory. And then they bring their kids in.” Most new customers, she said, heard about it from friends.1 LAist counted it among the oldest restaurants in the SGV.2 AFAR called it “a mainstay in the San Gabriel Valley since its opening in 1989.”10

If you go

Lu’s Garden is at 534 E. Valley Blvd., Suite 12, in San Gabriel’s Valley Plaza.4 Go hungry, point at three trays, and let the red pot do the rest. Need dinner after 9? Lu’s is open until 11. Get directions on Google Maps.
